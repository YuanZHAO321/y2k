## Slide 2

### Force

- Deformation occurs when a force is applied to a body; the concept of stress is used to describe the physical state of a material body on which forces are applied. Hence, as structural geologists interested in deformation, we need to get to grips with stress

- The concept of stress arises from a consideration of surface forces

- There are two kinds of forces:

- body forces which act at a distance on all the volume elements of a rock (e.g., gravity, electromagnetic forces)

- surface forces which arise directly from the action of one body on another across the surface between them (e.g., when we push a block we apply a surface force across the area of contact between our hand and the block), or which arise indirectly from the action of one part of a body on another part across some imaginary internal surface – they describe the interatomic forces exerted by the material on one side of the imaginary surface upon the material on the other side

- The magnitude of a body force is directly proportional to mass or volume; the magnitude of a surface force is directly proportional to the area of surface over which it acts

## Slide 3

### Traction

- A large force obviously has a greater effect on an object than a small force. However, the response of the object is governed by the intensity of the force, not by its magnitude

- The starting point for considering stress is therefore traction

- Traction (T) is force intensity and is defined as

- T  =  F/A

- force

- area over which

- the force acts

- The SI unit of force is the Newton (N) - the force required to accelerate 1 kg at 1 ms-2

- Hence the SI unit of traction is N/m2 which is known as a Pascal (1 Pa = 1 Nm-2)

- The Pascal is a small quantity – we commonly work in megaPascals (MPa),

- where 1 MPa = 106 Pa  (NB. 1 kbar = 100 MPa)

**Notes:**

Strictly, traction is defined as the limit of the ratio F/A as we shrink A to zero, that is, as we shrink the surface over which the force acts to a point. A particularly clear exposition of the concept of traction is given by Pollard & Fletcher (p.197-200) but also see the file StressNote.pdf that accompanies this PowerPoint presentation
Note that the definition of the unit of force follows from Newton’s 2nd law, F = ma (1 N = 1 kg m s-2). Hence in base SI units (the relevant base units are here, kg, m, s), 1 Pa = 1 N/m2 = 1 kg m s-2/m2 = 1 kg m-1 s-2
In igneous and metamorphic petrology, you will have met the pressure unit, kilobar. 1 kbar = 100 MPa. You will find it very convenient to remember this conversion

## Slide 4

### The traction vector

- The traction acting on a surface within a loaded body is a vector that may be obtained by adding components acting normal and parallel to the surface

- q

- Tn	=  Fn / A2

- =  ( F cos θ ) / A2

- =  ( F / A1 ) cos2 θ

- Ts	=  Fs / A2

- =  ( F sin θ ) / ( A1 / cos θ )

- =  ( F / A1 ) sin θ  cos θ

- =  ( F / A1 ) ( sin 2θ ) / 2

- θ  is the angle between the direction of the applied force and the normal to the surface (anticlockwise = positive)

- Hence the magnitude and orientation of the traction vector as obtained by combining Tn and Ts varies with the orientation (θ  ) of the surface

**Notes:**

Note the difference between resolving the force vector into normal and shear components (Fn = F cos θ ; Fs = F sin θ respectively), and obtaining the traction vector from its normal and shear components (Tn = T cos2 θ ; Ts = (T sin 2θ)/2 respectively). From the perspective of the trigonometrical analysis presented on this slide, the difference arises because the traction vector depends upon the size of the area (A2) across which the traction acts, and this depends upon the orientation (θ) of the surface. For a formal derivation of this same result, which accommodates the definition of traction as the limit of the ratio F/A as we shrink A to zero (see notes to last slide), see Pollard & Fletcher, p.203-205

## Slide 5

### Sign conventions  ;  tractions → stresses

- If the surface upon which a traction is applied is to be in mechanical equilibrium, then by Newton’s 3rd law there must be an equal and opposite traction operating on the other side

- Positive tractions are defined as compressive and with an anticlockwise sense of shear

- (Mohr circle convention)

- NB. the definition of compressive tractions as positive is a geological (rock mechanics) convention; in the engineering community, compressive tractions are negative

- Traction describes force intensity at a point on a surface of specified orientation;

- The state of stress describes force intensity on surfaces of all possible orientations through the point

**Notes:**

Newton’s 3rd law: to every action there is always opposed an equal reaction
The words “Mohr circle convention” are included because in the matrix (tensor) notation of stress the sign of the shear stresses is defined in a different way (see Slides 7 and 12)
Some care is needed with using ‘anticlockwise ‘ and ‘clockwise to define +ve and -ve shear respectively because whether a rotation is clockwise or anticlockwise depends upon the direction of observation. See for yourself – if you rotate a piece of paper clockwise and then continue to do so while looking at the piece of paper from the other side, you’ll see that it is now rotating anticlockwise. Anticlockwise +ve is true if the x-axis points right and the y-axis points up (the conventional orientations of these axes when we plot a graph) because then according to the right-hand rule we are looking in the -ve z-direction – with your right hand put your thumb pointing right along the x axis and your first finger pointing up along the y axis; your second finger (z axis) is now pointing towards you; now put your thumb pointing along the z axis towards you and you will see that your fingers curl in an anticlockwise sense, that is, anticlockwise is +ve. However, clockwise rotations are +ve when we look in a +ve axis direction (with your right-hand thumb pointing that way, your fingers curl in a clockwise sense). If we were to define our axes so that x pointed left and y pointed up, then anticlockwise would be -ve. You’ll not go wrong in this course unit if you simply remember anticlockwise = +ve (as it was when we considered shear strain, Class 3 Slide 4)
The geological convention of positive compressive stresses follows from the fact that stresses within the Earth are dominantly compressive. Engineers prefer to consider tensile stresses as positive because they are concerned with the failure of engineering constructions – the tensile strength of materials is generally smaller than the compressive strength and is therefore of greater significance to them. In both cases, the definition is designed to avoid unnecessary numbers of negative signs in the various deformation modelling equations
Note that if we are being strict in terminology, in stress we have compression and tension, while in strain shortening is contraction and lengthening is extension. Note also that extension can occur even when all stresses are compressive – the deformation response depends upon the relative magnitudes of the stresses in different directions and so if we compress strongly in one direction, extension will occur in directions at a high angle to this provided that the compressive stresses acting in those directions are relatively small

## Slide 6

### Stress at a point in 2D

- In 2D, if we calculate the traction acting on planes of all possible orientations through a point, the tails of the traction vectors define an ellipse (stress ellipse)

- The state of stress, σ , is therefore given by the information that is required to define the shape and orientation of this ellipse → this is given by knowledge of the tractions acting on two mutually perpendicular surfaces

- Hence the state of stress is defined by four numbers:

- two normal stresses,  σn   =   σxx , σyy

- two shear stresses,  τ   = σxy , σyx

- σxy

- pole to

- plane

- direction

**Notes:**

The 2D analysis applies for biaxial compression (or tension), that is, when the body (the yellow square in the figure on the slide) is being compressed in two perpendicular directions. Note that this method of defining the stress ellipse requires that all tractions be either positive or negative and not some combination of these
In general, the traction vector is not at right angles to the surface upon which it is acting. This means that given a particular traction vector, the orientation of the surface on which it is acting is not immediately obvious (notice on the figure on this slide how the orientations of the surfaces of the yellow square upon which the red traction arrows are acting are not obvious from the orientations of the red arrows themselves). A simple construction allowing the surface orientation to be obtained from the traction vector using the stress ellipse is described by Ragan (p.226)
There are two surfaces (at right angles to each other) for which the traction vector is parallel to the pole (normal) to the surface – the traction vector then has zero shear component and so equals the normal component. These two orientations correspond with the maximum and minimum values of the magnitude of the traction vector, that is, with the two axes of the stress ellipse (the significance of this point is developed in Slide 8). Hence it follows that the stress ellipse may be defined by plotting the normal component of traction acting on planes of all possible orientations through a point (for the analysis behind this approach to defining the 2D stress ellipse, see Price & Cosgrove, p.6-7)
The conventional symbol for stress is σ. In the subscript to these stresses the first letter refers to the direction of the normal to the surface and the second letter to the direction in which the stress component of interest is acting. When the stress on one specific surface is being considered, the normal stress (= normal component of traction on that surface) is commonly written as σn and the shear stress (= shear component of traction on that surface) as τ

## Slide 7

### The 2D stress matrix

- The four numbers defining the state of stress in 2D may be written as a 2×2 matrix

- For mechanical equilibrium, σxy = - σyx

- To eliminate the minus sign so that σxy = σyx , it is convenient when working with the stress matrix to use a different sign convention for shear stresses: shear stresses are positive if

- the shear sense on the sides of the square that cut the negative coordinate axes are in a positive direction (tensor convention)

**Notes:**

For those of you who are unfamiliar with matrices, note that the terms in each row have the same first subscript and the terms in each column have the same second subscript
The leading diagonal of the matrix contains the normal stresses, and the off-diagonal terms are the shear stresses
In this case, the conditions for mechanical equilibrium are the conditions required to stop the yellow square from spinning. For this to be so, the two shear stress terms must be equal and opposite
The tensor convention for assigning the signs of the shear stresses makes the stress matrix symmetric which is mathematically very convenient. The tensor convention also works for the signs of the normal stresses – note that for the stress state on the top diagram on this slide, the stresses are compressive and if we look at the direction that the normal stress arrows are pointing on the negative faces of the square, we see that they are pointing in a +ve direction
Looking at the faces of the square that cut the -ve axes works in rock mechanics where compressive stresses are +ve. In the engineering sciences, where compressive stresses are -ve (see Slide 5), the signs of the normal and shear stresses are assigned by looking at the faces of the square that cut the +ve axes. The practical class handout illustrates this
“Tensor convention” refers to the fact that stress is a tensor quantity (see Slide 13)
Figures by Steve Covey-Crump – note how the shear stresses on the square in the top figure are both negative in the tensor convention, whereas in the Mohr circle convention the pair acting horizontally are negative (they act to spin the box clockwise) and the pair acting vertically are positive (they act to spin the box anticlockwise)

## Slide 8

### Principal stresses

- It is always possible to choose an orientation of the coordinate axes such that the shear stresses are zero parallel to these axes

- In this orientation the normal stresses parallel to the coordinate axes are described as principal stresses, σ1 and σ2, where by convention, σ1 ≥ σ2

- The greatest principal stress (σ1 ) is the semi-major axis of the stress ellipse

- The least principal stress (σ2 ) is the semi-minor axis of the stress ellipse

- Principal stresses in terms of the stresses in an arbitrary frame

- σ1, σ2   =   ½ (σxx + σyy )  ±  ½ [ (σxx - σyy )2 + 4σxy 2 ] ½

- tan 2θ   =   2σxy / (σxx - σyy )

- where θ  is the angle measured from σ1 to the normal to the plane

- Stresses on a plane in terms of the principal stresses

- σn   =   [ (σ1 + σ2 ) / 2 ]  +  [ (σ1 - σ2 ) / 2 ] cos 2θ

- τ   =   [ (σ1 - σ2 ) / 2 ] sin 2θ

**Notes:**

Following the way that matters were presented on Slides 4-6, note that when the shear component of traction is zero, the traction vector is acting parallel to the pole to the surface. Surfaces on which this is so are described as principal planes of stress
The derivation of the two sets of equations on the bottom right of this slide is given in the document accompanying this PowerPoint presentation (StressNote.pdf). The first pair of equations is helpful when we know σxx, σyy, and σxy and want to calculate the principal stresses; the second pair of equations is helpful when we want to go the other way

## Slide 9

### Mohr circle representation of stress (in 2D)

- σn   =   ½ (σ1 + σ2)  +  ½ (σ1 - σ2) cos 2θ

- τ   =   ½ (σ1 - σ2) sin 2θ

- have the same form as the parametric equations for a circle and so there is a straightforward graphical approach that allows the stresses on a plane of any orientation to be calculated. The diagram that implements this approach is known as the Mohr diagram

**Notes:**

The parametric equations for a circle centred on the x-axis are:    x = C ± r cos ϕ  and   y = ± r sin ϕ  where C is the centre of the circle, r is its radius, and ϕ is the angle that a radius of the circle makes with the +ve x-axis
Note the conventions: θ is the angle from σ1 to the normal of the plane of interest and plots on the Mohr diagram as anticlockwise (+ve) up
Note that the convention for assigning signs to the shear stresses outlined on Slide 5 must be used, NOT the tensor convention
The circle on this diagram describes all possible combinations of σn and τ, that is, how σn and τ co-vary as the orientation of the plane is varied. Hence from the diagram, given σ1 and σ2 then σn and τ may be found for any plane of interest. Alternatively, given σn and τ on any two planes, σ1 and σ2 may be determined. This was very helpful in the days before calculators, and remains very helpful now if you are not comfortable with matrix manipulation

## Slide 11

### Stress at a point in 3D

- In 3D the state of stress is fully defined by the stresses acting on any three mutually perpendicular surfaces

- Using a Cartesian coordinate frame, the component of an applied force F acting along the x-axis is Fx, and gives rise to:

- σxx = Fx / Ax  (normal stress)

- σyx = Fx / Ay  (shear stress)

- σzx = Fx / Az  (shear stress)

- Similarly, Fy → σyy, σxy, σzy,  and Fz → σzz, σxz, σyz

- The stress ellipse becomes a stress ellipsoid and three principal stresses (σ1, σ2, σ3 ) are defined

- The state of stress at a point is defined by nine numbers but for mechanical equilibrium

- σxy = - σyx  ; σyz = - σzy  ; σxz = - σzx

**Notes:**

Again (see Slide 3), strictly, the state of stress at a point is the limit of these ratios as one shrinks the areas to zero, e.g., σxx is the limit of Fx/Ax as Ax tends to zero
The stress ellipsoid defined in this way is sometimes called Lamé’s stress ellipsoid. The axes of the ellipsoid define the orientations and magnitudes of the three principal stresses. By convention, σ1 is the greatest principal stress (longest axis); σ2 is the intermediate principal stress (intermediate axis), and σ3 is the least principal stress (shortest axis). On planes oriented with one of these principal stresses as its pole, the shear stresses are zero, that is, the traction vector equals the normal stress acting on that plane

## Slide 13

### Stress as a tensor

- Stress is an example of a tensor quantity. The individual components are defined in a coordinate frame that has a particular orientation; if that frame is rotated the values of the components change. However, they change in accordance with a specific transformation equation, and so if the values are known in any one coordinate frame, they can be calculated in any other

- It is always possible to align the coordinate frame such that all the shear stresses are zero – this corresponds with alignment parallel to the axes of the stress ellipsoid

- The only non-zero terms in the stress matrix are then the three normal stresses

- These normal stresses are the three principal stresses

- σ1 = σ11  ; σ2 = σ22  ; σ3 = σ33

**Notes:**

We noted previously (Class 3) that strain is a tensor quantity
To use the transformation equation mentioned on this slide requires a familiarity with direction cosines. Many texts describe these (e.g., Nye, 1985, Physical Properties of Crystals: Their Representation by Tensors and Matrices (new edition), Oxford University Press; Newnham, 2005, Properties of Materials: Anisotropy, Symmetry, Structure, Oxford University Press) since they form a first step in the manipulation of all physical quantities that are not scalars or vectors
For those with a little more maths than A level provides (that is, for those of you who have done some elementary matrix algebra), it follows that the magnitude of the three principal stresses are the three eigenvalues of the matrix, and the orientation of these principal stresses are its three eigenvectors. These eigenvalues and eigenvectors may be calculated given the full stress matrix in any one orientation of the coordinate frame (an illustration of this procedure for the 2D case is given in the solution set to one of the exercises in the Stress practical)

## Slide 15

### Mean stress, deviatoric stress

- Any stress tensor can be split into two matrices in which the first represents the mean stress and the second the deviatoric stress

- where σm = (σ11 + σ22 + σ33) / 3

- The mean stress causes/arises from dilation (volume change) while the deviatoric stress causes/arises from distortion (shape change)

- In the lithosphere the mean stress is generally similar in magnitude to the confining pressure (which is controlled by burial depth and the density of the overlying rock column). It is usually considerably greater than the deviatoric stress

- Nevertheless, it is the deviatoric stress that is of primary interest when considering how geological structures form

**Notes:**

My “causes/arises from” alternatives are simply different perspectives: one can consider stress as the cause of strain or alternatively one can consider stress as arising as the result of strain (see the points on Slide 39)

## Slide 19

### Stress measurements

- Knowledge of the state of stress in the Earth is important for, e.g.,:

- Safe design of constructions including tunnels, quarries, dams

- Enhancing borehole stability and providing better controls on drill-head steering during well drilling

- Controlled fracture of rocks at depth to enhance oil/gas and geothermal energy production

- Improving our understanding of where and when natural hazards such as earthquakes will occur

- At a fundamental level, improving our understanding of how plate tectonics works

- Many of the methods that we use to estimate present-day levels of stress in the Earth have been developed in the engineering, mining, and energy industries, but methods have also been developed by geologists for estimating palaeostress from the microstructures of rocks that have been deformed and then exhumed at the surface

**Notes:**

For an overview of techniques for measuring present-day stresses, see Ljunggren et al., 2003, International Journal of Rock Mechanics & Mining Sciences 40: 975-989, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2003.07.003. For borehole stress measurement techniques that are widely used in the petroleum industry, see Zoback et al., 2003, International Journal of Rock Mechanics & Mining Sciences 40: 1049-1079, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2003.07.001; or Zoback, 2007, Reservoir Geomechanics, p.167-265

## Slide 20

### Stress measurements: borehole breakouts

- Borehole breakouts are zones of failure in the wall of a borehole – these give the borehole an irregular, typically elongate, shape

- The spalling of fragments occurs preferentially parallel to the minimum horizontal stress (σh), orthogonal to the maximum horizontal stress (σH)

- The shape and orientation of the hole is assessed using a dipmeter and a well imaging tool which has arms that can be pressed outwards against the walls as the tool is moved along the borehole

- The shape of holes drilled for road or tunnel blasting may also be used in this way, albeit less reliably

**Notes:**

Borehole breakouts occur parallel to the minimum horizontal stress (σh) because, assuming that the vertical stress is the greatest principal stress, this is the direction in which the deviatoric stress is greatest – the breakout results from compressive failure of the borehole wall. If the compressive failure strength of the rock is known, the width of the breakout zone may be used to quantify the magnitude of σH (see Zoback et al., 2003, International Journal of Rock Mechanics & Mining Sciences 40: 1049-1079, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2003.07.001; or Zoback, 2007, Reservoir Geomechanics, p.196-198)
Figures from Fossen, p.84-85

## Slide 21

### Stress measurements: overcoring

- Overcoring is a strain relaxation method

- A hole is drilled in the rock and strain gauges attached to the surface of the hole

- An annulus is then drilled around the hole to leave a hollow cylinder of rock on which the stress from the surrounding rock has been released

- The release of stress causes elastic deformation of the cylinder so that its dimensions and its initially circular cross section change

- The stress can be calculated from these changes provided that the elastic properties of the rock are known

- A similar approach may be taken by extracting a core of rock and measuring its expansion once it is freed from the constraints of the surrounding rock

- Such techniques are limited to depths of a few 10s metres and are commonly used in tunnels

**Notes:**

A strain gauge is a long wire. The electrical resistance of that wire equals the resistivity of the wire material multiplied by (wire length ÷ wire cross-sectional area). The length and cross-sectional area of the wire change as the sample is deformed and so the resistance changes. From a previous calibration of the change in resistance with strain, the strain of the sample may then be inferred by monitoring the change in resistance
Figure from Twiss & Moores, p.235

## Slide 22

### Stress measurements: hydraulic fracturing

- Hydrofracking is a technique for establishing the orientation and magnitude of the principal stresses

- A section of borehole is sealed off with two inflatable rubber packers and fluid is pumped in to increase the fluid pressure between these two seals

- At some critical pressure, an extension fracture forms, and at that moment the fluid pumping system is stopped

- The fluid pressure then drops as the fluid flows into the crack until a pressure is reached that is just sufficient to keep the crack open

- This latter pressure is taken as sh, and from this, together with knowledge of the tensile strength of the rock and the critical pressure at which the fracture formed, it is possible to calculate sH

- The fractures form parallel to σH and so this allows the orientation of σH to be determined

**Notes:**

The fractures form parallel to σH because the hoop stresses (stresses parallel to the circumference of the borehole) are least compressive in this direction and so this is the place where it is easiest to open an extensional fracture
Further details of the calculation of stresses using the hydrofracking technique are given by Twiss & Moores, p.234. For extra information on the subtleties of the technique, see Zoback et al., 2003, International Journal of Rock Mechanics & Mining Sciences 40: 1049-1079, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2003.07.001; and Couzens-Schultz & Chan, 2010, Journal of Structural Geology 32: 1061-1069, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2010.06.013
Figure from Twiss & Moores, p.236

## Slide 23

### Estimating stress from earthquakes and faults

- The orientation of the principal stresses and their relative magnitude as expressed by the stress ratio  Φ  = (σ2 - σ3) / (σ1 - σ3)  may be estimated from

- (a) earthquake first motion studies, or

- (b) fault plane and slip vector orientation measurements

- In each case, information from several differently oriented faults is needed to constrain the analysis

- If (σ1 - σ3) and one of the principal stresses (usually the vertical stress) can also be determined, then using Φ  the magnitudes of all the principal stresses may be obtained

- The orientation and spatial distribution of stylolites, vein systems, and conjugate fractures may all be used to estimate the orientation of the principal stresses, both in currently active tectonic settings at the Earth’s surface, and within the geological record

- The analysis of these features forms the core material of the next two classes

**Notes:**

Methods of determining stresses from fault plane and slip vector measurements are described by Ramsay & Lisle, 2000, The Techniques of Modern Structural Geology, Vol. 3: Applications of Continuum Mechanics in Structural Geology, Academic Press (Session 32). See also Fossen, p.224-230, and Yamaji, 2007, An Introduction to Tectonophysics: Theoretical Aspects of Structural Geology, Chapter 11. For a review and perspective on these methods, see Simón, 2019, Journal of Structural Geology 125: 124-133, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2018.02.011
Accompanying this PowerPoint presentation is a folder called “Stresses from Faults” which contains a worked example of how to obtain principal stress orientations from fault plane and slip vector orientations. ## Slide 27

### One principal stress is vertical

- Rocks at the Earth’s surface are in contact with air or water, neither of which can sustain a shear stress. Hence there is no shear stress at the Earth’s surface and so one of the principal stresses at the Earth’s surface must be vertical and the other two horizontal

- Existing data suggests that this is a good approximation throughout the upper crust (that is, to a depth of 15-20 km)

- The vertical stress is usually set equal to the overburden given by

- σv   =   ρrockgz

- where ρrock is the average density of the rock column to a depth of z below the surface, and g is the acceleration due to gravity

- In the continents ρrock ~2.7 g/cm3, which gives a vertical stress gradient of 26.5 MPa/km. This fits existing data well

- Stereogram of the principal stresses in southern Africa. The data are clustered in the centre and spread around the rim → one principal stress is vertical, the other two horizontal

**Notes:**

“air and water can’t sustain a shear stress”. To be strictly accurate, the shear stresses generated in flowing air or water are miniscule in comparison with tectonically induced shear stresses. Although the shear stresses in flowing air or water are miniscule in comparison with tectonic shear stress, they are important in understanding sediment transport processes
Notice that the σV = ρgz formula is effectively just a different way of saying F = ma :
	vertical stress (σV) is F/A (where A is cross-sectional area). Density (ρ) = m/V (where V is volume). Acceleration due to gravity (g) = a. Depth (z) = V/A.
	So, σV = ρgz   →   (F/A) = (m/V) a (V/A)   →   F = ma
Porous sedimentary rocks have a smaller density (ρrock = 2.1-2.5 g/cm3)

## Slide 28

### Depth variation of pore fluid pressure

- Assuming that pores are interconnected all the way to the surface, the depth variation of pore fluid pressure is

- pfluid   =   ρfluid g z   ~   g z

- where ρfluid is the pore fluid density (~ 1 g/cm3)

- If the pores are not interconnected (e.g., a porous sandstone between compacted mudstones), then the pore fluids may be over-pressured

- In such cases, pore fluid pressure varies between hydrostatic (above formula) and lithostatic (formula on previous slide) but it cannot be much greater than lithostatic

- The pore pressure parameter λ  ≡ pfluid/σv

- For pore fluids connected up to the surface

- λ   =   pfluid/σv  =  ρfluid/ρrock   ~   0.4

- so that λ  > 0.4 is a measure of over-pressure

- Blue data – vertical stress

- Red data – pore fluid pressures

**Notes:**

Note that in using formulae like p = ρgz care is required with units. In SI units we have p as Pa (= N/m2), ρ as kg/m3, g as m/s2, and z as m. It is best to work in these units to avoid confusion. However, you will get the same answer for p but in MPa if you use ρ as g/cm3, g as m/s2, and z as km. E.g., if ρ = 2.5 g/cm3, g = 10 m/s2, and z = 4 km, then p = 100 MPa, or doing it properly with ρ = 2500 kg/m3, g = 10 m/s2, and z = 4000 m, then p = 100×106 Pa = 100 MPa
Over-pressured pore fluids are defined as pore fluids with a pore fluid pressure greater than gz. Pore fluid pressure cannot exceed lithostatic pressure (or not by very much) because then the effective pressure would be tensile and rocks are weak in tension – if pore fluid pressure exceeds lithostatic pressure the rock fractures
Over-pressuring is important for borehole engineers because it may mean that the over-pressured rocks are poorly compacted/cemented and hence that the borehole is liable to collapse. Moreover, if a borehole penetrates an over-pressured fluid reservoir, the fluid will erupt to the surface

## Slide 29

### Horizontal stresses: uniaxial strain reference state

- Consider a case where there is burial with no lateral strain because the rocks to either side do not allow it (e.g., burial in a sedimentary basin)

- The vertical stress (= σ1) is given by  σv = ρrock g z  and the horizontal stress, σH = σh (= σ2 and σ3 respectively), by

- σH   =   [ ν / (1 - ν ) ] sv   =   [ ν / (1 - ν ) ] ρrock g z

- where ν  is Poisson’s ratio. For rocks 0.25 > ν  > 0.33 and so the horizontal stress is expected to be about one half to one third of the vertical stress

- The change in horizontal stress during burial and uplift for this uniaxial strain model is

- σH    =   [ ν / (1 - ν ) ] σv  +  [ E / (1 - ν ) ] α T

- where the effect of temperature changes (T ) has been included  (E = Young’s modulus; α = thermal expansion coefficient)

**Notes:**

Reference states are important for defining the state of stress with depth in circumstances where there are no tectonic stresses. They are then the starting point for considering what happens when a tectonic stress is added. The lithostatic depth variation of stress described on the previous two slides is effectively a reference state, although not one that provides a good indication of horizontal stresses
The uniaxial reference state equation on this slide is one that is widely employed to constrain the magnitude of the horizontal stresses for elastically isotropic materials. It is straightforwardly derived (from the equations for elastic deformation - see Optional PowerPoint presentation Slides 10-11) if one recalls that the strain in given direction (here the H direction) comprises three components: the strain produced by the stress in that direction ( εH1 = σH / E ) less the expansion arising from the Poisson effect induced by the stresses applied in the other two orthogonal directions  ( εH2 = -νεV = -νσV /E and εH3 = -νσh /E ), where E is the Young’s modulus of the rock and ν is Poisson’s ratio of the rock. Hence
		εH  =  εH1 + εH2 + εH3  =  (σH /E ) - (νσV /E ) - (νσh /E )  =  (1/E )[σH - ν(σV + σh)]
If we require that εH = 0 (no strain in the H direction) then
		0  =  (1/E )[σH - ν(σV + σh)]
Since (1/E ) is not zero,
		0  =  [σH - ν(σV + σh)]
Letting σh = σH (because in the uniaxial reference state the stress is the same in all horizontal directions) and rearranging
		0  =  σH - ν(σV + σH)     →     σH - νσH  =  νσV     →     σH  =  [ ν / (1 - ν) ] σV
For the inclusion of the temperature term in the above derivation, see Haxby & Turcotte, 1976, Geology 4: 181-184, https://doi.org/10.1130/0091-7613(1976)4<181:SIBTAO>2.0.CO;2
The figure is from Fossen, p.89

## Slide 32

### Tectonic stresses

- Deviations from the states of stress that we have discussed so far arise predominantly from tectonic processes

- Tectonic stresses are classified into three regimes depending upon which of the three principal stresses is vertical (Andersonian classification)

**Notes:**

Anderson noted that there are three possible arrangements of principal stresses when one is assumed to be vertical (no shear stress parallel to Earth’s surface), and so there are three possible arrangements of fault geometry with respect to these stress orientations if we assume that conjugate faults form (at ± 30° to σ1) with σ2 lying in the plane of each fault (parallel to their line of intersection). Anderson outlined his scheme in Anderson, 1905, Transactions of the Edinburgh Geological Society 8: 387-402, https://doi.org/10.1144/transed.8.3.387. See also Anderson, 1951, The Dynamics of Faulting and Dyke Formation With Application to Britain (2nd edition). The classification has since been developed to accommodate situations of transtension and transpression (see Simpson, 1997, Journal of Geophysical Research 102: 17909-17919, https://doi.org/10.1029/97JB01274)
It should be noted that the assumption that σ2 lies in the fault plane is an oversimplification and cannot be true in the case where three or more differently oriented faults that do not share a common line of intersection are simultaneously active. Hence, although the Andersonian scheme is extremely widely used, it does have limitations (for further discussion on this point, see Healy et al., 2015, Journal of Structural Geology 80: 57-71, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2015.08.013)

## Slide 34

### Other sources of stress: residual stress

- There are microscale ways in which stresses may be induced in rocks, including:

- Changes in temperature: differences in thermal expansion between two minerals in a rock induce thermal stresses during heating or cooling

- Changes in pressure: differences in compressibility between minerals induce stresses during pressurization/depressurization

- Deformation itself generates lattice defects (e.g., dislocations) which induce stresses within the lattice that remain after the deformation has ceased

- Crystallization of a melt sets up stresses

- Metamorphic reactions with a volume change induce stresses

- Stresses of these kind are stored within the rock as residual stresses that may be relieved during uplift and erosion by fracturing

### A word on lithospheric strength

- The stress at any given depth is commonly approximated as the strength of the rocks at that depth. This is given by constitutive equations that combine material specific parameters with the important deformation variables (temperature, stress, strain-rate)

- cataclastic deformation

- Intracrystalline plasticity and diffusive mass transfer

- The four most widely used constitutive equations for specifying rock strength: at any depth, the one that gives the smallest (σ1 - σ3) applies

**Notes:**

The notion that the stress in the lithosphere equals the strength of the rocks under the conditions (temperature, pressure, strain-rate, etc. – see Optional PowerPoint presentation Slide 6) that they experience, is captured by saying that the lithosphere is ‘critically stressed’. For a development of such ideas, see Zoback & Townend, 2001, Tectonophysics 336: 19-30, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0040-1951(01)00091-9; and Zoback et al., 2002, International Geology Review 44: 383-401, https://doi.org/10.2747/0020-6814.44.5.383
The meaning of the terms ‘cataclastic’, ‘intracrystalline plasticity’, and ‘diffusive mass transfer’ are given in the Optional PowerPoint presentation. You don’t need to worry about these terms in this unit – they just refer to different ways in which the rock responds to stress. Near the surface of the Earth where the rocks are relatively cool and where pressures (from the weight of overlying rock) are small, rocks tend to be brittle (fail by fracturing = cataclastic deformation); deeper where the rocks are hotter and at higher pressure, they flow without forming fractures – intracrystalline plasticity and high temperature diffusive mass transfer are two groups of processes by which they do this
Note that the four equations on this slide are gross simplifications of real material strength but, nevertheless, they are widely regarded as useful first order approximations. The equations for cataclastic deformation – really just one equation rewritten for σ1 vertical (extension) and for σ3 vertical (compression) – are essentially independent of rock type. This independence of rock type is known as Byerlee’s law, and we will return to it in Class 7. The equations for intracrystalline plasticity and diffusive mass transfer do not include a variable that accounts for the mechanical condition of the deforming material and so are described as steady state creep laws

## Slide 39

### Stress or strain?

- Throughout this presentation the material has been presented as if stress is the cause of deformation. This is not always appropriate

- The cause of a mechanical process is determined by boundary conditions, that is, by the conditions that are externally imposed on a body

- If stresses are imposed and maintained on the boundaries then stress is the cause of the deformation and strain is the response to it

- If, however, the boundaries are required to move by a prescribed amount or at a prescribed rate, then strain is the cause of the process and stresses develop in response to it (e.g., the uniaxial strain reference state)

- Many of our measurements in structural geology are actually of strain rather than stress and so care needs to be taken when inferring stresses from those data, particularly when the strains involved are finite rather than infinitesimal

**Notes:**

The uniaxial strain reference state (Slide 29) is built on the notion that the horizontal stresses arise because there is no strain in the horizontal direction – the stresses arise from the imposed zero horizontal strain
When we look at geological structures, we are looking at strains not stresses. Hence drawing stress directions on maps from observations of geological structures is a risky business – we are implicitly making an interpretation of how the stresses arise from the strains. We shall see in the next couple of lectures that the temptation to do this is considerable – that doesn’t make it wrong, but we do need to acknowledge what we are doing
A discussion of this matter, as applied to the extraction of the orientations and relative magnitudes of the principal stresses from fault slip data, is given by Twiss & Unruh, 1998, Journal of Geophysical Research 103: 12205-12222, https://doi.org/10.1029/98JB00612
