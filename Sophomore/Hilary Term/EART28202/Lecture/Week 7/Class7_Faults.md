## Slide 2

### Faults

- A fault is a surface or narrow zone along which there is measurable (at the exposure scale) surface-parallel displacement

- This definition is identical to that of a shear fracture except for the scale of the displacement

- Although described as a surface, faults have thickness (they are zones of deformation)

- A fault may therefore be considered as a tabular volume of rock comprising a central core formed by intense shearing, together with a surrounding volume of rock (damage zone) that has been less intensely affected by brittle deformation

## Slide 4

### Fault surface dip

- A fault divides the rocks that it cuts into two blocks. For faults that are not vertical, the block below the fault is the footwall and the block above the fault is the hanging wall. For vertical faults the sides of the fault are named by their geographical location, e.g., west side

- Faults are described by their dip: low-angle faults dip less than ~30°; high-angle faults dip more than ~60°

- Listric faults flatten downwards (common); anti-listric faults steepen downwards (unusual)

- For faults with a staircase geometry in cross section, the parts that are at a relatively high angle to the layering are ramps and the parts that are sub-parallel to the layering are flats. We will refine the ramp-flat terminology in Classes 8-10

**Notes:**

For vertical faults there isn’t a side that is below the fault and a side that is above it, that is, there is no footwall and hanging wall. Hence, in this case we have to refer to west side, east side, etc.
Listric and anti-listric faults are curved faults

## Slide 5

### Slip vector

- The vector connecting the location of a point on the fault plane before and after faulting is the slip vector

- The slip vector may be separated into:

- strike-slip component (parallel to the strike of the fault) and which has dextral or sinistral sense

- dip-slip component (parallel to the true dip of the fault) and which has normal or reverse sense

- Hence, we have pure dip-slip, pure strike-slip, or more generally, oblique-slip faults

- If the slip vector changes orientation along strike, the fault is described as a rotational fault

**Notes:**

Remember that to define the sense of strike-slip displacement, stand on one side of the fault (it doesn’t matter which) and see which direction the other side has moved: if it is to the left it is sinistral and if it is to the right it is dextral. Sinistral and dextral derive from the Latin, ‘sinister’ and ‘dexter’ – the Romans were suspicious of left-handed people
Remember that to define the sense of dip-slip displacement, look at the direction that the hanging wall has moved: if it has moved down the fault plane it is normal and if it has moved up the fault plane it is reverse. This terminology comes from the Welsh coal miners – they mostly encountered normal faults
The figure top right which defines the slip vector shows a dextral-normal oblique-slip fault

## Slide 7

### The orientation and magnitude of the slip vector

- The orientation and magnitude of the slip vector may be determined if we have

- the strike/dip of the fault

- and either

- the direction of slip on the fault plane (typically from slickensides) and one apparent offset of a surface with known strike/dip

- or

- two apparent offsets of surfaces with known (and different) strike/dips

- The orientation and magnitude of the slip vector may be found by constructing a fault plane section with the aid of a stereogram (as we did in Practical 4)

- In areas with topography, the fault plane section may be constructed using stratum contours

## Slide 8

### Separation of layers

- When we have the strike/dip of the fault and the offset of only one planar feature (e.g., a lithological contact), the term separation is used

- Evaluating separation terms commonly utilizes cut-off lines = the intersection of a surface of interest with the fault plane. There are two of these – one for the footwall and one for the hanging wall

- Strike separation is the apparent offset parallel to the strike of the fault; all other separation terms are defined in the vertical plane that contains the dip direction of the fault

**Notes:**

When you are mapping faults, it is commonly the case that you have some knowledge of the strike/dip of the fault (perhaps obtainable using stratum contours), and you have the apparent offset of some contact of known dip. In this situation you do not have enough information to determine the slip vector, but you can evaluate the separation terms using trigonometry. This strategy is widely employed within the petroleum and mining industries so that something quantitative can be said about the magnitude of slip
The spacing between the footwall and hanging wall cut-off lines when plotted on a map is the heave (see figure top right)

### Folded surfaces near faults

- The layering cut by faults often bends in a way that allows the movement sense to be determined. These structures are described as drag folds

- Drag folds form most easily when the cut-off lines are at a high angle to the slip vector, and so in sub-horizontal sedimentary rocks they are most commonly seen on high angle normal and reverse faults

## Slide 12

- Modelling the geometry of fault propagation folds

- Growth of parallel kink-like folds: loss of slip ahead of the fault-tip is accommodated by growth of the crest of the fold

- Trishear approach: loss of slip ahead of the fault-tip is accommodated in a triangular zone of shear planes that radiate from the fault tip

**Notes:**

Attempts to model the formation and geometry of fault propagation folds mostly fall into the two categories that are illustrated on this slide. For an overview, see Hughes & Shaw, 2015, Geological Society of America Bulletin 127: 1752-1765, https://doi.org/10.1130/B31215.1. There are also helpful descriptions given in Rick Allmendinger’s Structural Geology Lab Manual, p.213-218 (available at https://www.rickallmendinger.net/)

For an account of how fault-propagation folding may be modelled using the kink-like fold approach, see Suppe & Medwedeff, 1990, Eclogae Geologicae Helvetiae 83: 409-454, https://www.e-periodica.ch/digbib/view?pid=egh-001%3A1990%3A83%3A%3A230#418
For a summary of the development and use of the trishear model, see Hardy & Allmendinger, 2011, American Association of Petroleum Geologists Memoir 94: 95-119, https://doi.org/10.1306/13251334M943429

In the trishear model strain is distributed in a triangular zone ahead of the fault tip. The hanging wall side of the triangle moves at constant velocity above a fixed footwall, the triangular zone is symmetrical with respect to the fault, and the velocity vector is the same for all points located along any ray originating from the fault tip. Hence across the triangle zone the velocity increases towards the hanging wall and the velocity vector gradually changes orientation accordingly (see top diagram of middle figure on the slide). This generates the folding. The triangular zone moves through the layers as the fault tip propagates and no further folding occurs once the fault has cut through it. The precise geometry of the folding depends upon the choice of the apical angle, the fault dip and magnitude of slip, and the ratio of how fast the fault tip propagates relative to the slip on the fault. Trial and error is used to vary these parameters until the results match the geometry obtained from field observations. For downloadable software that implements trishear modelling, see program FaultFold on Rick Allmendinger’s site (https://www.rickallmendinger.net/)

Figure middle (from Fossen, p.200) showing the parameters used in trishear modelling and an example of its implementation for folds developed ahead of the tip of a reverse fault
Figure right (from Fossen, p.199) showing trishear modelling of folds ahead of an upward propagating normal fault

## Slide 14

### Folded surfaces near faults: rollover antiforms

- Alternatively, folding may occur where faults change orientation along their length – either in map view (change in fault strike) or in section (change in fault dip)

- Changes in fault dip require internal deformation within the hanging wall to avoid space problems – in normal faults if the fault steepens downwards a hanging wall synform is required whereas if it shallows a hanging wall antiform is needed

- On a listric normal fault the downwardly decreasing dip commonly leads to a  roll-over antiform within the hanging wall

- Note that in this case the direction of bending is opposite to that in drag folds and so this is sometimes described as reverse drag

## Slide 15

### Slickensides

- In response to movement, fault surfaces commonly become smooth and polished and are then described as slickensides (sensu stricto)

- Fault surfaces (including slickensides), particularly where they are not perfectly planar, commonly develop three types of strongly oriented lineations, that are also described as slickensides (sensu lato)

- Ridges and grooves formed by gouging of the fault surface and by accumulation of debris behind asperities

- Slickenfibres formed by growth of minerals parallel to fault slip vector during slow shearing

- Mineral streaks formed from pulverized and streaked out mineral debris

**Notes:**

Note that “slickensides” is not always used in accordance with its precise definition. Strictly, “slickensides” refers to the polished surface. However, it is often used in a more general sense to refer to the lineations (variously described as slickenlines, slickenside lineations, striations, slickenfibres) on a fault surface
Highly polished (mirror-like) fault surfaces are commonly observed on faults formed at shallow crustal depths. In carbonate-rich lithologies they arise from decomposition (chemical breakdown) of the carbonates during seismic ruptures – the solid reaction products of the decomposition (for dolomite = MgO + Mg-calcite) are nanometric-size particles that form thin (10-100 µm) layers on the fault surface. As such, they are indicators of fast (seismic) slip-rates (see Slide 30). For further discussion, see Fondriest et al., 2013, Geology 41: 1175-1178, https://doi.org/10.1130/G34641.1
Photo (from a PowerPoint presentation by Richard Lisle): a highly polished and lineated fault surface, Comillas, northern Spain

### Failure criteria – intact rocks

- In the brittle regime rocks become stronger with increasing confining pressure. We may show this graphically by plotting the maximum principal stress at failure against confining pressure (in axial compression experiments these are σ1 and σ3 respectively)

- Alternatively, we may plot a Mohr diagram of the values of σn  and τ  on the fault plane at failure for each confining pressure – if the σ1 vs. σ3 plot is a straight line, so is the failure envelope on a Mohr diagram

- intercept (τ0) = cohesive strength     ;     slope (μ = tan ϕ  ~ 0.6) = coefficient of internal friction

- the angle (α) between the fault plane and σ1 follows from the slope and is typically ~30°

- f

- t0

**Notes:**

The figures show the stress at failure for experiments at three different confining pressures plotted as σ1 vs. σ3, and the same data plotted on a Mohr diagram. In the Mohr diagram the red dots show the stresses on the fault plane that formed when the sample failed; the red line is the failure envelope. Because the failure envelope is a straight line, the angle between the fault plane and σ1 is the same in all three tests. [Note that when we described Mohr diagrams in Class 5 Slide 9, we specified the orientation of a plane by the angle (θ) between σ1 and the normal to the plane, not the angle (α) between σ1 and the plane. However, note that α = 90° - θ. I showed the Mohr circle construction using α instead of θ in Class 5 Slide 10
By looking at the Mohr diagram on this slide, it is apparent from the fact that the internal angles of a triangle add up to 180°, that for a linear failure envelope α = 45° - (ϕ/2). Since ϕ ~ 30° then α ~ 30°
The equation τ = τ0 + σn tan ϕ, which describes a linear failure envelope, is known as the Coulomb failure criterion. Rocks actually tend to have slightly non-linear failure envelopes. When this is the case, the equation describing the failure envelope, written as τ = f(σn), is known as the Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion

## Slide 23

### Andersonian classification of faults

- If one of the principal stresses is vertical and α ~ 30°, then

- when σ1 is vertical  →  we have normal faults dipping at ~60°

- when σ2 is vertical  →  we have vertical strike-slip faults

- when σ3 is vertical  →  we have reverse faults dipping at ~30°

**Notes:**

For the reasoning behind the approximation that one of the principal stresses is vertical, see Class 5 Slide 27
This slide describes the Andersonian classification of faults – see Anderson, 1905, Transactions of the Edinburgh Geological Society 8: 387-402, https://doi.org/10.1144/transed.8.3.387, and Anderson, 1951, The Dynamics of Faulting and Dyke Formation With Application to Britain (2nd edition). We briefly noted this classification in Class 5 Slide 32. The classification adopts the idealization that σ2 lies in the plane of the fault, and hence leads to the notion that faults have a tendency to form conjugate pairs at ±30° to σ1 (with σ2 being parallel to the line of intersection of the two faults). This classification is extremely widely used. However, it is commonly found, both in nature and experiment, that three or more differently oriented faults are simultaneously active, not just two conjugate faults. In such cases σ2 cannot be in the plane of the active faults unless they all share a common line of intersection. The significance of this reality, and the consequent limitations of the Andersonian fault classification scheme, are discussed by Healy et al., 2015, Journal of Structural Geology 80: 57-71, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2015.08.013

## Slide 25

### Failure criteria – previously faulted rocks

- Natural faulting does not generally take place in “intact” rocks but rather it occurs in rocks that contain pre-existing fractures. In the case of major faults, faulting occurs by reactivating old faults. Faulting, therefore, is a matter of attaining the stresses required for frictional sliding

- The failure envelope for frictional sliding has a similar slope (coefficient of internal friction) to that for intact rocks, but the cohesive strength (τ0) is smaller

- Almost all lithologies (except those rich in sheet silicates) have a similar coefficient of frictional sliding

- at large stresses, μ ~ 0.6 → ϕ ~ 30°

- This is Byerlee’s law

**Notes:**

Figure (redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Byerlee, 1978, Pure and Applied Geophysics 116: 615-626, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00876528) showing the stresses on the fault plane that are required to produce frictional sliding. The figure incorporates data from a wide range of different lithologies
Byerlee’s law (the equations stated in the figure) is widely used to describe the strength of the upper crust. Conversely, by making the assumption that the crust is on the verge of failure (that is, that the crust is “critically stressed”), Byerlee’s law may be used to estimate the state of stress in the crust. The critically-stressed condition of the crust is discussed by Townend & Zoback, 2000, Geology 28: 399-402, https://doi.org/10.1130/0091-7613(2000)28<399:HFKTCS>2.0.CO;2, and by Zoback & Townend, 2001, Tectonophysics 336: 19-30, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0040-1951(01)00091-9 (see also Class 5 Slide 37)
At σn = 200 MPa the value of τ given by the two parts of Byerlee’s law is the same:  0.85σn = 50 + 0.6σn  →  0.25σn = 50  →  σn = 200
Note that Byerlee’s law is not really a ‘law’ but rather it is an empirical observation

## Slide 26

### Failure criteria – the effect of pore fluid pressure

- Pore fluid pressure promotes brittle failure – on a Mohr diagram, if we increase pore fluid pressure, we shift the Mohr circle describing a stable state of stress (one below the failure envelope) to the left; if the pore fluid pressure is large enough, the failure envelope is intersected and failure occurs

- Note that the effect of pore fluids is NOT to lubricate the fault, but rather to jack the fault apart so that there is less normal stress pushing the fault walls together

**Notes:**

The points made on this slide were noted in the last class (see Class 6 Slide 16). The fact that pore fluid pressure only affects normal stresses not shear stresses (so that the Mohr circle shifts left without changing its diameter) was also noted in Class 5 Slide 16
Figure by Steve Covey-Crump. In the presence of pore fluids, the normal stress (x-axis) becomes the effective normal stress (= normal stress minus pore fluid pressure) – the failure envelope is a relationship between shear stress and effective normal stress at failure (on the previous slides, it is implicitly assumed that the rocks are dry, that is, pore fluid pressure is zero, and so the effective normal stress = the normal stress)

## Slide 27

### From microcracking to failure

- Brittle failure occurs through the formation and coalescence of microcracks; these develop around flaws in the material

- The formation of a new crack relieves the local stresses and so the next crack forms elsewhere in the sample

- However, stresses are concentrated around crack tips → regions with high crack density (and hence more crack tips) come to be more highly stressed → when there are sufficient cracks to make this effect significant, microfracturing focuses in these regions → coalescence of cracks and localization of the deformation on a through-going fracture

**Notes:**

Stresses around cracks may be analysed by treating the crack as having an elliptical shape (in 2D). The stresses around elliptical holes (flaws) within an externally loaded plate may be calculated to determine (a) where around the flaw they are at a maximum (this will be the point at which the crack will grow), and (b) at what externally applied load crack propagation will occur. This forms the foundation of one of the most important theories of brittle failure (that of Griffith)
Figure (from Mayr et al., 2011, Geophysics 76(3): MA21-MA32, https://doi.org/10.1190/1.3569579) showing the location of microcrack events at different times in an axially compressed sandstone sample as it was being loaded to failure. The colour coding in the two middle diagrams represents two different sub-intervals of time (black before green, and blue before red)

### From microcracking to fracture propagation

- If a macroscopic fracture already exists, microcracking is focused in the region just ahead of the fracture tip where the stresses are greatest. This complex region of deformation is called the process zone

- This concentration of microcracking activity is why most faults have a well-defined core of intense cataclastic deformation and a surrounding damage zone of less intense fracturing

## Slide 30

- Dynamic fracturing

- When fracture propagation is fast (as in earthquakes), damage around the fault is linked to the transient stress conditions at the fracture tip (dynamic fracturing)

- Pervasive fracturing occurs, particularly on the side of the fault where the transient stresses are tensile

- t / d ~ 1

- Magnitude and orientation of σ1 at the tip of a shear fracture propagating to the right at 0.9Vs

- t / d ~ 0.1

- Microstructural features that are unique to seismic rates of slip (e.g., friction melts, particular fracture patterns) provide evidence for palaeo-earthquakes

- Fault zone thickness is greater at given fault displacement than in fault core-damage zone scenarios

**Notes:**

Damage that occurs during the transient stress conditions arising during fast fracture propagation is commonly described as ‘coseismic’. These stresses arise because the slip velocity rises from zero behind the tail of the rupture up to a maximum and then back down to zero ahead of the rupture tip, that is, because the slip-rate varies along the rupture. Dynamic fracturing occurs for fault slip rates of > 1 ms-1 (or perhaps even at slip rates > 0.1 mms-1)
During dynamic fracture, loading is so fast that inertial terms need to be included in the equations of motion that are used to determine the deformation response. Theoretically, the terminal velocity of a fast-propagating fracture is the Rayleigh wave velocity (VR). This is about 0.92 × shear wave velocity (Vs), or more precisely,   VR/Vs  ~  [0.862 + (1.14ν)] / (1+ν)  where ν ~ 0.25 for rocks = Poisson’s ratio
For a review of features that may indicate seismic slip-rates (palaeo-earthquakes) rather than slower slip, see Rowe & Griffith, 2015, Journal of Structural Geology 78: 1-26, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2015.06.006. For such features in limestones, see e.g., Delle Piane et al., 2017, Journal of Structural Geology 103: 17-36, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2017.09.003; Delle Piane et al., 2018, Geology 46: 163-166, https://doi.org/10.1130/G39584.1. For an analysis of quartz deformation microstructures that was used to infer an interval of coseismic deformation within a period of much slower deformation, see Trepmann & Seybold, 2019, Geoscience Frontiers 10: 43-54, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gsf.2018.05.002
Figure top right (from Di Toro et al., 2005, Nature 436: 1009-1012, https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03910) showing the calculated maximum principal stress magnitude (colour coding) and orientation (black lines) around a fracture propagating to the right at a speed of 0.9 × shear wave velocity (~0.98 of the Rayleigh wave velocity). Stresses in the top half of the figure are compressive; those in the bottom half are tensile. Since rocks are generally weaker in tension than in compression, fractures will develop preferentially in the tensile areas and have an orientation locally parallel to the black lines. Di Toro et al. observed melt filled fractures (pseudotachylytes) in this orientation around the Gole Larghe Fault in northern Italy
Figure bottom left (from Fondriest et al., 2017, Earth and Planetary Science Letters 461: 8-19, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.epsl.2016.12.024) showing the relationship between fault zone thickness and fault displacement observed for normal/reverse/strike-slip faults (a relationship noted in Class 4 Slide 5) with, in red, observations made on a fault within dolomites in the Foiana Fault Zone, northern Italy. The larger red star shows the thickness/displacement ratio in a region of suspected coseismic damage – note that it lies well above the trend of the other data, that is, at given fault displacement the thickness of the damage zone was much larger

### Displacement variation along a fault  2

- Given adequate data, displacement profiles showing displacement vs. distance along a fault, may be constructed. These are classified as peak, bell-shaped, and plateau types

- Large faults slip along a limited portion of their surface and so the total displacement is the sum of displacements from many individual events. The shape of the displacement profile therefore depends upon the size and location of the individual events

- Hence fault displacement profiles can provide information about the growth history of the fault and the nature of interactions with other faults

### Fault linking – relay ramps

- As faults grow, they become more likely to interact with nearby faults. Two faults may join to form a single much longer fault. Growth in this manner gives rise to a number of important structures

- A

- D

- B

- C

**Notes:**

Consider two faults whose tips approach each other. Before the tips reach each other but after their strain fields have started to interfere, they are said to underlap. Once the tips have passed each other, they are said to overlap. The zone of overlapping (relay ramp) becomes deformed as the result of displacement transfer from one fault to the other. Eventually this relay structure fails (breached relay ramp), and the two faults become connected. Typically, there is a fault bend at the connection point
For an example of a study that seeks to use measurements of underlap and overlap to establish criteria for whether or not fractures will link in this way, see Lamarche et al., 2018, Journal of Structural Geology 108: 171-179, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2017.11.016. Linking of fractures is significant because fracture connectivity controls rock permeability

### Fault bends (changes in dip)

- If fault slip is fast, the decompression may be explosive, leading to brecciation

## Slide 48

### When faults end

- The end of a fault (where the discontinuity in displacement goes to zero) is the tip-line. A tip-line must form a closed loop – it cannot simply end. For faults that break the surface (faults that do not are described as blind faults), the tip-line connects the ends of the trace of the fault in a line that loops around below the surface

- The only variant on this geometrical necessity occurs if a fault ends by joining another fault of the same type. The line of intersection between two faults is then described as a branch-line

- In some cases, a fault block is completely surrounded by faults and is then described as a horse

- A full understanding of these geometric aspects is critical when mapping faulted terranes if a restorable cross-section is to be constructed

**Notes:**

In the remaining classes we will attempt to make sense of these remarks since they go to the heart of the task of creating a self-consistent structural interpretation
A restorable cross-section is one that can be undeformed to reproduce the original stratigraphy before deformation. A section that does not restore is one that leaves gaps or locates two bits of section in the same space when one attempts to reverse the deformation – such a cross-section is WRONG unless it arises from something ignored in the method used to restore the section
