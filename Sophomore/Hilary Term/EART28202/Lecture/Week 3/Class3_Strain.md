## Slide 3

### Changes in size  (measures of linear strain)

- Changes in size can be described as changes in length measured parallel to a set of reference directions; a change in length parallel to a given direction is described as a linear strain (or sometimes as a longitudinal strain)

- Several different parameters are used to specify linear strain, each of which has its own advantages in different applications:

- Engineering convention

- Engineering strain (extension):   e  =  ΔL / L0  =  ( L - L0 ) / L0

- where L is the final length of the line, and L0 is the starting length. It may be positive or negative depending upon whether the line has increased or decreased in length

- Stretch:   s  =  L / L0  =  ( 1 + e )

- Quadratic elongation:   λ  =  ( L / L0 ) 2  =  ( 1 + e ) 2

- Reciprocal quadratic elongation is also useful:   λ ´ = 1 / λ

- Logarithmic strain (natural strain):   ε  =  ln ( L / L0 )  =  ln ( 1 + e )

- exx = linear strain //x

**Notes:**

In the engineering sciences an increase in length is defined as positive; in rock mechanics a decrease in length is defined as positive. This is because in engineering the consequences of tensile forces are usually more relevant (materials are much weaker when being pulled apart than when being compressed), whereas in rock mechanics rocks at depth are almost always in a state of compression (due to the weight of the overlying rock) and so shortening is usually more relevant; the different sign definition simply removes the excessive appearance of negative signs in the relevant mechanical equations. The equation for engineering strain on this slide follows the engineering convention (lengthening is +ve); in the rock mechanics convention we have instead  e = L / L0 = (L0 - L) / L0 which is simply the negative of the engineering convention
It is common practice to multiply strains by 100 to quote them as a percentage, e.g., a strain of 0.001 = 0.1%
When specified within a coordinate frame, linear strains should be accompanied by a subscript indicating the direction in which the strain is given, e.g., exx is the strain parallel to the x axis, eyy is the strain parallel to the y axis, and ezz is the strain parallel to the z axis. The doubling of the subscript reflects the matrix notation used for specifying the state of strain at a point (see Slides 9-13)

### Changes in shape  (measures of shear strain)

- Changes in shape can be described as changes in angle between two lines; the change in angle between two lines is described as a shear strain

- The change in angle between two lines that were originally perpendicular is known as the angular shear, ψ

- Angular shear is positive for an anticlockwise rotation, and negative for a clockwise rotation

- Engineering shear strain

- γ   =   tan ψ

- Gamma prime is also useful:

- γ ´   =   γ/λ

- Tensor shear strain is one half the engineering shear strain

- eij  =  γij/2    ( i ≠ j )

**Notes:**

Different people have used different sign conventions for shear strain; the one shown on this slide is now standard. To establish the sign, consider how the pole to the shear plane has rotated. On a 2d plot like that shown here, a clockwise rotation is negative. For those of you familiar with the right hand rule for working out the sign of a rotation, note that the sign convention given on this slide is consistent with that: using your right hand, put your thumb along the x axis and your first finger along the y axis; your second finger (z axis) is now pointing towards you; now put your thumb pointing along the z axis towards you and you will see that your fingers curl in an anticlockwise sense, that is, anticlockwise is +ve
Note that the definition of engineering shear strain follows naturally from elementary trigonometry:  tan ψ  = x/y = γ
Double subscripts are needed when specifying shear strain in a coordinate frame. These indicate (1) the orientation of the shear plane and (2) the direction of shear in that plane

## Slide 6

### Displacement, strain, deformation

- Quantifying deformation involves comparing the size, position, and orientation of some geological feature of interest in its initial and final states. In doing this, it is convenient to think of the feature as being made up of a continuum of particles and to compare where each of these particles are in the initial and deformed state

- Displacement describes the change in position of a given particle

- Strain describes the relative change in position of particles within the feature, that is, it describes the change in distances between those particles. When these are small, strain

- is described as infinitesimal; when these are large (measurable) strain is described as finite

- Deformation refers to displacement; it may or may not involve strain, e.g., rigid displacement of a body is deformation, but it is not strain

- Deformation without (mostly) strain

**Notes:**

In structural geology, strain rather than displacement is of particular interest in because it is strain that leads to, or is the consequence of (depending upon how you look at it), internal forces (stresses) within a body
Photo (Gudmundsson, 2011, Rock Fractures in Geological Processes, p.65): slip on a reverse fault that ran underneath this house in Taiwan in 1999 produced deformation of the house that was primarily by rigid body rotation (see next slide for a definition of rigid body rotation). The lack of strain is a testament to the construction methods used when building the house

## Slide 7

### Displacement

- There are four basic types of displacement:

- Rigid body translation – every particle in the body moves in the same direction and by the same distance

- Rigid body rotation – uniform physical rotation of the body; the displacement vectors of the particles are not all the same (they depend upon the location of the particles relative to the axis of rotation) but the distance between particles remains unchanged

- Distortion (shape change) – movement of particles so that they change position relative to each other; the displacement vectors are not all the same

- Dilation (change in volume without change in shape)

- It follows that while deformation involves all four types of displacement, only distortion and dilation produce strain (i.e., change the distance between particles)

**Notes:**

Figure by Steve Covey-Crump. The displacement vectors (red arrows) connect the position of a particle before deformation with its position after deformation. An array of such vectors, defined for many particles within a body, is described as a displacement field

## Slide 8

### Deformation gradient matrix

- In 2d, if the original position of a particle is denoted by ( x , y ) and the position after deformation by ( x* , y* ), then we may write

- x*   =   D11 x  +  D12 y  +  A1

- y*   =   D21 x  +  D22 y  +  A2

- The deformation gradient matrix is here defined to give the deformed position using the original position (Lagrangian specification) but it may alternatively be defined to give the original position using the deformed position (Eulerian specification)

- Note that by working with gradients, the effect of rigid body translation is eliminated – it is generally not possible to determine the exact amount of rigid translation that a rock has experienced since its formation

**Notes:**

Since only distortion and dilation produce strain, the mathematical treatment of strain begins with the steps needed to separate out the rigid translation and rigid rotation from the overall deformation. This is done by working with displacement gradients (see next slide)

## Slide 9

### Displacement gradient matrix

- The deformation gradient matrix includes rigid body rotation which, like rigid translation, does not contribute to the internal deformation of the body

- To separate out the rigid rotation, we work with displacements,

- u   =   x*  -  x     ;     v   =   y*  -  y

- and we examine how displacement varies in the neighbourhood of a point (i.e., we examine the displacement gradients)

- We split the displacement gradient matrix into symmetric and antisymmetric parts; the former gives strain (distortion + dilation), and the latter the rotation

**Notes:**

Note that the displacement gradient matrix can be specified with respect to the original particle positions (Lagrangian specification) or with respect to the deformed particle positions (Eulerian specification). If the displacements are small enough then the displacement gradients are the same whether they are calculated with respect to the original positions or with respect to the deformed positions. Treating the displacements as small enough for this to be true is described as the infinitesimal strain approximation. It leads to a great simplification of the mathematics and is valid for many rock mechanics problems of interest (e.g., deformation around boreholes, deformation during the passage of a seismic wave)
The use of bold text for u in the bottom diagram indicates a vector, here with components u parallel to the x-axis and v parallel to the y-axis. The upside-down triangle (∇) in the bottom diagram is known as the ‘nabla operator’ (or ‘del operator’) which simply tells the reader to take derivatives of the quantity on which it is acting (here the displacement vector, u). In this case ∇u is shorthand for the displacement gradient matrix shown in the top right diagram. The superscript T in the matrix equation refers to the transpose of the matrix ∇u, that is, it is obtained by swapping the rows and columns of the matrix. The matrix equation shown in the bottom diagram looks horrible, but it is actually extremely simple once you have a familiarity with how to add matrices together
The outcome of this slide: when we refer to strain, we are actually referring to the symmetric part of the displacement gradient matrix. To reiterate what was said on Slide 6: strain describes only the part of the deformation (albeit the interesting part) that is the result of (or the cause of) the distortion producing forces within the body

### Longitudinal strains

- The physical meaning of the strain components on the leading diagonal of the strain matrix can be examined by considering three points P at ( x , y ), Q at ( x + Δ x , y ), and R at ( x , y + Δ y ), that are deformed without change in angle to new positions P*, Q*, R*

- Longitudinal strains are change in length/original length

which from the diagram is

## Slide 12

### Tensor shear strains

- The physical meaning of the off-diagonal terms in the strain matrix becomes apparent if we allow a change in angle between the lines PQ and PR

- The off-diagonal terms are half the tangent of the change in angle between two initially orthogonal lines, that is, they are the tensor shear strains

- Note that since the strain matrix is the symmetric part of the displacement gradient matrix,

- exy  =  eyx

**Notes:**

Again, the origin of the diagram (by Steve Covey-Crump) shown on this slide is explained in the document, StrainNote.pdf, that accompanies this PowerPoint presentation. As noted on the previous slide, the expressions for the strain components are only valid for infinitesimal strains
Using basic trigonometry (tan ψ = opposite / adjacent), if we look at the P*Q*R* part of the diagram we see
	tan α  =  [ (∂u/∂y)y ] / [y + (∂v/∂y)y]  ≈  [ (∂u/∂y)y ] / [y] =  (∂u/∂y)  where the approximation applies provided that  (∂v/∂y) << 1 so that y >> (∂v/∂y)y
	tan β  =  [ (∂v/∂x)x ] / [x + (∂u/∂x)x]  ≈  [ (∂v/∂x)x ] / [x] = (∂v/∂x) where the approximation applies provided that  (∂u/∂x) << 1 so that x >> (∂u/∂x)x
Hence
	½ [(∂u/∂y) + (∂v/∂x)]  =  ½ [tan α + tan β ]
Also
	tan α + tan β  ≈  tan (α+β )  provided α and β are small
Hence
	½ [tan α + tan β ]   ≈   ½ tan (α+β )
½ tan (α+β ) is half the change in angle between two lines that were originally orthogonal, and so it equals half the engineering shear strain

## Slide 13

### Strain as a 2nd rank tensor

- The values of the components in the strain matrix change as the orientation of the coordinate frame is changed, but they do so in a very specific way, that is, they obey a particular transformation equation that is used to define a class of physical quantities called 2nd rank tensors

- A tensor is a mathematical quantity that describes the physical state/properties of a material

- Tensors are distinguished as being of rank n where 3n is the number of terms required to specify them (in 3d); in 3d the strain is a 3 x 3 matrix (9 components, n = 2)

- e12 = e21

- e13 = e31

- e23 = e32

- Strain at a point is not a single number

- x,y,z labelling of axes replaced by x1,x2,x3

- Symmetric part of displacement gradient matrix

**Notes:**

Scalars (i.e., physical quantities with magnitude only, e.g., temperature) are 0th rank tensors since they require 30 = 1 term to specify them; vectors (i.e., physical quantities with magnitude and direction, e.g., force) are 1st rank tensors since they require 31 = 3 terms to specify them. Quantities like strain that relate two vectors (for strain that is the displacement vector and the position vector) are 2nd rank tensors and require 32 = 9 terms to specify them. Another 2nd rank tensor that we will meet in Class 5 is stress (this relates force and the vector describing the size and orientation of the area over which it is acting). Quantities that relate a vector and a 2nd rank tensor (e.g., piezoelectricity which relates stress and the electric dipole moment (polarization) vector or conversely, strain and electric field) are 3rd rank tensors requiring 33 = 27 terms to specify them, while quantities that relate two 2nd rank tensors (e.g., elasticity, which relates stress and strain) are 4th rank tensors requiring 34 = 81 terms to specify them. In each case, in order to be a tensor, the components of the tensor must change as the orientation of the coordinate frame used to specify them is rotated, in a way that obeys a particular equation defined for tensors of its rank
Note that given the state of strain at a point in one coordinate frame, the state of strain in a coordinate frame of different orientation may be calculated using the 2nd rank tensor transformation equation
The classic text describing tensors is Nye, 1985, Physical Properties of Crystals: Their Representation by Tensors and Matrices (paperback edition), Oxford University Press

## Slide 14

### The strain ellipse (2d)

- Strain in two dimensions is conveniently visualized using the strain ellipse. This ellipse represents the deformed shape of an initially circular marker, and illustrates how linear strain and angular shear vary with direction

- There are two perpendicular directions (the major and minor axes of the ellipse) where the extensions are at maximum and minimum values and the angular shear is zero. These max and min values are termed the principal strains, e1 and e2 (where, by definition, e1 ³ e2)

**Notes:**

The dashed circle (omitted from the right-hand figure) is the undeformed circle, and it has a radius of 1; the ellipse is what that circle turns into after deformation. We could think about this in an alternative way – the ellipse describes how the value of (1+exx) changes as the coordinate frame is rotated
Note the spelling of ‘principal’.
If the (x,y) coordinate frame is aligned with the principal strains (the axes of the ellipse) then all the off-diagonal terms in the strain matrix are zero (the shear strains are zero) and the values of terms on the leading diagonal of the matrix are e1 and e2. Those of you with some knowledge of matrix algebra may therefore appreciate that the principal strains are the eigenvalues of the strain matrix and the directions of the principal strains are the eigenvectors of the strain matrix

## Slide 15

### The strain ellipsoid (3d)

- Extending the strain ellipse idea to 3d, we have the strain ellipsoid. This represents the deformed shape of an initially spherical object

- The three semi-axes of the ellipsoid are the three principal strains; these directions are commonly described as the X, Y, Z directions, and their magnitudes are

- X = ( 1 + e1 )   ;   Y = ( 1 + e2 )   ;   Z = ( 1 + e3 )

- It is often convenient to work with the ratios of the principal strains

- RXY = ( 1 + e1 ) / ( 1 + e2 )     ;     RXZ = ( 1 + e1 ) / ( 1 + e3 )     ;     RYZ = ( 1 + e2 ) / ( 1 + e3 )

- The volumetric strain is

- ( V / V0 )  =  ( 1 + eV )  =  ( 1 + e1 ) ( 1 + e2 ) ( 1 + e3 )

- The three principal strain directions are orthogonal, and were also orthogonal in the undeformed state, that is, parallel to them the shear strain is zero. They are the only directions in which this is true

**Notes:**

The significance of working with ratios of the principal strains will be explored in Exercise 1 of the practical

## Slide 16

### Some important reference states of strain (2d)

- There are some simple reference states of strain that it is important to be familiar with

- Two end-member types of constant area, two-dimensional strain are

- Simple shear: in which the 2d body is subjected to a uniform shear parallel to some given direction without area change; the principal axes of strain rotate during the deformation, and hence such deformation is described as rotational and non-coaxial

- Pure shear: extension or contraction involving no area change in which the principal axes of strain do not rotate; such deformation is described as irrotational and coaxial

**Notes:**

In simple shear an initial square or rectangle is transformed into a parallelogram; in pure shear an initial square or rectangle is transformed into a rectangle of different aspect ratio. One might think that pure shear and simple shear are too simplistic to be of much interest in real deformation, but field observations suggest that situations where these idealizations may be applied are actually very common
Strictly speaking, coaxial and irrotational are not synonymous: rotational/irrotational refers to whether the finite principal strain axes change orientation with respect to some coordinate frame; a coaxial deformation is one in which the finite principal strain axes are parallel to those of the incremental strain ellipsoid (see Slide 21)
Figure from Fossen, p.32

### The Flinn diagram

- The shape of the strain ellipsoid can be visualized by plotting RXY against RYZ (usually logarithms of these ratios are plotted). Such a diagram is called a Flinn diagram

## Slide 20

### Homogeneous and inhomogeneous strain

- Strain is homogeneous if changes in size and shape for each small part of a body are proportionately identical to that for the body as a whole. Planar surfaces remain planar, straight lines remain straight and parallel lines remain parallel

- Strain is inhomogeneous if changes in size and shape vary from place to place within the body

- The remarks so far in this presentation refer to homogeneous strain. Real strains are inhomogeneous (e.g., straight lines become curved during folding). However, inhomogeneously strained bodies can be analysed by subdividing them into a collection of small, approximately homogeneously deformed, domains

**Notes:**

In the figure showing homogeneous strain, note that the small square is strained into a parallelogram in exactly the same way as all the other squares, and in exactly the same way as the whole square itself. In the figure showing inhomogeneous strain, the small black square is sufficiently small for its strain to be approximately homogeneous (straight lines remain straight, etc.) but this strain is not the same as the strain of the other elements or of the whole square

## Slide 21

### Progressive deformation  1

- Strain relates the undeformed to the deformed state but says nothing about the deformation path between these two states

- The strain path, and its consequences for the structures that form, may be considered by splitting the deformation up into a series of small strain increments. The strain in each increment is described by the incremental strain ellipse

- Evolution of the finite strain ellipse during superposition of nine identical strain increments. The finite strain ellipse changes ellipticity and orientation; the incremental strain ellipse (given in step 1) is constant

**Notes:**

The incremental strain ellipse is the same in each increment because the displacement changes between any two stages are constant
Figures from Ramsay & Huber, p.218 and 219

## Slide 22

### Progressive deformation  2

- Since the finite strain ellipse may change shape and orientation in a different way to that of the incremental strain ellipse, it becomes interesting to consider the superposition of the two

- Each strain ellipse may be divided into four quadrants in which two lines of no finite longitudinal strain separate sectors in which lines have been lengthened (L) or shortened (S)

- When the finite and incremental strain ellipses are combined, if they are in different orientations, eight sectors are now defined, representing four combinations of deformation circumstances

- Lines which have shortened and in the next increment are continuing to shorten (SS)

- Lines which have lengthened but in the next increment are shortening (LS)

- Lines which have lengthened and in the next increment are continuing to lengthen (LL)

- Lines which have shortened but in the next increment are lengthening (SL)

## Slide 25

### Practical strain measurement

- In principle, measuring strain requires us to determine all six independent terms of the strain tensor. This task is simplified if we can carry out the measurements in the directions of the three principal strains because then the shear strains are all zero. This requires us to have some understanding of how the orientations of the principal strains relate to fabrics such as cleavage and stretching lineations

- Most strain analysis is carried out in 2d on planar sections through the strain ellipsoid – the results from differently oriented planes are then combined to give a 3d picture

- 2d strain analysis relies on the deformed rocks containing some object of known original size or shape. The majority of these strain markers are one of the following:

- Objects that were initially circular (spherical in 3d)

- Objects that were originally elliptical (ellipsoidal in 3d)

- Objects of known original size (length)

- Objects that originally possessed bilateral symmetry

- We will explore key techniques in the practical and so the subject is only outlined here

**Notes:**

Recall that the state of strain in 3d is specified by nine numbers but because the strain matrix is symmetrical, three of the shear strains are equal to three other shear strains and so there are only six independent numbers – three longitudinal strains and three shear strains (see Slide 13). If we choose our coordinate frame to be aligned with the directions of the principal strains, then all the shear strains are zero in that frame and so to specify the state of strain we only need to find the magnitude of the three principal strains (see Slides 14-15) . If we have the three principal strains, then it is possible to calculate all the strain components in any other direction using the 2nd rank tensor transformation equation
It follows that, when measuring strain in the field it is super-important to measure the orientation of cleavage (strike/dip) and any stretching lineation (plunge/plunge direction) if present. These establish a reference frame within which your strain measurement results can be interpreted

## Slide 29

### Practical strain measurement  (elliptical markers 2)

- The method used to analyse elliptical strain markers is the Rf /ϕ ´ method. In ideal form this requires that the elliptical markers all have the same initial ellipticity ( Ri ) and that there is initially a significant variation in the orientation of their long axes. The final (measured) ellipticity ( Rf ) after the imposition of a known strain ( Rs ) can be theoretically predicted as a function of initial orientation ( ϕ ) and hence final orientation ( ϕ´ )

- The method requires the measured Rf to be plotted against final orientation ϕ´ and by then comparing the result with theoretical curves for different Ri and Rs. Exercise 4 of the practical takes you through the practicalities

- Strategies for coping with populations of strain markers that have a range of initial ellipticities have been developed

**Notes:**

All R are long/short axis ratios. Conglomerate clasts often have a preferred orientation (e.g., through imbrication) but there are usually enough anomalously oriented clasts to allow the technique to be applied
The Rf/ϕ technique is outlined in a short book by Lisle, 1985, Geological Strain Analysis: A Manual for the Rf/ϕ Technique, Pergamon Press. An Excel spreadsheet approach for implementing the technique is described by Chew, 2003, Computers & Geosciences 29: 795-799, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0098-3004(03)00027-X. See also Ramsay & Huber, Session 5

## Slide 30

### Practical strain measurement  (known length)

- The strain markers described so far deform with the rock as a whole (or are assumed to). However, other markers may deform by brittle fragmentation without any change in length within the fragments. By adding together the lengths of the fragments in the direction of interest, L0 may be found, and compared with the deformed length, L, measured from the rock

- Linear markers that are much more competent than the matrix, e.g., belemnites or acicular needles of some strong mineral phase (e.g., rutile in quartz)

- Linear markers that are easily disaggregated (e.g., crinoid stems)

- Fossils with some characteristic dimension (e.g., spacing of thecae in some species of undeformed graptolites)

- Two elongated belemnites: the lower one must be close to the maximum extension direction; the fragments in the upper one have been rotated within an overall dextral shear strain field

**Notes:**

Photo (Fossen, p.60) of fractured and extended belemnites in Jurassic limestones in the Swiss Alps. Note that the rotation of the upper belemnite is a rigid body rotation and so should not be used to infer angular shear
Although calculating strains by adding together the length of fragments in the direction of interest seems straightforward, there are different strategies for doing this (see Ragan, p.321-324)

### Practical strain measurement  (Mohr circle)

- Practical strain analysis using changes in line length and/or changes in angle is most conveniently carried out graphically using the Mohr construction

- The strain parameters, reciprocal quadratic elongation, λ ´ = 1 / ( 1 + e ) 2, and gamma prime, γ ´ = γ / ( 1 + e ) 2, may be written in the same form as the parametric equations of a circle. This circle gives the state of strain for any line inclined at angle ϕ ´ to the maximum extension direction λ 1´ in a plane

- Mohr construction illustrated for a line oriented at angle ϕ ´ to the maximum extension direction, λ1´. The line is represented by point P; the angular shear is given by the angle ψ, and the extension may be obtained by reading off the λ´ axis

- Note that the clockwise up convention is relatively new – Ramsay and Huber use the opposite convention

## Slide 34

### Comments on strain measurement

- The strain values obtained using the preceding techniques indicate the strain state of the markers. If there are differences between the mechanical properties of strain marker and its host matrix, care is needed when interpreting the strain results

- Some markers (e.g., reduction spots, lapilli) have very similar mechanical properties to their host rock; others (e.g., pebbles within a conglomerate) do not. In such circumstances analysis of fabrics such as cleavage provide an indication of any microscale variations in strain

- Polymict conglomerate (left) and lapilli tuff (right). The irregularity of the cleavage planes in the conglomerate points to local variations in strain; the lack of cleavage refraction in the tuff shows

- that the strain in the lapilli-rich layer is the same as in the adjacent tuff

- On a field scale, it is necessary to establish whether a strain measurement has regional significance or whether it is related only to some local structure

**Notes:**

Lapilli are originally spherical masses of tuffaceous material within a matrix of identical tuff. They are thought to form around water droplets as rain falls through an erupting cloud of ash

### Fabrics and the shape of the strain ellipsoid

## Slide 36

### Cleavage = XY plane of the strain ellipsoid

- Cleavage in deformed rocks is generally assumed to represent the XY plane (principal flattening plane)

- Shortening in the Z direction (the normal to the XY plane) may or may not be compensated by extension within the plane of cleavage – if it is not then volume loss in this direction is implied

- Within shear zones, cleavage tracks the XY plane across the shear zone (Class 4)

- However, cleavage formed as shear bands (e.g., within shear zones, as in the figure above left) does not directly relate to the strain ellipsoid; nor does crenulation cleavage

**Notes:**

Volume loss in the Z-direction may occur through pressure solution (see Optional PowerPoint presentation)
Passchier & Trouw, 2005, Microtectonics (2nd edition), p.94-95, offer some cautionary words about equating cleavage with the XY plane. Note that crenulation cleavages do not represent the XY plane for the total strain in the rock since they are formed well after deformation has begun

## Slide 37

### Strain variation across folds

- The different folding mechanisms lead to different patterns of strain variation within the profile plane

- Variations of these patterns occur depending (a) upon the extent of layer-parallel shortening before initiation of the fold, and (b) upon the nature of post-buckle flattening

- Tangential Longitudinal Strain

- These are plane strain models in which Y is parallel to the fold axis and XZ is the profile plane. In shear folding, Y is not generally parallel to the fold axis, and X and Z do not generally lie in the profile plane

- Shear folding

- Flexural flow

**Notes:**

Note that in the TLS folds, strain is at a maximum in the hinge and a minimum at the inflection point (although the figure on the slide doesn’t capture this because it doesn’t reach the inflection point), whereas in the other two folding mechanisms the reverse is the case. In TLS folds the strain is contractional on the inner arc, extensional on the outer arc, and between these two states is a surface of zero strain described as the neutral surface

### Cleavage refraction and strain
