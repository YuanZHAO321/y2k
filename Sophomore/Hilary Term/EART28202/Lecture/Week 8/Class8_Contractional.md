## Slide 2

### Contractional tectonic regimes

- In contractional tectonic regimes shortening is achieved by some combination of

- Volume loss (pressure solution/compaction)

- Pure shear (horizontal shortening with accompanying vertical thickening)

- Buckling

- Movement on contractional faults with folding related to that movement

- The essential features of the first three of these have been already been covered; this class focuses on number 4

**Notes:**

Pressure solution as a deformation process is described in the optional PowerPoint presentation on Rock Deformation Mechanisms, pure shear was described in Class 3 (Strain), and buckling in Class 2 (How Folds Form). In a crude sense, one may think of the first three shortening mechanisms simply as ways of accommodating movement on thrust faults. From this perspective, when one sees a fold the question to ask is ‘Where is the fault that led to its formation?’ If you start to think along these lines whenever you are looking at structures in the field, you will automatically find yourself a long way down the path of integrating the structures with the regional geology

## Slide 3

### Thin-skinned and thick-skinned tectonics

- Although the basic geometry of contractional fault systems was established over 100 yr ago, the subject was set on a much more systematic footing in the 1980s when it was realized that the bulk of the shortening in contractional orogenic belts is localized on to thrust faults

- In the foreland these thrust systems are typically restricted to the cover rocks (the basement is not involved), and so the style of deformation is described as thin-skinned

- In the higher metamorphic grade hinterland, the thrust systems are thicker and the basement is involved, and so this style of deformation is described as thick-skinned

- A section across the Alps

### Local contractional regimes

- Contractional fault systems with similar geometrical features occur at all scales, and not just at the scale of the orogenic belt

- E.g., thrust fault systems may be found at the toe of a landslip or at the toe of a delta slope or within other gravity-driven soft sediment deformation scenarios

### Wrinkle ridges (local contraction) on the Moon

- “Local” contractional features (wrinkle ridges) occur on the Moon within the interior of mare and are accompanied by extensional fractures (rilles) around the mare margins

- These fractures were formed during basin-localized flexure of the lunar crust under the weight of the mare basalts

- Mare Serenitatis

**Notes:**

### Lobate scarps produced by global contraction

- Global distributions of lobate scarps are common on many planetary bodies and reflect global contraction due to net cooling of the planetary interior

- Analysis of the geometry of these features indicates that they are the surface expression of antiforms formed above the tips of reverse faults that break the surface

- Left: Discovery Rupes, which at 500 km+, is the longest lobate scarp on Mercury

- Right: detail of a lobate scarp in the northern hemisphere of Mercury

## Slide 12

### Thrusts

- A thrust is a predominantly low angle fault or shear zone with a predominantly reverse dip-slip sense of movement

- In rocks that young upwards, thrusts bring older rocks over younger rocks, or if the metamorphic grade decreases upwards, thrusts bring rocks of higher metamorphic grade over rocks of lower metamorphic grade

- Thrusts exist at all scales, from those with extents and displacements of millimetres, through to those on the scale of mountain ranges with displacements of 10s or 100s of kilometres

- Glarus Thrust in Switzerland

## Slide 13

### Nappe,  klippe/window,  allochthon/autochthon

- The hanging wall of a low angle, large displacement thrust is wide compared with its thickness and so is described as a thrust nappe or thrust sheet

- Erosion through a thrust sheet may mean that it is discontinuously exposed: an erosional remnant of a nappe is a klippe; an erosional hole exposing the units beneath the thrust is a (tectonic) window

- Thrust sheet is transported (allochthon);

- Footwall is close to its original location (autochthon);

- Slices of basement/cover caught up in the thrusting and moved relatively short distances (parautochthon)

**Notes:**

‘Nappe’ is French for ‘tablecloth’

### Shape of the thrust surface: ramps/flats

## Slide 17

### Frontal/oblique/lateral ramps

- Ramps that form perpendicular to the main thrust transport direction are frontal ramps. These ramps are pure dip-slip reverse faults

- Oblique ramps are oblique to the transport direction and are oblique-slip reverse faults

- Lateral ramps are parallel to the movement direction and are strike-slip faults. Effectively, they transfer slip from one frontal ramp on to another and so are described as transfer faults, or, if sub-vertical and cutting through the hanging wall, as tear faults

**Notes:**

We will return to the ‘transfer fault’ and ‘tear fault’ terminology in the final class (Strike-slip tectonic regimes)

## Slide 18

### Thrust systems: fault imbrication and duplexes

- Thrusts often come as sets of listric faults, each fault with the same sense of slip, so that the individual thrust sheets overlap like a set of roofing tiles. This situation is described as an imbricate fan. Leading imbricate fans have maximum slip on the frontal fault; trailing imbricate fans have maximum slip on the most internal fault

- Imbricate faults merge down on to a sole or floor thrust. They may also merge up on to a roof thrust. An imbricate system that is bounded by a floor and a roof thrust is known as a duplex, and the internal blocks (which are bounded completely by faults) as horses

- The major floor thrust at the base of a stack of thrust nappes and/or imbricate system is described as a décollement or basal detachment

**Notes:**

Recall (Class 7 Slide 4) that a listric fault is a (curved) fault that gradually decreases in dip downwards

### Formation of hinterland-dipping thrust duplexes

- Thrust duplexes form sequentially, usually propagating towards the foreland: a horse is formed in the footwall, is displaced forwards, and becomes inactive; a new horse is then formed in the footwall . . .

## Slide 29

### Branch-lines

- No cross section though a thrust system should be constructed without considering how the individual faults link up to form three-dimensional networks of faults

- Tip-lines: the line marking where fault displacement dies out

- Branch-lines: the line marking where a fault splits in two (trailing branch-line) or where two faults merge (leading branch-line)

- Cut-off lines: the line marking the intersection of some contact with the fault surface

- There are topological constraints on the interrelationship of these lines, and these may be particularly useful in regions with large topography

- Simple imbricate splay

- Around a horse

**Notes:**

Note that around a horse a branch-line forms a closed loop with leading and trailing segments; around an imbricate splay, the branch-line connects the ends of the tip-line so that the tip-line can form a closed loop
It is beyond the scope of this class to consider how we may apply the topological constraints noted on this slide – you just need to be aware that such constraints exist. For an account, see Diegel, 1986, Journal of Structural Geology 8: 269-279, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(86)90048-9
Whenever you map a fault, think about how it connects with other faults to produce a consistent slip displacement story – then you will be well on the way to incorporating these matters intuitively

## Slide 31

### Fault-bend folds

- From the moment that a ramp is formed and the hanging wall starts climbing over it, the hanging wall is deformed into a fault-bend fold

- The shape of fault-bend folds is determined by the geometry of the ramp because they are geometrically-necessary folds

- This means that fold geometry can be predicted from ramp geometry and vice versa

- Development of a fault-bend fold. Notice how the hanging wall units are rotated one way and then the other as they climb up and over the ramp

**Notes:**

## Slide 32

### Strains during fault-bend folding

- The rotation of the hanging wall, first one way and then the other, has implications for the development of strain in the hanging wall rocks

- Lower flat: shear coupled with drag on the fault plane leads to cleavage (S1)

- Ramp: cleavage steepens and intensifies

- Upper flat: reversal of shear sense in unbending leads to shortening of the cleavage and, in principle, to the development of a crenulation (S2)

- Whether a cleavage (and a crenulation) is observed, depends upon the ramp angle and the mechanical properties/behaviour of the hanging wall rocks

**Notes:**

I am not aware of actual examples of crenulations that have been interpreted as having being formed in this way, but this does not mean that there aren’t any. A candidate place to look for them would be within relatively incompetent units (mudstones) in interbedded successions
Figure from Sanderson, 1982, Tectonophysics 88: 201-233, https://doi.org/10.1016/0040-1951(82)90237-2. The mechanism of folding is assumed to be flexural flow (with no deformation in the toe of the thrust sheet); other mechanisms of folding (see Class 2) modify the strain variations shown

## Slide 33

### Fault-propagation folds

- Ahead of a thrust tip the deformation is often accommodated in more ductile fashion through the formation of an asymmetric antiform-synform pair that verges towards the foreland. These are described as fault-propagation folds

- Fault-propagation folds differ from fault-bend folds in that they move with the propagating fault tip rather than being stationary at the location of the ramp

- With some assumptions about layer thickness changes, the fold geometry may be modelled as a function of displacement gradient and fault dip

- Progressive development of a fault-propagation fold. Displacement on the fault goes to zero at the tip and continued shortening is accommodated by growth of a fold pair ahead of the tip

**Notes:**

We noted fault propagation folds and how their development may be modelled in the last class (Class 7 Slides 11-12). Modelling usually proceeds either by assuming predominantly layer parallel slip and the subsequent growth of kink-like folds (as pictured on this slide), or through distributed shearing in a triangular zone ahead of the fault tip (trishear model). ## Slide 43

### Wedge models

- Fold and thrust belts show an overall wedge-shaped geometry in cross-section, thinning towards the foreland

- Mechanically, such a wedge is analogous to a wedge of sand in front of a moving bulldozer – the sand deforms until it reaches a constant critical taper

- The forces applied are (a) push from behind and (b) gravity

- The resulting angle of taper (a + b ) depends upon the relative magnitudes of the frictional resistance at the base and the strength of the wedge material

- Increasing friction increases the angle of taper (more push is required from behind and so the material piles up higher) – low friction means smaller angles of taper

- Increasing wedge material strength decreases the angle of taper (a stronger wedge can be thinner and still slide)

**Notes:**

The emplacement of large thrust sheets was the subject of a landmark mechanical analysis by Hubbert and Rubey who modelled a thrust sheet as thin rectangular slab (Hubbert & Rubey, 1959, Geological Society of America Bulletin 70: 115-206, https://doi.org/10.1130/0016-7606(1959)70[115:ROFPIM]2.0.CO;2). This analysis emphasized the point that rocks are far too weak for a thin sheet of rock to be pushed over other rocks for 100s km without the sheet deforming internally (it is like trying to push a wet napkin across a tabletop – the napkin will not move as a single rigid unit). Yet thrust sheets that have moved this far are observed. Various ways of resolving this paradox, while retaining the rectangular slab geometry, have been proposed but none are entirely satisfactory (for a discussion see Twiss & Moores, p.258-267). Much greater success has been obtained with the critical wedge model. Details of how thrust sheets may be modelled as wedges are given by Dahlen, 1990, Annual Reviews of Earth and Planetary Sciences 18: 55-99, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ea.18.050190.000415
Figures from Fossen, p.369

## Slide 44

### Wedge models  2

- Assuming friction and strength remain approximately constant, the shape of the wedge is scale invariant – consequently, any process that acts to change the angle of taper during movement must be compensated

- As the wedge thickens, the surface slope (a ) increases but so does the basal slope (b ) because the weight of the wedge depresses the lithosphere. The wedge adjusts either by lengthening (imbrication and duplex formation at its toe), or by thinning (gravitational collapse of its surface and/or internal extensional deformation)

- As the wedge thins (e.g., by erosion), the surface slope is maintained by uplift through internal shortening (reverse faults, folding)

- Such adjustments are accompanied by syntectonic sedimentation (flysch) which redistributes matter on the surface of the wedge so as to maintain a stable surface slope

**Notes:**

Figure from Fossen, p.369
For an influential model describing these compensating deformation features of thrust wedges as they develop, see Platt, 1986, Geological Society of America Bulletin 97: 1037-1053, https://doi.org/10.1130/0016-7606(1986)97<1037:DOOWAT>2.0.CO;2

## Slide 45

### The role of gravity

- Gravity plays a prominent part in the development of contractional orogenic belts – the dominant driving force?

- As an orogenic belt develops, crustal thickening is greatest in the centre of the orogen – if weak enough (perhaps because of heating), this thickened and elevated crust collapses under its own weight, and thrust nappes are pushed out on to the foreland

- Different cases may be considered depending upon whether flow is in the direction of surface slope (spreading), or basal slope (gliding) or is an extrusion between constraints

- In such gravity-driven deformation one might expect a significant component of pure shear coaxial deformation throughout much of the wedge, the orientation of the strain ellipsoid reflecting the extent of any lateral constraints

**Notes:**

Note that the gliding model is perhaps only important at local scales since at the regional scale, the basal décollement in active orogenic belts generally dips towards the hinterland (and hence the flow would have to be up-slope). Gliding has similarities with land-slips. At scales of up to a few 10s km, gravitational collapse systems of this kind are called surge zones (see Coward, 1982, Journal of Structural Geology 4: 247-256, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(82)90012-8)
In contrast, the spreading model allows up-slope flow because it is the surface slope that counts. This has similarities with the flow of glaciers
For a more detailed account of gravity models and their implications for strain history, see Merle, 1989, Tectonophysics 165: 57-71, https://doi.org/10.1016/0040-1951(89)90035-8

## Slide 46

### Strain through thrust sheets

- The relative significance of “push from behind” and gravitational effects is reflected in the distribution of strains through a thrust sheet

- Gravity-gliding: thrust sheet does not shorten or lengthen in the slip direction (simple shear)

- Push-from-behind: thrust sheet shortens in the slip direction (X is oriented at a steeper angle to the shear plane than in simple shear)

- Gravity-spreading: thrust sheet extends (and thins) in the slip direction (X has a sigmoidal variation reflecting the tendency for the surface slope to flatten out to the horizontal)

- Orientation of the long axis of the strain ellipsoid (X) through thrust sheets

- Such models of strain variation are simplified but may be used as a framework within which to interpret real strain measurements through thrust sheets, and thereby to say something about thrust sheet emplacement mechanisms

**Notes:**

X on this slide refers to the orientation of the greatest principal strain (the long axis of the strain ellipsoid, see Class 3), e.g., the direction of a stretching lineation
Twiss & Moores (p.675-676) provide a concise discussion of these different models of strain through thrust sheets. Interesting examples of attempts to split strains in thrust sheets into pure shear (shortening or extension) and simple shear components include Coward & Kim, 1981, Geological Society, London, Special Publications 9: 275-292, https://doi.org/10.1144/GSL.SP.1981.009.01.25, and Fischer & Coward, 1982, Tectonophysics 88: 291-312, https://doi.org/10.1016/0040-1951(82)90241-4

## Slide 51

### Balanced sections

- A balanced cross-section is one that is

- Admissible: the structures that it contains are geologically reasonable with respect to each other and for the tectonic context

- Restorable (retro-deformable): the section can be undeformed (working back in time) such that no significant gaps or overlaps are left

- A balanced section may be used to estimate the amount of shortening (or extension)

- Zagros fold and thrust belt

- All cross-sections should be balanced to check the self-consistency of the interpretation but note that a balanced section is not necessarily a unique solution to the data

**Notes:**

The basic approach to balancing a section is to start from a point on the foreland that has not been thrusted and to restore the layering to its original length by sequentially removing the displacement on each fault
Figure from Fossen, p.444

## Slide 52

### Balanced sections: assumptions

- In constructing a balanced section, the following initial assumptions are usually made:

- No volume change

- Plane strain (all deformation is in the plane of the section)

- (If the line-length balancing technique is used) all folds are parallel folds formed by flexural slip so that layer thickness is preserved

- To some extent these assumptions may be relaxed by more sophisticated 2D- and 3D-balancing techniques (including unstraining the units), although to be properly constrained such techniques may require a formidable amount of extra information

- It follows that a balanced section through a thrust system should be constructed parallel to the transport direction

**Notes:**

Note that if flexural slip is true then there is no strain in the bedding plane – see Class 2 Slides 26-27. This may be tested in the field by examining fossils on bedding surfaces to check that they appear undeformed
A commentary on the assumptions used in balanced section construction is given by Moretti & Callot, 2012, Journal of Structural Geology 41: 64-75, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2012.02.014

### Area balancing

- When layers do not remain constant in thickness the area (A) of a given unit between faults may be measured instead of its length. Since the original thickness ( t0 ) of the unit is known from the template, then assuming plane strain, the original length ( L0 ) may be determined from

- L0  =  A / t0

- and added to the restoration

- Area balancing may be used independently of line-length balancing and so can be used to check line-length restorations

- Sections that do not balance need modifying – this may involve changing the ramp angle or adding / removing horses

**Notes:**

The formula on this slide follows because in plane strain the area (A = L0t0) remains constant during shortening
