## Slide 2

### Localization of deformation

- During natural deformation strain tends to be localized into zones

- The most obvious example of this is a fault where almost all the deformation is accommodated within a narrow zone surrounding the fracture surface

- Regions of highly localized deformation are described as shear zones

- Shear zones can form at all depths within the lithosphere, and at all scales – from the sub-microscopic up to the scale of a plate boundary where the shear zone penetrates the whole lithosphere

- Displacements across shear zones correspondingly range from the miniscule to 100s of kilometres

**Notes:**

For a summary of the nomenclature (definitions) of various types of structural discontinuities (joints, faults, fractures, shear zones, deformation bands) see Schultz & Fossen, 2008, American Association of Petroleum Geologists Bulletin 92: 853-867, https://doi.org/10.1306/02200807065
Photo (Fossen, one of the extra figures on the web-site for the book, folk.uib.no/nglhe/StructuralGeoBook.html): a fault at Moreno Bay, Antofagata, Chile. Note that in the centre of the image the localized zone of strong deformation is of finite width (width ~ ¼ the diameter of the coin that is used as a scale) and so this fault is not simply a discrete fracture surface

## Slide 3

### Brittle, semi-brittle, and ductile shear zones

- The mechanical properties of rocks change with temperature and pressure, and so the character of localized deformation zones changes with depth

- In the brittle upper crust, shear zones include faults and deformation bands

- At greater depths, where rocks flow rather than fracture, these zones of deformation are described as ductile shear zones

- In the transition between brittle and ductile behaviour, shear zones have a mixed, semi-brittle character, and are associated with en échelon arrays of extension cracks

**Notes:**

The change in the way that rocks deform with the deformation conditions is described in the Optional PowerPoint presentation
Strictly, a ductile shear zone is one in which some marker layer can be traced continuously across the shear zone, although if the displacements are large, this might only be true in principle rather than in actuality

## Slide 5

### Description of shear zones

- A shear zone is bounded by two margins (shear zone walls) that separate relatively undeformed rock lying outside the zone from more strongly deformed rock inside it. As surfaces, the orientation of these shear zone walls should be specified as strike/dip

- Once the margins of the shear zone have been defined, its thickness may be measured, and sometimes the displacement across it. Shear zone thickness tends to increase with displacement

- Even if the magnitude of the displacement is unknown, the geometry of the rock fabrics within the shear zone usually allow the sense of displacement (normal/reverse; sinistral/dextral) to be determined

- Relationship between displacement and thickness for ductile shear zones (red) and fault zones (yellow)

**Notes:**

As we shall repeatedly note when we consider faults in the forthcoming classes, the key things that need to be stated when describing shear zones and faults are
The orientation (strike/dip) of the shear zone walls
The orientation (plunge/plunge direction) of the slip vector
The magnitude and sense of displacement of the strike-slip and dip-slip components of the slip vector
On this slide, ‘displacement’ means ‘slip-vector’. For shear zones it is also useful to describe the thickness (width) of the shear zone
Figure from Fossen, p.331 (modified by Steve Covey-Crump). Note that for relatively small displacements (up to ~100 m), the general trend is that displacement (D) is approximately equal to deformation zone width (T), but note also that there is a large scatter (this is a log-log plot) and so the relationship is not particularly helpful as a means of assessing displacement

## Slide 6

### Kinematic classification of shear zones

- Shear zones are classified using the relative movement between the two shear zone walls

- This relative movement is some combination of

- pure shear

- simple shear

- volume change

- In low porosity rocks shear zones are often idealized as simple shear zones but real shear zones may simultaneously involve significant components of extension (transtension) or contraction (transpression) normal to their margins

- In high porosity rocks (e.g., some sandstones) the term bands is used for localized deformation zones – in these deformation bands the full range of compaction, dilation, and simple shear deformation may occur

## Slide 7

### Ideal shear zones formed by progressive simple shear

- The simplest model of shear zone deformation is progressive simple shear (most of the comments in this class are based on this model)

- Across a progressive simple shear zone, the strain is heterogeneously distributed, varying from zero at the shear zone boundaries to a maximum in the middle of the zone

- In the first small increment of strain, the orientation of the maximum extension direction is at 45° to the shear zone wall; with increasing strain it rotates towards parallelism with the shear zone walls without ever becoming exactly parallel

- γ  = shear strain; R = aspect ratio of the strain ellipse; θ´ = angle between the maximum extension direction and the shear zone wall

**Notes:**

Figure left from Twiss & Moores, 2007, Structural Geology (2nd edition), p.444
Figure right by Steve Covey-Crump showing the change in orientation of the maximum extension direction (θ´) and strain ellipse aspect ratio (R) with increasing shear strain. The calculations apply for simple shear only – any component of pure shear or volume change modifies the equations. The equation for θ´ is derived from Eqn. 2.4 of Ramsay & Huber, vol.1, and that for R is derived from Eqn. 2.7 of Ramsay & Huber, vol.1. Note that the values of θ´ are negative (clockwise angle) in a dextral shear zone; they are positive in a sinistral shear zone. γ is engineering shear strain (γ = tan ψ; see Class 3 Slide 4)

## Slide 8

### Estimating displacements across shear zones

- If the orientation of the long axis of the strain ellipse (θ´) is known at several places across a shear zone, then assuming simple shear, the shear strain at these places may be calculated. The displacement across the shear zone is then the area under a curve of shear strain vs. distance across the shear zone normal to the shear zone walls

- The assumption of simple shear may be checked by showing that the aspect ratios (R) of the strain ellipses given by strain markers are as predicted by simple shear

- displacement

- γ  =

- width

**Notes:**

From the orientation of the long axis of the strain ellipse θ´ then the tan 2θ´ formula on the bottom right figure may be used to determine the shear strain γ. The equation written in yellow on the slide simply expresses the usual trigonometrical relationship, tan θ = opposite over adjacent (see the diagram bottom left, where θ is ψ). Hence, we see that “displacement = γ × shear zone width”. In the figure bottom left γ is constant across the shear zone, but if instead it varies across the shear zone, then the “displacement = γ × shear zone width” equation becomes the integration that yields the area under the “shear strain vs. distance across the shear zone curve”
The cross-check that simple shear applies makes use of the formula for R (the aspect ratio of the strain ellipse) on the bottom right figure – the calculated value of R from the γ values determined from the measured θ´ may be compared with the measured values of R
For further details on this technique, see Ramsay and Huber, vol.1, Questions 3.4 and 3.5 (p.37ff.). Note that looking at the diagrams on the slide it is tempting to think that θ´ = 90 - ψ but this is NOT correct – θ´ records the direction of maximum extension while ψ records the change in angle between two lines that were originally orthogonal
You may be able to employ this technique in fieldwork, but you do need a section through the shear zone that contains the slip vector (the XZ plane). The orientation of a foliation produced by the shearing can be used to give the orientation of the maximum extension direction = long axis of the strain ellipse = X direction (see Slide 12)

## Slide 10

### Shear zone rocks (fabrics)

- As strain accumulates in ductile shear zones formed at greenschist metamorphic grade and above, a foliation appears, and commonly within it, also a stretching lineation

- Objects within the protolith are stretched and rotated into the shear zone, and the fabric is intensified in the centre of the shear zone

- The curvature of the foliation provides a very useful indication of shear sense, but to be used in this way the foliation must be one formed during the shearing rather than a deflected pre-existing foliation

**Notes:**

When using the curvature of foliations to infer slip sense, be aware that how this curvature appears depends upon the orientation of the section through the rock (relative to the shear plane) on which the fabric is being viewed – care is required

## Slide 11

### Shear zone rocks (mylonites)

- In the middle of a shear zone, the strain may be so large that pre-existing textures are completely transposed. The resulting banded rock is described as a mylonite

- Formally, mylonites are fault rocks containing a well-developed foliation that arises during tectonic grain size reduction

- Mylonite zones can be up to several kilometres thick and may contain a rich variety of deformation features that are indicative of local variations in finite strain

**Notes:**

Mylonites are formally defined as “A fault rock which is cohesive and characterised by a well-developed schistosity resulting from tectonic reduction of grain size and commonly containing rounded porphyroclasts and lithic fragments of similar composition to minerals in the matrix”. They are subdivided according to the proportion of original grains that remain unrecrystallized: (1) protomylonites – less than 50% new recrystallized grains; (2) (meso)mylonites – 50-90% new recrystallized grains; (3) ultramylonites – more than 90% new recrystallized grains. For formal definitions, see Brodie et al., 2007, in: Metamorphic Rocks: a Classification and Glossary of Terms (editors D. Fettes & J. Desmonds), p.24-31. There are additional categories: an augen mylonite contains distinctive large crystals around which the foliated matrix is wrapped, and a blastomylonite is a mylonite that has undergone significant grain growth during or after deformation
Photo top left (Fossen, one of the extra figures on the web-site for the book, folk.uib.no/nglhe/StructuralGeoBook.html): Proterozoic migmatite, mylonitized during Caledonian deformation
Photo top right (by Roberto Weinberg, users.monash.edu.au/~weinberg/Pages/Zanskar_SZ/Zanskar_SZ.htm): the thick mylonites of the Zanskar Shear Zone, to the west of Pensi La, Reru Valley, High Himalaya

## Slide 12

### Relationship of shear zone fabrics to finite strain

- The foliation tracks the principal flattening plane (XY plane) across the shear zone

- XZ plane

- Hence in shear zones formed by progressive simple shear:

- the foliation is at ~45° to the shear zone walls at the margins and rotates towards parallelism with those walls in the centre of the shear zone

- the stretching lineation is parallel to the maximum extension direction

- (X direction)

**Notes:**

Recall the terminology from Class 3: the principal flattening plane is the plane at right angles to the Z axis of the strain ellipsoid (Z = the shortest axis). Hence if Z is at right angles to this plane, that plane contains the longest (X) and intermediate (Y) axes of the strain ellipsoid, that is, it is the XY plane
At the margins of a shear zone the foliation is commonly found to be a little less than 45° because a small amount of strain is required before the foliation is sufficiently well developed to be visible

## Slide 13

### Shear zone fabrics (stereograms)

- This geometry implies that on a stereogram:

- across the shear zone, the stretching lineation (X) and poles to the foliation (Z) should be spread along the same great circle (XZ plane)

- the stretching lineation should reorient towards the slip vector as one moves towards the centre of the shear zone (stretching lineations are usually only seen near the centre of the shear zone)

- the pole to the foliation should reorient towards the pole of the shear plane as one moves towards the centre of the shear zone

- the pole of the XZ plane (= Y) should lie in the shear plane

**Notes:**

Hence some or all this information may be used to establish the orientation of the shear zone and the slip vector within it. These matters are so useful that we’ll devote time within the practicals to exploring them
There are other helpful features derivative from these relationships, e.g., the intersection of the foliation plane (measured anywhere in the shear zone) with the shear plane is parallel to Y and therefore at 90° to the slip vector as measured in the shear plane (see figure bottom right on previous slide), and so if one has some approximate idea of the strike/dip of the shear plane, an approximate idea of the slip vector orientation can be obtained from a foliation measurement

Figure bottom by Steve Covey-Crump

## Slide 14

### Deflection and extension of markers

- Deflected markers across shear zones offer insight into the shear sense, provided that the usual precautions about inferring slip sense from apparent offsets are taken

- The obliquity of the incremental strain ellipse to the shear zone margins means that layers crossing the shear zone close to the maximum extension direction should be stretched/boudinaged

**Notes:**

The caution about using apparent offsets is because the apparent offset of a layer across a fault depends upon (a) the orientation of that layer relative to the fault plane, and (b) the orientation of the surface of observation relative to the fault plane

## Slide 15

### Analysis of lines/planes deformed into a shear zone

- Pre-existing lineations and planar features that are rotated into a shear zone may be used to determine the orientation of the shear plane and slip vector

- On a stereogram, as one moves into a shear zone:

- the orientations of a pre-existing lineation are spread along a great circle towards the slip vector; the intersection of this great circle with the great circle describing the shear plane is the orientation of the slip vector

- the poles of a pre-existing planar feature (e.g., a dyke margin) are spread along a great circle towards the pole to the shear plane; the pole of that great circle (fold axis) lies in the shear plane

**Notes:**

These points will be explored further in practicals to come later in the course. For a diagram illustrating why point (1) is true, see the shear folding figures (Class 2 Slide 30)

## Slide 16

### Folded markers

- Markers oriented close to the maximum shortening direction of the incremental strain ellipse fold as they cross the shear zone. However, with increasing shear, these folded markers may rotate into an extensional field of the incremental strain ellipse and then start to stretch

- Markers passively folded in this way have Class 2 (similar) geometry with thickened hinges and attenuated limbs

- The folds may be asymmetric, but care is required when inferring shear sense from their vergence

## Slide 17

### Intrafolial folds

- In mylonitic shear zones often the foliation is itself folded. These folds are commonly isoclinal with axial planes in orientations that are indistinguishable from the main mylonitic foliation. Such folds are described as intrafolial folds

- Folding of this kind results from local distortions in the flow field during shearing

- When viewed in the XZ plane the asymmetry of the folds indicates the sense of shearing

- Note the importance of looking in the XZ plane

**Notes:**

Recall (Class 1 Slide 17) that isoclinal folds are folds with a very small interlimb angle
When the strains are large and any layering crossing the shear zone has been rotated into parallelism with it, the term “intrafolial fold” is often loosely applied to the folded layering as well as to the folded foliation

## Slide 20

### S-C fabrics

- As strain increases, a set of shear bands (C - surfaces) forms parallel to the shear zone walls. These deflect the main foliation (S - surface) in the same sense as the shear sense

- With further increase in strain, the angle between the C and S surfaces becomes very small, and a new set of shear bands (C´- surfaces) forms at 15-35° to the shear zone walls

- Across the C´- surfaces the sense of deflection of the main foliation is extensional, and in the same general direction as the shear sense (= extensional crenulation cleavage)

- S

- C´

**Notes:**

The C´-surfaces are in the same orientation as Riedel shears in clay-bearing fault gouges (see Slide 45). Their extensional character means that they are commonly described as extensional crenulation cleavages
The distinction between C- and C´-surfaces is often not made in the literature

Photomicrograph bottom (from Passchier & Trouw, Fig.5.15): mica-schist, Menderes Massif, Turkey (width of view = 3.5 mm), sectioned parallel to the XZ plane

### S-C fabrics (further examples)

- Shear zone rocks with these fabrics are described as S - C mylonites – these fabrics, particularly the C´ surfaces, make superb shear sense indicators

- C surfaces are indicative of inhomogeneous simple shear; they can develop early in the deformation and are often straight and continuous

- C´ surfaces tend to develop late in the deformation and are commonly short, wavy and anastomosing

## Slide 22

### Shear sense from objects within mylonites

- A number of objects found within the matrix of mylonites, together with the deflections of the foliation around these objects, provide excellent shear sense indicators

- Six types of object are particularly helpful. It is good practice when making observations to note which of these six types is being considered because although their geometry for shear sense evaluation is similar, they each form by a different mechanism

- The use of sigmoidal inclusion trails in syntectonic porphyroblasts as shear sense indicators is problematic. Establishing whether they have formed by porphyroblast rotation, matrix deformation around the porphyroblast, or some other mechanism is non-trivial (see Passchier & Trouw, Fig. 7.34 in § 7.6.8)

**Notes:**

NOTE: porphyroblasts grow in response to changing metamorphic conditions; porphyroclasts are the remnants of grains within an originally coarse-grained rock
Types of object:
Naked clasts/mineral fish have no rims
Mineral fish are lozenge-shaped
In a mantled porphyroclast the rim is made of the same mineral as the clast
In the other two types of rimmed porphyroclast the rim contains different minerals to the clast
Sigmoids are polycrystalline aggregates of a mineral with the shape indicated in the figure top right
For those of you who are not taking Metamorphic Minerals and Processes, there are some helpful comments on using inclusion trails within porphyroblasts to infer the timing of deformation and porphyroblast growth on https://www.alexstrekeisen.it/english/meta/porphyroblastic.php. When those inclusion trails are sigmoidal, porphyroblast growth could be interpreted as occurring at the same time as the deformation (that is, that it was syntectonic), and it may be possible to infer the sense of shear. For example, in the photomicrograph at bottom right of this slide, the sigmoidal inclusion trails suggest that the garnet was rotating anticlockwise as it was growing (sinistral sense of shear implied)

### Mantled porphyroclasts

- Mantled porphyroclasts consist of a central single crystal with a fine-grained mantle of the same mineral. The mantle may be drawn out into wings that stretch and change shape while the core remains rigid

- Viewed in the YZ plane the wings are symmetric (ϕ - type) and so cannot be used to infer the sense of movement

- Symmetric wings: note that in this case these are not actually mantled clasts but strain fringes (see below) because the rims (quartz) are of a different mineral than the porphyroblasts (garnet). However, the same logic about the sense of movement applies

## Slide 26

### Sigmoids and quarter structures

- Sigmoids have similar geometry to σ - type mantled porphyroclasts but they lack the central rigid clast, perhaps because it has been entirely recrystallized

- The way in which the foliation wraps around the porphyroclast may be used to infer shear sense (quarter structures)

**Notes:**

Quarter structures: the oblique orientation of the incremental strain ellipse to the margins of the shear zone leads to an asymmetry in the way that the foliation rotates around objects within shear zones. In compressional sectors the foliation may thin and intensify, while in extensional sectors it may thicken and fold

## Slide 27

### Strain fringes

- The oblique orientation of the incremental strain ellipse to the margins of shear zones may also lead to asymmetry in the development of strain fringes around objects in shear zones because the fringes develop preferentially in the direction of maximum extension

- If the strain fringe is fibrous, then the fibres may provide information about the reorientation of the rock relative to the incremental strain ellipse during progressive deformation

**Notes:**

## Slide 30

### Shape fabric and lattice preferred orientations (LPO)

- The foliations in a mylonite may be defined (1) by a preferred orientation of micas, (2) by a mineralogical layering, and/or (3) by a grain shape preferred orientation

- The principal foliation (S-surfaces) tracks the orientation of the finite strain ellipse, and so it follows that the grain shape preferred orientation does this too (hence the potential of Fry-type strain analyses)

- At the deformation conditions in which ductile shear zones form, the grain shape fabric is usually accompanied by a lattice preferred orientation (LPO) of the individual grains (e.g., an alignment of the crystallographic c-axes)

- Shear sense may be inferred from the asymmetry of this LPO

## Slide 33

### Combined ductile and brittle deformation

- In upper crustal environments, particularly when fluids are present, shear zones commonly show many of the features of ductile deformation that we have been describing (e.g., grain shape fabrics, LPOs, strain fringes) but they also show features indicating brittle deformation (e.g., fractures and veins)

- In semi-brittle shear zones the fractures commonly form en échelon arrays

- In simple shear, these fractures (tension gashes) form at 45° to the shear zone wall, parallel with the maximum shortening direction of the incremental strain ellipse, and may therefore be used to infer shear sense

## Slide 37

### The vein infill

- Fracturing in the presence of a solute-rich fluid frequently leads to precipitation and the formation of a vein

- The crystals in such veins commonly have a fibrous morphology in which the fibres grow parallel to maximum extension direction of the incremental strain ellipsoid, and become curved as the deforming rock progressively rotates with respect to that strain ellipsoid

- The sense of curvature depends upon the direction of crystal growth and hence upon the way in which the fracture opens

- syntaxial

- antitaxial

- stretching / ataxial

**Notes:**

For veins in which the fibres track the orientation of the incremental strain ellipsoid as the fracture opens, whether the fibres grew inwards as the fracture opened (syntaxial) or outwards as the fracture opened (antitaxial) may be assessed by observing which part of the fibre is at ~45° to the fracture (vein) walls – the part that is at ~45° is the last to form
Tectonic veins can provide information about palaeostress fields, the kinematics of deformation (pure shear, simple shear or some combination), the metamorphic conditions (temperature, pressure, fluid pressure), and the composition/source of the fluids. Consequently, they are tremendously useful and should not be overlooked when mapping. For a helpful review of many of these aspects of veins, see Bons et al., 2012, Journal of Structural Geology 43: 33-62, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2012.07.005. The subject is also thoroughly discussed by Passchier & Trouw, Chapter 6
Photo top right (from Passchier & Trouw, Fig.6.2): fibrous calcite vein in a slate, Appalachians, New York. This is an antitaxial vein – note the sense of fibre curvature and the thin median line through the middle of the vein
The diagrams (from Ramsay & Huber, vol.1, p.241) show the three types of fibrous vein that are commonly distinguished. To these a fourth may be added which is some combination of these when there has been more than one fracturing event

## Slide 38

### The vein infill  2

- In syntaxial veins the fibres grow inwards, often into an open crack

- In antitaxial veins a thin fracture forms and the fibres, seeded from this fracture surface (median line), grow outwards as the crack opens

- In stretching veins the fibres are built up by repeated small opening and cementation episodes – the location of the crack-seal events is commonly haphazard and so the fibres do not track the strain increments, but they do indicate the overall opening direction

- Syntaxial – fibres grow from wall inwards

- Antitaxial – fibres grow from centre towards wall

**Notes:**

Figure bottom left (from Bons et al., 2012, Journal of Structural Geology 43: 33-62, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2012.07.005) highlights that there is a continuum between ataxial (stretching) veins and syntaxial veins, and so the distinction of vein type is really only one that distinguishes this syntaxial/ataxial continuum from antitaxial veins
If syntaxial veins grow into an open crack, then the fibres cannot not track the opening trajectory of the crack (the crack opens before the fibres start growing). In the situation described on the previous slide where the fibres in a syntaxial tensional gash track the opening direction of the growing and rotating tension gash, the gash must open gradually (in small increments) with the fibres growing as it does so. Really this just describes an incrementally growing ataxial vein rather than a syntaxial vein sensu stricto. Antitaxial veins do track the opening trajectory of fractures
For a more subtle account of fibrous vein formation, focusing on bedding-parallel antitaxial veins in mudstones, see Zhao & Li, 2022, Journal of Structural Geology 161, article 104653, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2022.104653
Photos top right and bottom right (from Bons et al., 2012, Journal of Structural Geology 43: 33-62, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2012.07.005): syntaxial vein, Biure, Spain; antitaxial vein, Oppaminda Creek, Arkaroola, South Australia

### Brittle fault rocks (breccias)

- Fault breccias contain more than 30% clasts that are over 2 mm in size

- They may be subdivided into crackle, mosaic and chaotic breccias depending upon how well the clasts fit together. This subdivision effectively equates to the % clasts over 2 mm (crackle 100-75%; mosaic 75-60%; chaotic 60-30%)

- All three types of fault breccia in close proximity

**Notes:**

Fault breccias typically lack a strong foliation but, nevertheless, the clasts are commonly neither randomly aligned or nor randomly distributed
Fault rocks (breccias, gouges and cataclasites) have historically (since the 1970s) been subdivided as cohesive or incohesive depending upon their perceived state of cohesion at the time of fault movement. The current mood (Woodcock & Mort, 2008, Geological Magazine 145: 435-440, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0016756808004883) is to subdivide primarily on the basis of fragment size. For details of the practical application of this classification, see Mort & Woodcock, 2008, Journal of Structural Geology 30: 701-709, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2008.02.005

## Slide 42

### Brittle fault rocks (cataclasites)

- Cataclasites are all non-foliated fault rocks with less than 30% clasts over 2 mm that are not in the special categories of pseudotachylyte or fault gouge. [Foliated fault rocks of this clast size are mylonites.]

- They may be subdivided into protocataclasites (0-50% matrix), mesocataclasites (50-90% matrix) and ultracataclasites (90%+ matrix) – in each case the matrix comprises any clast that is < 100 µm

- The matrix clasts are crushed fragments that form a cohesive, often flinty, rock

## Slide 45

### Fabrics in clay-bearing fault gouges

- Clay-bearing fault gouges have a range of fabric elements that can serve as shear sense indicators. The most important are:

- P-foliation due to the alignment of platy minerals

- R1 Riedel shears with slip synthetic to shear sense

- Y-shears parallel to the shear zone walls

- B-shears that run along the shear zone walls

- Other subsidiary shears (R2 Riedel shears; P-shears; X-shears), fractures in the tension gash orientation (T-fractures), and fractured and boudinaged clasts with the same geometries as described for mylonites, may also be present

- XZ plane

**Notes:**

The lettering of these fabrics means that they can be referred to as Y-B-P-R fabrics in distinction to the S-C-C´ fabrics described on Slide 20. Note that geometrically the P-foliation in a brittle shear zone is the same as the S foliation in a ductile shear zone, and the R1 Riedel shears are the same as the C´ foliation (compare the figure on this slide with that on Slide 20). This geometrical correspondence means that some have used the two nomenclatures interchangeably
For an attempt to find a reason to distinguish the two nomenclatures when it is applied to fabrics in brittle shear zones, see Volpe et al., 2022, Journal of Structural Geology 165, article 104743, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2022.104743. They consider that the Y-B-P-R nomenclature is best suited to frictionally-strong fault rocks that form very narrow shear zones which are characterized by intense cataclasis, whereas the S-C-C´ nomenclature is best suited to phyllosilicate-rich fault rocks that are characterized by a more distributed deformation and an intense foliation, and in which deformation is dominated by frictional sliding along phyllosilicate foliae. These distinctions are rather too subtle for the content presented in this lecture where the frictionally-strong fault rock category has been treated as unfoliated cataclasites, the S-C-C´ nomenclature is reserved for high temperature ductile shear zones, and the Y-B-P-R nomenclature for clay-bearing fault gouges. For the moment, that is more conventional
