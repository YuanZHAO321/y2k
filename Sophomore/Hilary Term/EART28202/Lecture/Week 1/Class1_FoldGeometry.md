## Slide 2

### Folding

- A geological surface (e.g., bedding, cleavage) that is curved is said to be folded. Folds occur on all scales from the microscopic to the regional

- There is a great variety in the geometry of folds, and this reflects differences in the way that they formed – if we are to interpret the significance of these geometric variations, first we need to describe their geometry in a rigorous way

- Such descriptions also allow us to make reliable extrapolations of the geometry of folds at depth or laterally into areas of no exposure

**Notes:**

For folding at the microscopic (thin section) scale, see Passchier & Trouw, 2005, Microtectonics (2nd edition). This is available as an ebook from the library. ## Slide 3

### Essential geometric elements: fold axis/profile plane

- Folds that can be generated by translating (no rotation) a straight line are described as cylindrical; folds that cannot be generated in this way are described as non-cylindrical

- The line used to generate the fold is the fold axis

- In cylindrical folds the fold shape on parallel cross section planes, whatever their orientation, is constant → we choose one of these orientations to describe fold geometry, and by convention that orientation is the plane that is perpendicular to the fold axis (profile plane)

**Notes:**

Real folds are not perfectly cylindrical, but they may be subdivided into parts that are approximately cylindrical. The use of stereograms to describe fold geometry relies on the folds being cylindrical over the area from which the data have been collected
Note that the fold axis is not defined at any particular location on the folded surface, that is, the fold axis is not just along the hinge

## Slide 4

### Essential geometric elements: parts of a fold

- Variations in curvature around a fold define its essential elements: a line connecting points of maximum curvature is the hinge line; lines joining points of zero curvature (where the surface changes its sense of curvature) are inflection lines

- The region around the hinge line is the hinge zone; the regions between hinge zones are the limbs

- The line connecting the highest topographic points is the crest line, and that connecting the lowest points is the trough line. Non-cylindrical folds may have crest line and trough line culminations and depressions

**Notes:**

Some points to remember:
 the hinge line is parallel to the fold axis but unlike the fold axis, it is at a specific location on the fold;
 inflection lines separate different folds, that is, a single fold is the portion of the folded surface that lies between two adjacent inflection lines;
 the crest line and trough line each only coincide with a hinge line if the fold has a vertical axial plane (for the definition of an axial plane, see Slide 6)
Figures from Twiss & Moores, p.277, 278

## Slide 5

### Essential geometric elements: enveloping surfaces

- In a train of folds, the surface that contains the crest lines (or trough lines) is described as the enveloping surface. The dip of this surface is commonly called the sheet dip and is effectively the average dip of the folded surface

- The surface containing all the inflection lines is described as the median surface. This need not be halfway between the two enveloping surfaces that are on either side of it

- The enveloping and median surfaces are central to the notion of fold symmetry and fold scale

## Slide 6

### Essential geometric elements: axial surface

- The surface that contains the hinge lines of a nested stack of folded surfaces is called the axial surface (axial plane if that surface is planar)

- The line of intersection of the axial surface with any other surface of interest (usually the ground surface) is called the axial trace

**Notes:**

In principle, one could distinguish the hinge plane and the axial plane in the same way as the hinge line and fold axis are distinguished, that is, the hinge plane and axial plane are parallel with each other, but the former relates to a specific location (the hinge of the fold) whereas the latter does not (see notes to Slides 3 and 4)
Very important: on a geological map the plunge direction of the fold axis is not generally in the same orientation as the axial trace (it is ONLY in the same orientation if the axial plane is vertical or the fold axis is horizontal). Since the fold axis lies in the axial plane, on a stereogram it lies on the great circle that describes the orientation of the axial plane; hence the plunge direction of the fold axis can only be the same as the strike of the axial plane if the axial plane is vertical or the fold axis is horizontal (try it and see)

Figure right redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Twiss & Moores, p.280

## Slide 7

### Essential geometric elements: antiform/synform

- Folds that close down are synforms; folds that close up are antiforms. Folds that close horizontally are neutral folds

- Folds with the youngest rocks in their cores are synclines; folds with the oldest rocks in their cores are anticlines

- Folds face in the direction within their axial plane that they young. Hence, they face upwards, downwards or sideways. The fold to the right is an upward-facing anticline

**Notes:**

A fold is said to close in the direction that the limbs converge. Strictly, the direction of closure is the direction in which the limbs converge as seen within the plane that is at right angles to the hinge line (i.e., as seen within the profile plane)
Facing is an alternative terminology that is used to convey younging and in principle it gets around using the terms antiform/synform – an upward-facing anticline is an anticlinal antiform while a downward-facing anticline is an anticlinal synform

## Slide 9

### Fold symmetry  1

- A fold is symmetric if:

- the median surface and the axial surface are perpendicular, and

- the median surface and the axial surface divide an antiform-synform pair into mirror-symmetric quarter waves (each limb is the same length)

- If either of these conditions is not satisfied, then the fold is said to be asymmetric

**Notes:**

Note the spelling of asymmetry (one ‘s’ and two ‘m’)
Photo (Steve Covey-Crump): asymmetric fold at Stair Hole, Lulworth Cove, Dorset, formed during the Alpine orogeny
Figures bottom redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Ramsay & Huber, p.320

## Slide 10

### Fold symmetry  2

- Asymmetric folds usually violate both conditions for symmetry, that is, the median surface and axial plane are not perpendicular, and the fold limbs are of unequal length

- The shape of an antiform-synform pair in the profile plane (drawn with the short limb as the intermediate limb, and by convention looking down the fold axis) may then be described as being of S- or Z-asymmetry

- Alternatively, we may use the concept of vergence:

- the direction of vergence (a compass direction) is the horizontal direction within the profile plane that the top of the short limb must be rotated so that the short limb becomes parallel with the long limbs (= direction towards the antiform if you are on the long limb)

- Z-fold (assuming we are looking down plunge)

- Verge to the “right”

- S-fold (assuming we are looking down plunge)

- Verge to the “left”

**Notes:**

Whether an asymmetric fold pair has the appearance of S-asymmetry or of Z-asymmetry depends upon whether one is looking down the fold axis or up the fold axis (try it by drawing a ‘Z’ on a bit of paper – this looks like an ‘S’ if you look through the back of the paper). This means that for non-cylindrical folds in which the hinge-line passes through culminations and depressions so that the fold axis plunges first one way and then 180° in the other direction, congruent minor folds on the same limb may be of S-asymmetry in one place but Z-asymmetry in another. The direction of vergence does not suffer from this descriptive untidiness – all congruent minor folds on the same limb verge in the same direction. Consequently, vergence is favoured over S/Z terminology by many structural geologists (see Bell, 1981, Journal of Structural Geology 3: 197-202, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(81)90015-8). The S/Z terminology is, however, intuitively simpler for the novice structural geologist and so has wide currency – for a comments on when it might be preferable to use the S/Z approach rather than vergence and vice versa, see Weijermars, 1985, Geologische Rundschau 74: 611-622, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01821216

## Slide 12

### Fold symmetry  4

- Asymmetric folds which violate only one of the conditions for symmetry arise when the limbs are of different thickness

- In such cases, the axial plane does not bisect the angle between the limbs (as seen in the profile plane), and it becomes convenient to refer to the bisector, that is, the plane that does bisect this angle and which also contains the hinge line of the folded surface

**Notes:**

Since the definition of an axial surface requires at least two nested folded surfaces (see Slide 6), it follows that when you have a single folded surface, the axial plane is not defined. In this situation the bisector is all that can be specified. This situation most commonly arises in artificial Structural Geology classwork exercises; in nature if you have a folded bed, the top and bottom of the bed form two nested surfaces that you can use to define the axial surface

## Slide 14

### Fold scale

- The scale of folds is characterized by their amplitude (A) and wavelength (λ) as defined with reference to the enveloping surfaces and median surface. (If the median surface is not midway between the enveloping surfaces, then two values of amplitude are required)

- Two other length scales are important when it comes to considering how folds form:

- arc length, defined as the distance measured along the folded surface between two inflection points or (as illustrated above) twice that distance, and

- layer thickness

**Notes:**

Subtle variations in the definitions of amplitude and wavelength have arisen between different authors, largely because real folds are not perfectly cylindrical and so decisions have to be made about how to apply formal mathematical definitions (see Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 of Adamuszek et al., 2011, Journal of Structural Geology 33: 1406-1416, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2011.06.003)

## Slide 17

### Fold shape  1

- Various quantitative measures of fold shape have been defined:

- Fold aspect ratio: amplitude ÷ half wavelength

- Fold bluntness: a measure of how rounded or angular the hinge is

- Fold tightness: defined by the interlimb angle (the minimum angle between the limbs as measured in the profile plane, that is, the angle between the lines tangent to the inflection points of the fold in that plane)

- Quantitative measures of this kind are rarely used but the features that they describe are commonly noted using adjectives such as concentric, chevron, isoclinal

**Notes:**

Formal definitions of these three measures may be found in the textbooks by Twiss & Moores (p.284-285) and by Ragan (p.373-374)
Note that the use of half wavelength in the definition of fold aspect ratio makes sense if you recall that a fold is defined as that part of the curved surface lying between two adjacent inflection lines (see point 2 in the notes to Slide 4). Amplitude is defined that way, but wavelength is twice this distance (see figure on Slide 14)

### Fold classification by layer thickness variations  2

- A more rigorous treatment utilizes the concept of a dip isogon, which is the line connecting points of equal dip on either side of a folded layer (in the profile plane)

### Summary of key geometric descriptors

- The essential features that must be described (being quantitative whenever possible):

- Antiform/synform, anticline/syncline, symmetry, scale (wavelength, amplitude)

- Orientation of fold axis (plunge / plunge direction)

- Orientation of axial plane (strike / dip)

- Orientation of representative limbs (strike / dip of limbs at the inflection lines)

- Interlimb angle

- Orientation of bisector (strike / dip)

- Additional features useful for interpreting the mechanism of fold formation include:

- Fold hinge shape

- Layer thickness variations (parallel/similar or a full isogon analysis)

- Mechanistically significant features (to be discussed in Class 2) including the presence of slickolites, outer arc tension gashes, etc.

## Slide 24

- Congruent Minor Structures

**Notes:**

Congruent minor structures are small-scale structures that form at the same time as a larger scale structure, and which have a geometry that is systematically related to the geometry of that larger scale structure. Here we consider the suite of congruent minor structures and fabrics that form during folding; in later classes we will consider congruent minor structures that form around faults and shear zones. Obtaining an intuitive understanding of the geometry of congruent minor structures is absolutely essential for working out how to integrate relatively small-scale structural observations into a larger scale tectonic story. We will consider such syntheses of structural observations in last three classes of this unit, but what I will say then will not make much sense if you haven’t understood congruent minor structures first

## Slide 26

### Minor folds

- Around large-scale folds, frequently there are smaller, asymmetric minor folds (sometimes described as drag folds or parasitic folds)

- The direction of vergence of these minor folds changes across the fold hinge and so minor fold vergence (sense of asymmetry) may be used to locate this hinge

- The fold axis of these minor folds is the same as the fold axis of the larger scale fold

**Notes:**

The term ‘drag fold’ implies a rather dubious theory about how these folds form. ‘Parasitic fold’ is more general, and it also conveys the sense that these folds have formed on a larger scale structure (the term ‘minor fold’ does not quite capture this meaning)

## Slide 27

- Formation of planar and linear fabrics

- During deformation several different processes may result in the formation of a planar fabric or a lineation

- Passive rotation of elongate crystals

- Mineral shape change by solution and precipitation in strain shadows

- Mineral shape change by plasticity

- Combination of 1 and 2 in polymineralic aggregates

- Grain growth of micas parallel with their basal planes; grains that are oriented parallel with the foliation grow to greater lengths

- Oriented nucleation and growth of new grains

- Mimetic growth of elongate grains due to restrictions on growth direction imposed by a pre-existing foliation

- Restricted growth parallel with platy minerals

## Slide 28

### Cleavage  (definitions)

- The most significant planar fabric in folded rocks is cleavage or schistosity

- Cleavage = the fabric formed in fine-grained rocks up to a scale at which individual cleavage-forming minerals are visible to the naked eye (applies at low metamorphic grade)

- Schistosity = coarser-grained foliations formed at higher metamorphic grades

- Both may be described as penetrative or as spaced

**Notes:**

The term ‘fracture cleavage’ is commonly used for a set of closely spaced fractures in brittle lithologies; the description ‘phyllitic’ may be used for fabrics that are transitional between cleavage and schistosity; the term ‘gneissosity’ may be used for a deformation-induced fabric in very high-grade rocks, although this is now largely out of favour because of the possible confusion it may cause with compositional layering in gneisses
There are many terms that have been developed to describe the morphology of cleavage and schistosity (e.g., see Passchier & Trouw, 2005, Microtectonics (2nd edition), Fig. 4.7, or Price & Cosgrove, p.434-5) but while these allow a certain descriptive precision, they are not particularly useful

## Slide 33

### Cleavage  (refraction)

- Cleavage is often observed to change dip (but not strike) as it passes from one lithology to another. This phenomenon (cleavage refraction) reflects differences in the resistance to flow (the competence) of the layers. Cleavage in more competent lithologies (typically, ones that are less clay-rich) tends to be at a higher angle to bedding than it is in less competent layers

**Notes:**

More competent lithologies are ones that are more resistant to flow
With caution, refracted cleavage may be used as an indicator of graded bedding – it usually becomes more nearly parallel to bedding as the clay mineral content increases towards the top of a normally graded bed. Using this logic in the photo on the left the units are younging towards the left (look at how the cleavage curves across the lighter coloured, more quartz-rich units). The cleavage/bedding asymmetry (S in this view) suggests antiform to the left, but the beds are also younging in this direction. Hence to the left is a synclinal antiform. In this case the cleavage is actually a cleavage generated in a later folding event than the one that formed the antiform and so we can’t really say this – care is required in making too many inferences from a photo at this scale (one needs to match the generation of cleavage to the generation of fold). In the photo on the right the cleavage refraction suggests younging to the right (the cleavage/bedding asymmetry suggests synform to the right – synclinal synform)

### Cleavage  (crenulation)

- The fine-scale layering of cleavage and schistosity means that if the rock is deformed for a second time, the cleavage or schistosity is refolded on a sub-millimetre to centimetre scale. The hinges of these small folds are described as a crenulation lineation and their axial planes as a crenulation cleavage

**Notes:**

If a rock with a well-developed deformation fabric is re-deformed, then a new fabric is formed. We describe this as a crenulation cleavage. Hence, by definition, the existence of a crenulation cleavage means that the rocks have been deformed more than once

In the photo on the right, the cleavage running left-right is folding an earlier cleavage running approximately up-down. The folds are sufficiently well defined to measure an axial plane (= crenulation cleavage)

## Slide 39

### Mineral elongation lineations

- Lineations formed during deformation and often lying within the cleavage plane, include:

- Preferred orientation of minerals with an acicular or prismatic habit

- Small, elongate concentrations of individual minerals

- Minerals with elongate mineral overgrowths

- Grains or other discrete objects that have been deformed into well-aligned elongate shapes

- Aligned biotite porphyroblasts formed mimetically, parallel to a pre-existing lineation – the biotite porphyroblasts overgrow a crenulation (S2) but are parallel to a strong lineation (L1) seen elsewhere in the vicinity

## Slide 40

### Stretching lineations

- Mineral elongation lineations formed parallel with the maximum extension direction of the structure on which they occur, are described as stretching lineations

- Stretching lineations are parallel with the tectonic a-direction (transport direction) – the orientation of this direction may be at any angle to the fold axis within the axial plane

- Garnet porphyroblasts with elongate quartz overgrowths parallel with a well-developed stretching lineation

**Notes:**
