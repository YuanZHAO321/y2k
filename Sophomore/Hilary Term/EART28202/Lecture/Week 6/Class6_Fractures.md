## Slide 7

### Classification of fractures

- A fracture is a surface across which there is a discontinuity in displacement and mechanical properties. Although described as surfaces because they are very narrow in one dimension compared with the other two, at some scale they always have thickness

- Fractures are classified by the displacement across the fracture surface during their formation

- Extension fractures (Mode I): displacement normal to the fracture walls in an opening sense

- Shear fractures (Mode II, Mode III): displacement parallel to the fracture walls – the two modes distinguish two orthogonal slip directions

- Contraction fractures (Mode IV): displacement normal to the fracture walls in a closing sense (anticracks)

- Hybrid fractures (mixed mode): fractures that are some combination of the other types (usually extension + shear OR compression + shear)

## Slide 10

### Contraction fractures (for completeness)

- stylolite (left); slickolite (below)

- Contraction fractures include stylolites. These irregular surfaces have interlocking ‘teeth’ oriented normal to the surface – this implies ‘closing’ in this direction

- Stylolites with teeth oblique to the surface (properly described as slickolites) indicate that these contractional features can also be of hybrid character

**Notes:**

For a comprehensive review of the geometry of stylolites, how they form, and what may be deduced from them about the deformation conditions, see Toussaint et al., 2018, Journal of Structural Geology 114: 163-195, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2018.05.003. Of particular interest is the fact that the roughness of stylolites can be used to infer stresses at the time they were formed (e.g., Rolland et al., 2014, International Journal of Rock Mechanics & Mining Sciences 67: 212–225, http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrmms.2013.08.021; Beaudoin et al., 2016, Tectonics 35: 1687–1712, https://doi.org/10.1002/2016TC004128). This kind of stress analysis can, in principle, be used to place constraints on the growth of folds (e.g., Lacombe et al., 2021, Solid Earth 12, 2145–2157, https://doi.org/10.5194/se-12-2145-2021)
Stylolites have commonly been treated as anticracks (see Fletcher & Pollard, 1981, Geology 9: 419-424, https://doi.org/10.1130/0091-7613(1981)9<419:AMFPSS>2.0.CO;2). The idea is that dissolution on a stylolite produces an elliptical hole; as this hole closes under compressive stress, the stresses around the surface of the hole become more strongly compressive, thereby driving further dissolution; the stresses around the hole are taken simply as the reverse of the stresses around an elliptical hole that is subjected to tensile rather than compressive stresses – hence the term ‘anticrack’. More recent analyses of the stresses around stylolites, however, suggest that these are not well described using the anticrack model but rather that stylolites are better described as elliptical inclusions inside which some volume reduction has occurred. The analysis of the stress around such inclusions shows that in some regions around the inclusion, the stresses are indeed strongly compressive but in other regions they are strongly tensile and/or involve a significant component of shear. This latter analysis does a much better job at accounting for the extension and shear fractures that are commonly seen around stylolites (for a discussion, see Aharanov & Karcz, 2019, Journal of Structural Geology 118: 299-307, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2018.11.002)
Establishing the nature of the stress field around stylolites is not as arcane as it might seem. Petroleum and aquifer geologists working in carbonate reservoirs are strongly interested in stylolites because of their potential impact on fluid permeability (e.g., see Nelson, 1981, American Association of Petroleum Geologists Bulletin 65: 2417-2425, https://doi.org/10.1306/03B599AE-16D1-11D7-8645000102C1865D) – insoluble residues along stylolitic surfaces potentially impede the flow of fluids through the reservoir (although this effect on permeability may not always be as large as is generally thought – see Heap et al., 2014, Geology 42: 51-54, https://doi.org/10.1130/G34900.1). If extension fractures can form around stylolites, their effect on permeability is potentially still less significant
Slickolites are commonly found around flexural slip folds
Photo top (Steve Covey-Crump): stylolite from Carboniferous limestones at West Angle Bay, Pembrokeshire
Photo bottom (from Ramsay & Huber, p.656): slickolite from Holderbank, north Switzerland. The slip sense shown by the lazy arrows in red follows from the direction of maximum compression

## Slide 11

### Relationship with the principal stress directions

- Extension fractures open parallel to the direction of minimum compressive stress, σ3 (direction of least resistance), and propagate within the σ1 - σ2 plane

- Under tensile conditions this means that the opening direction is parallel to the stretching direction

- Contraction fractures form perpendicular to the direction of maximum compressive stress, σ1 (the σ2 - σ3 plane)

- Shear fractures form at an angle of less than 45° to σ1 and ideally contain σ2

**Notes:**

To be strictly accurate, what we see when observing fractures is strain not stress and so in making the points on this slide, we are implicitly assuming that the axes of stress and strain coincide. This is OK in this class because we are considering fractures with very small displacements across them

### Shear fractures (in experiments)

- Samples deformed experimentally in axial compression at elevated confining pressure form shear fractures at 20°- 30° to the maximum compressive stress

- In such tests, σ1 is axial stress and

- σ2 = σ3 is the confining pressure, and so the normal stress and shear stress on the fracture plane may be obtained from the angle that the fracture plane makes with σ1 using a Mohr diagram

- Why don’t fractures form at the orientation for which the shear stress on the fracture plane is greatest = 45° to σ1 (2θ = 90°)?  On a fracture plane at ~ 30° to σ1 (2θ = 120°), although the shear stress is slightly smaller, the normal stress is much smaller

## Slide 15

### The effect of confining pressure on fracture

- Plotting the normal stress (σn) and shear stress (τ ) on the fracture plane at failure for samples deformed at different confining pressures, defines a failure envelope on a Mohr diagram

- Experiments show a continuous gradation with increasing confining pressure between

- Extension fractures with fracture planes parallel to σ1,

- Hybrid extension + shear fractures with a fracture plane at < 13° to σ1,

- Contractional shear fractures with a fracture plane at > 13° to σ1

**Notes:**

In the figure, fractures form at the orientations given by the solid dots where the Mohr circle touches the failure envelope. Note the increasing diameter of the Mohr circle with increasing confining pressure. Confining pressure is σ3, which, in each case, is where the left side of the circle cuts the σn axis. Increasing confining pressure increases the normal stress across the fracture plane and hence suppresses fracture formation (see notes to the previous slide), that is, greater values of σ1 - σ3 (σ1 - σ3 = diameter of the Mohr circle) are required to cause failure
The three green Mohr circles show three different modes of extensional failure – which one occurs depends upon the difference between σ1 and σ3. Bullet point 1 on the slide refers to the failure mode depicted on the furthest right green extensional failure figure
For further information on the transition from extension to hybrid to shear fractures with increasing confining pressure, see the experiments of Ramsay & Chester, 2004, Nature 428: 63-66, https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02333

## Slide 16

### The effect of pore fluids

- Extension fractures open → they should be restricted to shallow depths (small confining pressures). But dykes and veins are found to great depths (into the mantle), and the reason why this is possible relates to pore fluid pressure

- Pore fluid pressure acts against the normal stresses, while leaving the shear stresses unaffected → on a Mohr diagram pore fluid pressures shift the Mohr circle left without changing its diameter (if the diagram is plotted with σn, eff not σn on the x-axis)

- Increasing pore fluid pressure leads to a shear fracture if the failure envelope is touched while the effective σ3 (= σ3 - pfluid) is still compressive (figure left) or to a hybrid or extension fracture if it is touched when the effective σ3 is negative (figure right)

**Notes:**

Fluid pressure acts outwards in a direction everywhere normal to the pore surface. Hence it affects only the normal stresses
Triggering of earthquakes under man-made lakes is a well-documented phenomena that is described using the principles outlined on this slide – water from the lake percolates down through fractures in the subsurface below, raising pore fluid pressures, which then ultimately leads to the earthquake. For a review see Gupta, 2002, Earth Science Reviews 58: 279-310, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0012-8252(02)00063-6. For an analysis of the earthquakes triggered in Egypt following construction of the Aswan Dam, see Adams, 1983, Nature 301: 14, https://doi.org/10.1038/301014a0; and Gahalaut & Hassoup, 2012, Journal of Geophysical Research 117(B2), article B02303, https://doi.org/10.1029/2011JB008796

## Slide 17

### Increasing the significance of fluid pressure

- Processes that increase pore fluid pressure relative to the confining pressure and may thereby lead to fracturing include:

- Compaction and cementation: reduces pore volume and so raises pore pressure

- Erosion: removes overburden (decreases confining pressure) and so, if the fluid cannot leak away, this increases the relative significance of the pore pressure

- Osmosis: separation of aqueous fluid reservoirs of different salinities by a semi-permeable membrane (e.g., clay-rich layer) causes fluid diffusion from the less saline fluid to the more saline fluid

- Production of fluid through diagenetic or metamorphic reactions (dehydration of hydrous minerals, e.g., clays, micas, amphiboles, and melting reactions)

- Differences in thermal expansion between the pore fluids and host rocks during heating

- An important first step in interpreting natural fractures is to consider the potential significance of each of these

## Slide 19

### Surface morphology (extension fractures)

- The surfaces of extension fractures commonly show a subtle pattern of ridges and grooves (hackle) diverging from a central axis (plumose structures or hackle plumes). These indicate the fracture propagation direction

- At the edge of the fracture face, the plane may segment into a series of planes slightly twisted from the main face to form a hackle fringe of en échelon extension fractures. Second order hackle plumes may be developed on the fringe faces

**Notes:**

Ideally an extension fracture expands radially from some nucleation point in such a way that at any instant during propagation the propagation front has the shape of an ellipse. The rate of propagation increases as the fracture grows and so the fracture surface becomes rougher. Eventually propagation is so fast that stress oscillations or readjustments at the crack tip in response to meeting some interface (e.g., a bedding plane) cause the fracture to bend / twist to form the hackle fringes

## Slide 20

### Surface morphology (extension fractures)  2

- Sometimes curvilinear rib marks and ripple marks cross the lines of the hackle

- Rib marks are cuspate in section or are composed of smoothly curved ramps and are perpendicular to the hackle lines. They are interpreted as arrest lines formed when fracture propagation temporarily stopped

- Ripple marks are rounded and oblique to the hackle lines and are thought to form during very fast fracture propagation

- Hackle marks on intersecting fracture surfaces may allow the relative timing of fracture formation to be established

**Notes:**

### Fracture propagation (shear fractures)

- In contrast, shear fractures cannot propagate in their own plane but instead propagate either by generating new extension cracks at their tips (wing cracks) or by linking up already existing extension cracks

- At the terminations of shear fractures the displacement of the main fracture is distributed on many smaller fractures, arranged as synthetic shear fractures (splaying), as extension fractures on one side of the fracture (horsetailing), or as antithetic shear fractures

- Horsetailing at the end of a shear fracture in gneiss

**Notes:**

The formation of wing cracks reflects the distribution of stresses around an elliptical hole (the crack) – see Twiss & Moores, p.226-230. We shall come back to this next week when we consider how shear fractures coalesce via wing cracks to form a through-going fault

## Slide 23

### Fracture interaction

- The stress field is perturbed around fractures. If a new fracture grows towards another, it encounters the local perturbation of the remote stress field that has been set up by the other fracture and curves so as to maintain a 90° angle to the local σ3

- The sense and extent of curvature depends upon the relative magnitude and orientation of the remote principal stresses and hence provides information about these stresses

**Notes:**

On the use of joint curvature to estimate palaeostresses, see Dyer, 1988, Journal of Structural Geology 10:685-699, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(88)90076-4; and Olson & Pollard, 1989, Geology 17: 345-348, https://doi.org/10.1130/0091-7613(1989)017<0345:IPFNFP>2.3.CO;2
Figure left (from Twiss & Moores, p.250) is an example of the interactions between a propagating fracture as it encounters an open old fracture (one that cannot sustain a shear stress). The remote stress field is such that within the locally perturbed stress field a tensile stress acts along the old fracture. In this case the new fracture intersects the old one at 90°. If a compressive stress acted along the old fracture the intersection would be at 0°. If the old fracture is closed and can support a shear stress, any angle between 0° and 90° is conceivable depending upon the remote stresses. For further discussion, see Twiss & Moores, p.248-250
Photo right (Steve Covey-Crump): mudcracks in the riverbed of L’Asse de Clumanc, Barrême, France. Note how the crack just to the right of the coin curves as it approaches the earlier crack

## Slide 25

### Measurements of fracture density

- Estimating fracture spacing and intensity is important for engineering and fluid flow applications

- Estimates are obtained by counting the number of fractures intersecting a line (preferably circular to avoid ‘cut effects’ arising from the orientation of the sampling plane)

- If combined with counts of fracture tips and fracture intersections, we can characterize the topology of fracture network and assess fracture connectivity

- Different impressions of joint frequency on different planes

**Notes:**

For fracture traces = intersection of a fracture on a plane of measurement, we may define fracture trace intensity = mean total length of fractures per unit area; fracture trace density = mean number of fracture centres per unit area; mean fracture trace length = mean fracture length for the individual fractures of a fracture population. Note that correlating the statistics of fracture traces measured in 2d with those of their parent 3d fracture network is a further step in the analysis (e.g., see Zhang et al., 2002, Géotechnique 52: 419-433, https://doi.org/10.1680/geot.2002.52.6.419)

Some important practical papers

## Slide 28

### Fractures in slightly deformed sedimentary rocks

- One of the features of horizontally bedded sedimentary successions that have been subjected to little or no deformation is the presence of well-defined, near vertical fracture sets

- These result from changes in horizontal stresses during erosion and uplift

- We saw in the last class that in the absence of tectonic stresses Poisson effects and changes in temperature during burial and subsequent erosion / uplift lead to a stress state in which the maximum compressive stress (σ1) is vertical and the least compressive stress (σ3) is horizontal

- If there is sufficient pore fluid pressure to make the effective σ3 tensile then a vertical fracture set forms normal to σ3

- This relieves the stress in that direction, and so if the other horizontal principal effective stress is also tensile, that becomes the maximum effective tensile stress, and a second set of vertical joints forms orthogonal to the first

**Notes:**

The relevant bit of the last class was the discussion of the uniaxial strain reference state for horizontal stresses in the lithosphere (Class 5 Slide 29). In the uniaxial reference state, the horizontal stresses are about 0.33 to 0.5 times the vertical stress
Photo (from Fossen, p.91): joints in the Permian sandstones of the Colorado Plateau, exposed in the Colorado River

## Slide 29

### Fractures in slightly deformed sedimentary rocks

- One may adapt simple models of this kind to include stresses set up by lithospheric flexure during burial and uplift, or to include far-field stresses

- E.g., analysis of the stresses in a rectangular slab subjected to horizontal compression, allows the stresses within a foreland sedimentary succession to be predicted: σ1 ~ horizontal and so since σ2 ~ σ3 one can make a case for σ3 being horizontal. Hence fractures should be vertical

- Near to the thrust front, where σ1 is large,  these will be conjugate vertical shear fractures, but as σ1 decreases away from the thrust front, vertical extension fractures are expected

- Such models help to explain why non-orthogonal vertical fracture sets are present in lightly deformed sedimentary successions

**Notes:**

The model predicting the stresses within a rectangular block subjected to horizontal compressive loading was first presented by Hafner, 1951, Geological Society of America Bulletin 62: 373-398, https://doi.org/10.1130/0016-7606(1951)62[373:SDAF]2.0.CO;2 (see also Twiss and Moores’ worked problem, p.201-208, although that does not consider the stresses within the block). The interpretation of the orientation of the fractures that Hafner’s model implies in foreland sediments is described in Price & Cosgrove, p.212-213; their Chapter 9 contains much else that is of interest for interpreting regional vertical fracture sets

### Exfoliation (sheet) joints

## Slide 34

### Joint infill, veins and hydraulic fracturing

- Fracture surfaces may contain a thin mineral deposit (e.g., quartz, feldspar, calcite, zeolites) that indicates the importance of fluids during their formation (assuming that the fluids did not come in later and use the fractures as conduits)

- Veins form when fluid pressure builds up sufficiently to generate extension fractures (hydraulic fracturing), and minerals are then precipitated out of solution – in most cases solubility decreases with decreasing fluid pressure (that is, as a fracture opens)

- In areas of very high fluid pressure tectonic brecciation may occur during hydraulic fracturing

**Notes:**

Since veins and joints are both extension fractures, it is tempting to treat them in the same way. However, they form at different times and under different conditions. ## Slide 39

### Formation of sills

- Intrusion of a horizontal sill (sheet) requires

- pmagma  - σv  >  Tv

- where Tv is the tensile strength of the rock in the vertical direction

- Sills must be fed by a feeder ‘dyke’, and so at the junction of sill and dyke (see figure right)

- σv - σh   =   Th - Tv

- Since both Tv and Th are small (the tensile strength of rocks is small), this means that the condition for the intrusion of a sill is that σv - σh ~ 0

- Magma will rise as a dyke while σv > σh but σv decreases towards the surface, and so there eventually comes a point where σv - σh ~ 0  and a sill becomes possible; a sill is then favoured if Th - Tv > 0 (as it commonly is in horizontally layered successions)

## Slide 50

### Evolution of polygonal fractures

- The cracks nucleate at flaws; as they propagate towards earlier formed cracks, they curve to meet them orthogonally (the minimum principal stress is parallel to the free surface of the earlier crack)

- In repeated opening/closing cycles, previously open cracks define lines of weakness that lead to an adjustment of the crack network to a polygonal shape

**Notes:**

Note that the free surface of an open crack does not support a shear stress and so one principal stress direction must be orthogonal to it and the other parallel to it. Since the crack is open, the principal stress orthogonal to the crack must be approximately zero, and likewise, since the crack is on the ground surface, the vertical stress (also a principal stress) is also zero. This leaves the principal stress that is parallel to the crack. In a shrinking mud this stress is tensile and so since the other stresses are ~0, this stress must be σ3. Hence as a propagating crack approaches this already open crack, it will do so at right angles to it. This leads to a rectilinear pattern of cracks (vertices 90°). The evolution of such a rectilinear pattern towards one with vertices nearer 120° is possible over many crack opening and healing cycles because while the cracks in any one cycle are influenced by the position of the cracks in previous cycles, they are still able to adjust their position slightly and to vary their order of opening (see Goehring, 2013, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A 371, article 20120353, https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2012.0353)
The formation of shrinkage cracks during dessication is not the only way to produce polygonal fractures in muds; polygonal fractures may be produced under water. Such fractures are described as syneresis cracks because a mud containing swelling clays (e.g., montmorillonite) can shrink if the salinity of the water that it is in contact with increases (increased salinity deflocculates clay particles and contracts the lattice structure of swelling clays) – in chemistry syneresis is the expulsion of fluid from a gel. Syneresis cracks are observed in the geological record (particularly in shallow water marine or lacustrine environments where changes in surface run-off can alter water salinity), although it is not always clear that salinity-induced syneresis is the reason why shrinkage of the muds occurred. An alternative model appeals to seismically-induced dewatering of the muds within thinly interbedded muds and sands located in the shallow sub-surface in order to explain why some ‘syneresis’ cracks (a) appear to propagate upwards as well as downwards, and (b) are rather larger than experimentally produced syneresis cracks. For the general characteristics of ‘syneresis’ cracks and the seismic model, see Pratt, 1998, Sedimentary Geology 117: 1-10, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0037-0738(98)00023-2

## Slide 51

### Columnar jointing

- For homogeneous media containing few flaws (e.g., crystallizing melts), rapid nucleation and propagation of multiple cracks from a few points of initiation → polygonal fracture patterns – this is the geometry that releases maximum strain energy per unit crack area

- These extension fractures (columnar joints) form perpendicular to surfaces of equal temperature (often perpendicular to the interface with the country rock)

**Notes:**

More precisely, polygonal fracture patterns with ~120° vertices form rapidly from few nucleation sites when the contraction in a given plane is approximately the same in all directions within that plane. In a cooling magma this will be true within a surface of equal temperature. The fractures initiate where the cooling rates are fastest (e.g., top and bottom of a lava flow) and propagate towards the slowest cooling part of the magma body. Classical studies of columnar joint formation in cooling igneous sheets are Peck & Minakami, 1968, Geological Society of America Bulletin 79: 1151-1166, https://doi.org/10.1130/0016-7606(1968)79[1151:TFOCJI]2.0.CO;2; and DeGraff & Aydin, 1987, Geological Society of America Bulletin 99: 605-617, https://doi.org/10.1130/0016-7606(1987)99<605:SMOCJA>2.0.CO;2. The latter study used hackle marks (see Slide 19) on the surface of the fractures to establish sequences and progression of fracture development
