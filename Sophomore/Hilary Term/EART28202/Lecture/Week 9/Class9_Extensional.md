## Slide 3

### Rifts

- The most obvious regional scale extensional tectonic system is the rift

- Rifting is usually some combination of

- active rifting: driven by rising hot mantle material that causes crustal doming and thereby generates tensile stresses, and

- passive rifting: developed in response to far-field stresses related to plate movement

- HORDA Platform

- WNW

- ENE

- The North Sea rift system formed in two E-W extensional episodes, one in the Permo-Triassic and the other in the Late Jurassic

**Notes:**

Active rifting arises from stretching over the top of a growing dome induced by, for example, an underlying thermal plume. Passive rifting arises from the tensional stresses that are set up by plates moving apart (such stresses can also arise locally in both contractional and strike-slip tectonic settings). The distinction between active and passive rifting is conceptually significant in setting up geophysical models of extension, but it is rather forced because there are active and passive elements within each rifting environment, e.g., in passive rifting, extension may lead to doming as relatively hot, deep rocks are brought nearer to the surface, but this doming is assigned as a secondary process. For this reason, some favour a classification of rifting that is tied more closely to the tectonic setting (see Merle, 2011, Tectonophysics 513: 88-95, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tecto.2011.10.004)

The orange-coloured units are Permo-Triassic, the yellow is Jurassic, the black and deep blue is Cretaceous, and the light blue is Cenozoic. ## Slide 4

### Rift development

- Rifting occurs in three phases

- Initial phase: rifting as a result of large-scale crustal doming; steep fracture systems form; possible intrusion of magma generated at depth

- Main stretching phase: formation of major faults/fault blocks that accommodate the crustal thinning

- Late subsidence phase: cooling and basin deepening once stretching has ceased; fault movement is mostly limited to that induced by differential compaction of the sedimentary succession

- The extensional development of the rift influences its sedimentary record: syn-rift sediments show thickness variations across faults (hanging wall thickening and footwall thinning/non-deposition); the post-rift sequence depends upon the geometry of the fault blocks and the extent of thermal subsidence

**Notes:**

The figures illustrate current understanding of how crustal thinning proceeds to the point where oceanic crust forms. (A) Thinning is initially accommodated by slip along conjugate shear zones that cut through the upper crust and lower crust with the middle crust between them deforming by ductile flow. (B) The upper mantle is thinned and replaced by deeper, hotter mantle that is intruded by mafic magmas. (C) Faults cut from the surface into the mantle, and seawater infiltrates these faults thereby initiating serpentinization. Serpentinization weakens the upper mantle layer promoting strain localization and allowing the normal faults to root at low angle. Ongoing extension leads to buoyant flow of the partially serpentinized mantle along the low angle detachment faults (see Slide 10), resulting (D) in horizontal stretching of the subcontinental mantle at the seafloor

## Slide 7

### Extension in active contractional orogenic belts

- Extension is not restricted to rifts and passive margins – some of the most impressive extensional faults are to be found in contractional orogenic belts

- While an ocean still exists between two converging continents, extension occurs by back-arc rifting and also in the outer arc of the oceanic plate where it bends on entering the subduction zone

- Normal faults may form in the orogenic wedge if it is over-thickened by incorporation of a basement slice

- A detached and heated basement slice may be buoyant enough to ascend, bounded by a thrust on its lower side and by a normal fault on its upper side (channel flow)

- Heating of thickened crust may lead to sufficient weakening for it to collapse under its own weight (gravitational orogenic collapse)

## Slide 8

### Post-orogenic collapse

- At the end of a contractional orogeny the relatively cool lithospheric mantle root under the orogenic belt, together with phase transitions such as eclogitization, may make that root sufficiently dense for it to delaminate and sink into the deeper mantle. Replacement by hot material leads to regional uplift and gravitational collapse in the crust above

- Extension induced by delamination may use the thrusts formed previously during collision (inversion), or may involve the formation of new normal faults

- The emplacement of post-orogenic minor intrusions (typically lamprophyres derived from metasomatized sub-continental lithospheric mantle) takes advantage of this extension

**Notes:**

Figure (redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Molnar et al., 1998, Geophysical Journal International 133: 568-584, https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-246X.1998.00510.x): a cartoon representing the life and times of a contractional orogenic belt: (1) initial lithospheric thickening during collision of relatively cool, strong lithosphere, (2) delamination of the lithospheric root occurs because it is denser than the underlying asthenosphere, (3) uplift ensues as warm asthenosphere replaces the cool lithospheric root, (4) the crust heats up and eventually becomes too weak to support its own weight, and so undergoes gravitational collapse
Delamination of this kind is an example of a Rayleigh-Taylor convective instability (an instability that occurs in a convecting system when a dense layer overlies a less dense layer). The switch from dominantly contractional to dominantly extensional deformation at the end of a collisional orogeny has been successfully modelled as a Rayleigh-Taylor instability by geophysicists (see Houseman & Molnar, 1997, Geophysical Journal International 128: 125-150, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-246X.1997.tb04075.x; Molnar et al., 1998, Geophysical Journal International 133: 568-584, https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-246X.1998.00510.x; Neil & Houseman, 1999, Geophysical Journal International 138: 89-107, https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-246x.1999.00841.x)
The word ‘inversion’ arises because thrusts formed during contraction are reactivated as normal faults, that is, the sense of movement on the fault system is reversed. On ‘inversion’ in the opposite sense (thrusts using pre-existing extensional faults rather than extensional faults using pre-existing thrusts), see Slide 20
Lamprophyre is a field term used to describe hypabyssal igneous rocks of wide-ranging composition that are typically alkali-rich and hydrous. They are commonly the crystallization products of very deeply derived magmas. Those who went on the Scotland Easter field course saw examples of post-orogenic lamprophyric intrusions. In the Scottish Caledonides, these lamprophyres, often ignored because they are only small intrusions, actually collectively account for 10-20% of the total Caledonian magmatism

## Slide 9

### Metamorphic core complexes

- During extreme extension (100%+), thinning and isostatic compensation elevates deeper levels of the crust. Eventually, the high-grade footwall rocks may be exposed at the Earth’s surface

- These are metamorphic core complexes, and they are particularly well-developed in the Basin-and-Range Province of western USA

- The isostatic uplift gives the main normal fault a dome-like shape often described as a turtle-back

- A key feature is the overprinting of ductile fabrics by brittle structures

- Badwater turtle-back, Death Valley

**Notes:**

The role of isostasy in the development of metamorphic core complexes is discussed by Wernicke & Axen, 1988, Geology 16: 848-851, https://doi.org/10.1130/0091-7613(1988)016<0848:OTROII>2.3.CO;2. For reviews of the formation of metamorphic core complexes, see Whitney et al., 2013, Geological Society of America Bulletin 125: 273-298, https://doi.org/10.1130/B30754.1, and Platt et al., 2015, Journal of the Geological Society, London 172: 9-27, https://doi.org/10.1144/jgs2014-036
The overprinting of ductile fabrics by brittle ones is because deeper, high temperature deformation in the shear zones is replaced by brittle fault displacement as the fault is unroofed (brought closer to the surface)

For an account of the Badwater and other Death Valley turtle-backs, see Miller & Pavlis, 2005, Earth-Science Reviews 73: 115-138, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.earscirev.2005.04.007

## Slide 11

### Local extension above domes/magma chambers

- Above structural domes (e.g., over a rising salt dome or a mantle plume), the uplifted and domed units are put into a state of circumferential extension

- The response is a set of normal faults that radiate out from a central point with displacement on these faults decreasing outwards

- Radial fractures within tarmac

**Notes:**

The formation of radial faults above diapirs is mechanically quite complex: some of the faults result from extension as the cover rocks dome and stretch above the diapir, whereas others arise from the stretching imposed as the stem of the diapir attempts to widen. For a discussion of these points, see Coleman et al., 2018, Geology 46: 655-658, https://doi.org/10.1130/G40338.1

### Local extension around loaded lithosphere

- The radial extensional fractures around  the vast volcanic edifice that is Tharsis on Mars do not reflect fractures formed in domed and stretched crust but rather are a consequence of stresses induced by the weight of the volcanic pile

**Notes:**

## Slide 14

### Local extension around loaded lithosphere  2

- If the load is wide compared with the planetary radius, then flexural subsidence is counteracted by the resistance to large scale changes in

- curvature of the lithospheric shell (membrane stresses)

- This leads to tensile hoop stresses around the load and hence to a set of radial extension faults

- Contrast this with the circumferential extension faults that arise around loads that are smaller in diameter where flexural subsidence is possible

- Mare Serenitatis

**Notes:**

The nature of membrane stresses can be visualized by trying to deform a table tennis ball. When applying a wide load (squashing the ball with the palm of your hand), the membrane stresses effectively support the load, but when the load is applied over a small area (e.g., by pressing the tip of a pencil into the ball), the load is supported by the flexural stresses. Note that the significance of membrane stresses depends critically on the flexural rigidity of the lithosphere and hence upon factors that control the thickness of the part of the lithosphere that behaves elastically
Thus, the radial fractures around the Tharsis bulge are attributed to the fact that the loading of the lithosphere by the volcanic edifice is over an area that is wide compared with the radius of Mars. Around the perimeter of the bulge there are circumferential wrinkle ridges (on wrinkle ridges, see Class 8 Slide 8) that support this interpretation. For further details of the Tharsis radial fracture story, see Watters & Schultz (editors), 2010, Planetary Tectonics, Cambridge University Press, p.207-212, and for an analysis of the timing of formation of the faults around Tharsis, see Bouley et al., 2018, Earth & Planetary Science Letters 488:126-133, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.epsl.2018.02.019
Figures (by Steve Covey-Crump based on diagrams by Banerdt et al., 1992, In: Kieffer et al. (editors) Mars, University of Arizona Press, p.249-297). The thickness of the arrows is intended to convey the magnitude of the stresses

## Slide 19

- Polygonal normal faults

- Layer-bound systems of small dip-slip normal faults (slip < 100 m), arranged in 3D arrays with a polygonal map pattern, are commonly developed over large areas (50000 km2 +) within clay-rich lithologies

- Polygonal fractures, Amroth

- Mode of formation remains controversial

- Vertical compaction with some lateral expansion in lithologies that have small coefficients of friction, OR

- Chemically driven volume contraction during diagenetic reactions

**Notes:**

Considerable attention has been given to polygonal fault systems because the faults may have a significant influence on fluid flow in mud-rich lithologies that are important as top-seals in hydrocarbon reservoirs or which are used for waste (CO2, nuclear) disposal. For a commentary on some of the interesting features and unknowns about polygonal fault systems, see Cartwright, 2014, Geology 42: 559-560, https://doi.org/10.1130/focus062014.1. For more detailed reviews, see Goulty, 2008, Petroleum Geoscience 14: 389-397, https://doi.org/10.1144/1354-079308-781; Cartwright, 2011, Marine and Petroleum Geology 28: 1593-1610, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpetgeo.2011.06.004
The vertical compaction explanation of the formation of polygonal faults notes that during vertical compaction of a clastic sediment with zero lateral strain, the ratio between the horizontal effective stress (σH) and the vertical effective stress (σV) is approximately given by
	K0  =  σH / σV  =  [ν/(1-ν)]
where ν is Poisson’s ratio (this is the uniaxial strain reference state, see Class 5 Slide 29). But for materials with zero cohesive strength, we may also write for frictional sliding on normal faults
	σH / σV  =  [(1+µ2)½ - µ]2
where µ is the coefficient of friction (the derivation of this relationship comes from the geometry of the Mohr circle diagram shown on Class 7 Slide 22, with σV = σ1 and σH = σ3 – see Class 7 Slide 23). Slip on the normal faults occurs if
	K0  <  [(1+µ2)½ - µ]2
For clay-rich lithologies K0 ~ 0.7 and µ ~ 0.1 and so this inequality is satisfied (0.7 < 0.819) and so slip occurs; in other lithologies K0 is smaller and µ is larger (typically K0 ~ 0.4 and µ ~ 0.6) and so the inequality is not satisfied (slip does not occur). For this model, see Goulty & Swarbrick, 2005, Journal of the Geological Society, London 162: 587-590, https://doi.org/10.1144/0016-764905-004. Clay mineral rich lithologies commonly have anomalously small coefficients of friction compared with most lithologies (see Class 7 Slide 25)
The explanation of the formation of polygonal faults that is based on volume contraction focuses on volume contraction by the removal of porosity. Historically, particular attention has been given to syneresis, that is, to fractures opened up when fine particles coagulate when subjected to electrostatic or osmotic forces of attraction. However, interparticle forces of attraction are very weak compared with confining pressures once the sediment has been buried to depths of more than a few tens of metres, and so attention has switched away from syneresis and on to the onset of diagenetic reactions (particularly those involving dissolution) as a way of promoting pore collapse and matrix contraction
The layer bound nature of polygonal fault systems with no comparable deformation features in the overlying and underlying units has been used to argue that they are not tectonic structures. However, syntectonic polygonal normal fault sets have been reported (see Petracchini et al., 2015, Frontiers in Earth Science 3, article 67, https://doi.org/10.3389/feart.2015.00067)
Figure top left (from Petracchini et al., 2015, Frontiers in Earth Science 3, article 67, https://doi.org/10.3389/feart.2015.00067)
Photo bottom right (Steve Covey-Crump): polygonal fractures developed in mudstone units within the Bishopton Mudstone Formation, Telpyn Point, Amroth, Pembrokeshire. Whether these polygonal fractures are a small-scale version of polygonal fault systems or just some weathering curiosity needs to be established

## Slide 20

### Inversion tectonics

- As a further motivation for considering the geometry of extensional fault systems, it is noted that contractional orogenic belts develop in sequences that have had a previous history of rifting and normal faulting on a passive margin. During contraction these normal faults may be reactivated as thrusts, and hence the geometry of extensional fault systems may profoundly influence the geometry of the subsequent thrust systems

- Normal faults tend to be steeper than thrusts, particularly towards the Earth’s surface, and so contraction commonly leads to more gently dipping shortcut faults near the surface

### Extensional faults  2

- Extensional faults exist with lengths and displacements of millimetres through to those in metamorphic core complexes that are on the scale of mountain ranges with displacements of up to ~ 100 km

- Those with large displacements are gently dipping and are described as detachments (an old term for these that is no longer used is a lag fault)

- Extensional detachments may be distinguished from thrusts because they bring younger rocks over older ones or rocks of low metamorphic grade over rocks of higher grade

- Whipple Mountain detachment, south-eastern California

**Notes:**

Note that the largest displacement extensional faults have rather less displacement than the largest displacement thrust faults (Class 7 Slide 38; Class 8 Slide 12)
Gently dipping extensional faults with large displacements present a significant mechanical paradox: if the rocks involved in the faulting have normal frictional properties, that is, frictional properties that follow Byerlee’s law (see Class 7 Slide 25), then since σ1 is vertical in an extensional setting, they should dip at ~60° (see Class 7 Slides 22-24) – faults with dips of less than 30° are very poorly oriented if the kinds of brittle failure analysis techniques that we used in Practical 6 are to be believed. Gently-dipping extensional faults are commonly explained as being due to the presence of minerals with low coefficients of friction (e.g., phyllosilicates) but this is not a very satisfactory explanation for the general case, and the matter remains something of an open question (for a survey of these issues, see Colletini, 2011, Tectonophysics 510: 253-268, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tecto.2011.07.015). For a more imaginative solution that has been applied to the Whipple Mountain detachment, see Axen, 2020, Geological Society of America Bulletin 132: 1817-1828, https://doi.org/10.1130/B35386.1
A jump in metamorphic grade across a fault can be used to establish whether the fault is extensional or not (see Wheeler & Butler, 1994, Journal of Structural Geology 16: 1023-1027, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(94)90083-3), and this is particularly useful in analysing the sense of movement of exhumed lower and mid-crustal faults – note that there should be a sharp change from high grade in the footwall to lower grade in the hanging wall if the fault is a large displacement extensional fault

Photo top right (Steve Covey-Crump): road surface on the Mam Tor landslip, 13 April 2012

The growth of extensional faults has significant consequences for the tectono-stratigraphic evolution of sedimentary basins. Two end-member models of extensional fault growth are widely cited:
     (a) Propagating Fault (or Isolated Fault) Model: extensional faults lengthen as displacement increases so that the linear relationship between log fault length and log max displacement (see Class 7, Slide 38) is maintained throughout their lifespan
     (b) Constant Fault Length Model: fault length is established very early and then remains approximately constant as displacement accrues, so that by the end of the fault’s lifespan it is at the place observed on the log fault length and log max displacement curve
Opinion seems to be crystallizing on a hybrid model where during the initial stages of displacement (first 20-30% of the fault’s lifespan) the fault lengthens rapidly, and then “stabilizes” so that there is little lengthening during further displacement (remaining 70-80% of the fault’s lifespan). For a commentary on these matters, with access into the previous literature, see Rotevatn et al., 2019, Journal of Structural Geology 125: 174-184, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2018.08.005

## Slide 53

### Pure shear vs. simple shear

- There are two end-member models of extension at the lithospheric scale

- Pure shear: the overall deformation across the region of extension creates symmetric thinning of the lithosphere, accommodated by brittle faulting in the upper crust and plastic deformation at greater depth

- Simple shear: extension is localized on to a dipping shear zone that transects the lithosphere

- In the pure shear model, the highest geothermal gradient is under the centre of the region of extension; in the simple shear model it is offset to one side. This has important consequences for uplift and subsidence patterns and hence for basin development

**Notes:**

There is a variant of the simple shear model in which the shear zone becomes horizontal at some depth below the brittle upper crust, with the units under it deforming overall by pure shear

### Explicit area balancing

- If the hanging wall is not a rigid block, area balancing techniques are required

- Note that the same assumptions as outlined when balancing sections in thrust systems apply, namely plane strain and no volume change, and so the section ought to be drawn parallel to the overall fault movement direction

- Under these assumptions the two areas ( A and Ax ) identified above are equal and so if we know the horizontal displacement (  L ) we can calculate the depth to the detachment ( D ) or vice versa

**Notes:**

The biggest difficulty with the no volume change assumption is that it ignores compaction (vertical shortening) of the sediments during burial. Hence, ideally compaction should be removed before restoration, e.g., by using some lithology-specific compaction vs. depth curve (see comments in Fossen, p.446-447)
Sections can be drawn oblique to the overall extension direction but corrections must then be applied to account for this obliquity

### Area balancing: variants of the Chevron technique

- Different ways have been developed to apply the geometry outlined on the previous slide to calculate

- the shape of a listric fault at depth from the known shape of a rollover antiform, or

- the shape of a rollover antiform from the known shape of a listric fault

**Notes:**

The name ‘Chevron’ refers to the oil company that first utilized these methods. Four methods of solving the two exercises stated on this slide are illustrated. Note that these are purely graphical constructions (kinematic models). In the oblique shear method, choosing the angle of shear is not easy, although commonly it is found that α~60° (effectively the conjugate orientation) works well. For a comparison of these methods and a strategy for choosing the angle of shear in the oblique shear method, see Uzkeda et al., 2014, Journal of Structural Geology 67: 20-36, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2014.07.006
I used to run a practical class that shows how to apply these methods to get (a) the shape of a listric fault at depth from the known shape of a rollover antiform, and (b) the shape of a rollover antiform from the known shape of a listric fault. The methods may be used to recover a curve of amount of extension vs. time, that is, to get the history of fault slip
