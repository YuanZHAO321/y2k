## Slide 2

### Strike-slip tectonic regimes

- Strike-slip faults are faults with a slip vector that is parallel with the strike of the fault, and therefore parallel with the surface of the Earth

- They include some of the longest faults in the world and are renown for their close association with major earthquakes – but they occur at all scales

- Most large-scale strike-slip faults are approximately vertical and so on maps they appear as straight lines. However, locally they curve and these geometrical irregularities generate a range of structures that are economically significant

- San Andreas Fault, California

## Slide 3

### Classification of strike-slip faults

- Different names have been applied to strike-slip faults and the way in which these terms are used has not been completely standardized

- Wrench fault is an old term that is effectively synonymous with strike-slip fault – it is not now commonly used

- Tear faults are relatively small-scale strike-slip faults that accommodate/distribute the slip within larger scale structures; transform faults and transcurrent faults are major regional strike-slip systems

- A transfer fault between two extension fractures

- A key criterion of distinction is the notion of slip transfer: transfer faults are strike-slip faults with tips that terminate against other faults, and which serve to transfer displacement from one fault to another

- Transform faults and some tear faults are transfer faults; transcurrent faults and other tear faults are not

**Notes:**

Photo (from Fossen, p.403): a transfer fault between two extension fractures on a path, Delicate Arch, Utah. Note that the movement sense indicated by the lazy arrows is opposite to the apparent offset of the extension fractures

## Slide 4

### Tear faults that are transfer faults

- In contractional and extensional terrains, transfer faults are approximately parallel with the regional transport direction and connect adjacent reverse or normal faults within larger scale contractional or extensional fault systems

- They may separate domains in which the extensional/contractional faults have different geometry and displacement (e.g., oppositely dipping rift margin faults)

- Tear faults that are transfer faults may reflect the response of the hanging wall to a lateral ramp in the footwall or they may actually be that lateral ramp

## Slide 7

### Oceanic transform faults

- The largest scale transfer faults are transform faults, the active parts of which form plate boundaries

- Oceanic transform faults are of three kinds depending upon the nature of the plate boundaries (ridge or trench) that they connect

**Notes:**

A transform fault is a strike-slip transfer fault that cuts right through the lithosphere. Transform faults are always parallel with the plate motion direction. The concept of transform faulting as something that makes continuous plate movement possible was first noted by Tuzo Wilson in the 1960s (Wilson, 1965, Nature 207: 343-347, https://doi.org/10.1038/207343a0) and was fundamental for the development of plate tectonic theory

## Slide 8

### Oceanic transform faults  2

- Ridge-ridge transform faults (overwhelmingly the most common kind) comprise active parts between the two ridge segments, and inactive parts – formerly active parts that have passed by the adjacent ridge segment

- Note that the slip sense on an active ridge-ridge transform fault is opposite to the apparent offset of the ridges

- A ridge-ridge transform fault is not a single plane but a fracture zone many kilometres wide – the width and structural complexity of this zone increases with increasing spreading-rate

**Notes:**

A ridge-ridge transform fault has been exhumed and exposed within the Troodos massif on Cyprus (see MacLeod & Murton, 1993, Geological Society of London, Special Publications 76: 141-176, https://doi.org/10.1144/GSL.SP.1993.076.01.07)

## Slide 9

### Continental transform faults

- Transform faults occur on the continents as well as in the ocean

- The 1200 km long and 100-150 km wide San Andreas Fault Zone is a particularly well studied example

- 600-1000 km strike-slip displacement

**Notes:**

For a major review of the features of continental transform faults, see Şengör et al., 2019, in: Duarte JC (editor), Transform Plate Boundaries and Fracture Zones, p.169-247, https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-812064-4.00009-8. This review describes features that would undoubtedly be of interest when considering strike-slip faulting on other planetary bodies
The San Andreas-Gulf of California system connects two plate triple junctions, one south of the Gulf of California at Rivera (R on the middle map) and the other on the north coast of California at Cape Mendocino (M on the middle map). It comprises two parts: (1) the San Andreas strike-slip fault zone, and (2) a set of transform faults/short ridge segments in the Gulf of California (transtension). Estimates of the total strike-slip displacement on this system range from 600 to 1000 km. Currently, the San Andreas Fault Observatory at Depth (SAFOD) programme is drilling deep into the fault zone to make geophysical measurements and to recover rock and fluid samples (http://earthquake.usgs.gov/research/parkfield/safod_pbo.php)

Note that the plate boundary along the California coast changed from being a subduction zone (see 30 Ma map) to a transform fault (all other maps). It is thought that water derived from dehydration of the mantle wedge above the relict subducted slab as it warmed up, migrated upwards into the crust and, by raising pore fluid pressures there, facilitated slip on the faults within the San Andreas system (see Kirby et al., 2014, Earth, Planets and Space 66, article 67, https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-5981-66-67). It also helps to explain why the plate boundary is a broad system of faults (the water was released over a wide area), and why fault-bound and diapiric serpentinite bodies occur within the Californian coastal ranges (water release from deeper in the mantle wedge helped to mobilize serpentinite bodies that originally were contained within the mantle wedge above the dehydration front)

## Slide 11

### Continental transform faults  3

- The Alpine Fault actually has a significant degree of compression acting across it, that is, it is a transpressive strike-slip fault. Hence in addition to 480 km of dextral strike-slip motion over the past 25 myr, there has also been ~20 km of uplift

- Seismic data indicate that the fault is not vertical but listric, and that it extends to a depth of at least 30 km

- Note that by definition (as plate boundaries), transform faults must cut right through the lithosphere

**Notes:**

Current rates of movement on the Alpine Fault are ~20-30 mm/yr strike-slip and locally up to 10 mm/yr uplift (for an examination of slip-rates on the Alpine fault, see Barth et al., 2014, Journal of Structural Geology 64: 39-52, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2013.08.009). It is estimated that about half a million cubic kilometres of rock have been eroded during this uplift, making New Zealand a fantastic place for sedimentologists and geomorphologists

## Slide 12

### Transcurrent faults

- Transcurrent faults are not transfer faults but rather they have free tips and so increase in length as the displacement accumulates

- Such intraplate faults can be 1000s km long and can accommodate 100s km displacement

- Prime examples come from East-Central Asia where there is a complex array of conjugate transcurrent faults – dominantly sinistral in eastern Tibet, and dominantly dextral in an area extending from Lake Baikal to Afghanistan and Pakistan in the west

- This array has been attributed to the indentation and sideways extrusion of Asia in response to the collision of India with Eurasia that has been ongoing throughout the Cenozoic

**Notes:**

For a summary of the key features of transcurrent faults, see Storti et al., 2003, Geological Society of London, Special Publication 210: 1-14, https://doi.org/10.1144/GSL.SP.2003.210.01.01

## Slide 14

### Transcurrent faults  3

- There is some ambiguity about what happens to transcurrent faults at depth. However, it is likely that any that are

- subvertical

- over 300 km long, and

- have an offset of over 30 km

- cut right through the lithosphere

- Post-orogenic lamprophyre dykes a few km NW of the Great Glen Fault were derived from different source rocks compared with those intruded a few km SE of the fault. Since the lamprophyre magmas were generated at depths of over 100 km, this implies that the Great Glen Fault is vertical and extends to at least these depths

**Notes:**

The lamprophyre dykes of north-western Scotland were emplaced at 400±10 Ma, after the Caledonian orogeny. Those north-west of the Great Glen Fault are relatively enriched in light rare earth elements and large ion lithophile elements (notably Ba, Sr and P) compared with those south of the fault, and they have completely different neodymium compositions. These differences require that the source rock (lithospheric mantle) of the lamprophyre magmas north of the Great Glen Fault had previously experienced a series of metasomatic events that the source rock of the lamprophyre magmas south of the Great Glen Fault had not. The mica and clinopyroxene phenocrysts found in the more mafic lamprophyre varieties could only have been in magmatic equilibrium at pressures of 20 kbar (2000 MPa) and temperatures of 1000°-1200°C, which equates to a depth of ~60 km. Consideration of the rare earth element chemistry and the K and Rb composition of the lamprophyres, and what this implies about the residual phases left in the source region when the magmas were formed, extends the estimated depth of formation to over 100 km. Hence the Great Glen Fault must transect the whole Caledonian lithosphere. For details, see Canning et al., 1998, Journal of the Geological Society, London 155: 425-428, https://doi.org/10.1144/gsjgs.155.3.0425
Figure from Storti et al., 2003, Geological Society of London, Special Publication 210: 1-14, https://doi.org/10.1144/GSL.SP.2003.210.01.01

## Slide 17

### Stretching faults

- We tend to think of the two blocks on either side of a fault as being approximately rigid, but when one side is more deformable than the other, the fault marks the boundary between relatively strongly deformed units and relatively undeformed units, and we then have a stretching fault → slip-parallel stretching of one side of the fault when compared with the other

- On a stretching fault the slip is zero at the fault tip and varies along the fault

- This style of deformation may occur on any fault, but it is particularly noteworthy on major strike-slip faults where the slip can vary by up to tens of kilometres along the fault

**Notes:**

The concept of stretching faults was introduced by Win Means (Means, 1989, Geology 17: 893-896, https://doi.org/10.1130/0091-7613(1989)017<0893:SF>2.3.CO;2; Means, 1990, Journal of Structural Geology 12: 267-272, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(90)90010-V). They are effectively surfaces across which there is a displacement velocity discontinuity. For a stretching fault, fault slip and the deformation of the “blocks” either side of the fault are geometrically independent, albeit driven by the same remote loads. Hence recognizing a fault as a stretching fault has significant implications for understanding the development of fabrics and minor structures associated with the fault, e.g., the stretching component may lead to a fault-parallel cleavage (extensional parallel to the fault) or a fault-normal cleavage (contractional parallel to the fault) associated with the deformation of the blocks either side of the fault, instead of/in addition to the fabrics described in the rest of this presentation which are produced by the slip. It also has implications for attempts to model the deformation
For further discussion, illustrated with examples of strike-slip stretching faults, see Rutter & Valetti, 2019, in: Duarte JC (editor), Transform Plate Boundaries and Fracture Zones, p.301-320, https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-812064-4.00012-8

### Simple shear/transpression/transtension

- For a sub-vertical strike-slip fault deforming in simple shear the map is approximately the XZ plane across a shear zone. Hence many of the features observed in strike-slip fault zones can be considered (at least, kinematically) by thinking about the structures we noted when examining shear zones (Class 4)

- In doing so, it is helpful to remember the orientation of the directions that are shortened and those that are extended during progressive simple shear (Class 3)

- But note – real strike-slip faults may involve a combination of simple shear and fault normal shortening (transpression) or fault normal extension (transtension)

**Notes:**

Recall that X is the long axis of the strain ellipsoid and Z is the short axis of the strain ellipsoid, that is, X is the direction of greatest extension and Z is the direction of greatest shortening (see Slide 37 or Class 3 Slide 15). For the orientation of X and Z across a shear zone, see Class 4 Slide 12. On shortening and extension directions during progressive simple shear, see Class 3 Slides 21-23
To the transpression and transtension variants (contraction or extension normal to the fault) we may also add the case where the rocks on either side of the fault deform by different amounts, that is, the stretching fault variant (extension or contraction component parallel to the fault, see Slide 17)

## Slide 20

### Analogue modelling

- Experiments performed on analogue materials have been tremendously helpful for establishing the evolution of structures within strike-slip fault zones and for refining our kinematic intuitions

- The classical experiments are those of Riedel in 1929 who set up two rigid rectangular blocks to represent basement, with a thick tablet of clay on top to represent the cover. By moving the blocks in a strike-slip sense, the traction at the interface between basement and cover produces stresses in the cover and hence it deforms

- This simple experimental set up has been developed to incorporate non-planar faults and materials with different properties (including sands instead of clay), but the essential principles of the experiment remain the same

**Notes:**

For a review of analogue models used to explore the evolution of structures within strike-slip fault zones, see Dooley & Schreurs, 2012, Tectonophysics 574-575: 1-71, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tecto.2012.05.030

### Associated fractures  2

- Other important secondary fractures that are associated with strike-slip faults are:

- P-shears: synthetic to the main fault

- Y-shears: parallel to the main fault

- T-fractures: extension fractures (tension gashes) that bisect the angle between the Riedel shears (i.e., they are parallel to σ1)

## Slide 25

### Fault bends and stepovers

- Although strike-slip faults are characteristically straight, they do contain bends (curved parts of a continuous fault trace connecting two parallel segments) and stepovers (overlapping en échelon fault segments)

- A bend or stepover is restraining if the rocks at the bend/stepover are compressed during slip on the fault, and releasing if they are pulled apart

- At bends and stepovers (and indeed along any broad curve in a strike-slip fault) the displacement on the fault cannot be purely strike-slip – in these regions additional processes are required to accommodate the deformation

**Notes:**

Releasing stepovers and restraining stepovers are more commonly described as extensional transfer zones and contractional transfer zones, respectively (see Class 7, Slide 42)
Figure by Steve Covey-Crump

## Slide 26

### Releasing bends/stepovers

- Slip at releasing bends and stepovers leads to extension fractures and, at larger scale, normal faults that may be arranged in map view as an extensional duplex

- The normal faults in such structures generate pull-apart basins that are commonly rhomb-shaped, and can be up to 10s km across

- In vertical section extensional duplexes widen towards the surface to create a flower structure. Extensional duplexes produce negative flower structures in which the component faults tend to be listric and the central part is structurally lowered

## Slide 37

### Pure strike-slip and the strain ellipsoid

- An understanding of strain in strike-slip fault zones is needed to establish why foliations and lineations in their fault rocks are oriented as they are, and this allows inferences to be made about the movement history + amount of transpression/transtension ± stretching

- Recall the X, Y, Z nomenclature for the axes of the strain ellipsoid: usually the foliation is the XY plane and any stretching lineation is the X direction

- For a vertical strike-slip fault deforming in simple shear (no transpression or transtension) we expect the foliation (XY plane) to be vertical and oblique to the margins of the fault zone, and the stretching lineation (X) to be horizontal

**Notes:**

Note that the foliation is approximately in the orientation of P-shears (see Slide 22). For a vertical strike-slip fault imagine turning the figure of the shear zone on the left so that the shear plane is vertical. The XZ plane is then horizontal which means that X is horizontal and the XY plane is vertical
In the following two slides we compare the fabrics formed in a pure strike-slip fault zone with those in transpressional and transtensional fault zones. The complications presented by fault-parallel stretching (see Slide 17) must be independently recognized and analysed

### Transtension, transpression and the strain ellipsoid

- The shape and orientation of the strain ellipsoid is influenced by the extent of transpression or transtension

- How these change with increasing strain may be modelled and illustrated on a Flinn diagram

- Effect of changing the relative amount of pure shear over simple shear (high pure shear = large α ) on the shape of the strain ellipsoid shown on a Flinn diagram

**Notes:**

An outline of the strain analysis required to construct the Flinn diagram shown bottom left is given by Fossen, p.410-413 (divergent strike-slip = transtension; convergent strike-slip = transpression), but is more fully described by Fossen & Tikoff, 1993, Journal of Structural Geology 15: 413-422, https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8141(93)90137-Y. ### Strain partitioning – Carboneras Fault Zone  3

- At still smaller scale

- The deformation within the strongly deformed fault gouge is distributed across each strand; P-foliations, R-, and Y-shears are well represented
