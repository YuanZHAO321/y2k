## Slide 2

### Types of folding

- One of the key reasons for describing the geometry of folds (Class 1) is to place constraints upon how they formed

- There are three basic types of folding:

- Buckling: folding that occurs when layers are loaded parallel to the layering

- Bending: folding that occurs when layers are loaded at a high angle to the layering

- Passive folding: folding of marker bands that cross or are contained within a deforming unit

- The focus today is primarily on buckling – there will be some brief remarks about folds formed by bending and about passive folding, but proper discussion of them will be deferred to the classes on tectonic regimes (Classes 8-10) and shear zones (Class 4)

**Notes:**

For a review of the sort of information that can be recovered from analysing fold geometry, see Hudleston & Treagus, 2010, Journal of Structural Geology 32: 2042-2071, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2010.08.011. This is rather technical for 2nd year level, but it provides an insight into what research level structural geology can involve
Buckling is the flexing or folding of a surface by a compressive stress directed along that surface; the folds develop as the amplification (growth) of a mechanical instability (‘active folding’)
Bending of a surface arises when non-uniform forces are applied across that surface (the term ‘forced folding’ is sometimes used)
Passive folding is purely a kinematic effect – it describes the shape of a marker line that passes across a deforming material and is carried along in the flow

## Slide 3

### Buckling  (introductory remarks)

- Buckling = folding of a surface by a compressive stress directed along that surface  →  requires

- the folded layer to be more competent (greater viscosity) than the interlayers (matrix)

- small irregularities (mechanical instabilities) to be present on the layer interfaces – these serve as nucleation sites for the folds

- The key variables that control the geometry of buckle folds have been identified from laboratory experiments on analogue materials and from numerical modelling

- In laboratory and numerical work, the following are considered separately:

- Buckling of single layers

- Buckling of multilayers

- Buckling of mechanically anisotropic units

**Notes:**

Viscosity is a measure of the resistance of a fluid to shear. Low viscosity materials are ‘runny’ whereas high viscosity materials are ‘stiff’ (compare the flow of water with that of syrup). On a geological timescale rocks flow and so can be considered as viscous fluids
Strictly defined, using terms that you will encounter later in the course unit, viscosity is the ratio of shear stress to shear strain-rate. Materials are said to be Newtonian viscous (or linear viscous) if their viscosity does not depend upon the shear force applied. The viscosity of rocks commonly decreases as the applied shear force is increased, that is, rocks are commonly ‘shear-thinning’, non-Newtonian fluids. Materials for which viscosity increases with shear force (‘shear-thickening’, non-Newtonian fluids) are not encountered in geological settings but there are non-geological materials that behave in this way – this is a reason why Einstein was interested in bread dough
The “small irregularities” are the mechanical instabilities around which the folds initiate. Without these there is nothing to kick the folding off
The theory of buckling is beyond the remit of this course – we shall confine ourselves to some of the features that the theory/laboratory experiments/numerical models attempt to explain. The development of buckling theory to describe folding in geological materials really began with the landmark studies of Biot and of Ramberg in the 1960s. For the papers describing these developments, see Hudleston & Treagus, 2010, Journal of Structural Geology 32: 2042-2071, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2010.08.011. An excellent introductory overview is given by Price & Cosgrove (Chapters 11-13)
Figure (from Ramsay & Huber, 1987, p.388): four views of the progressive development of folds in a laboratory experiment (using plasticine) that was designed to investigate buckling of a single competent layer embedded in a homogeneous, less competent matrix

## Slide 4

### Single layer buckling

- Classical buckling theory considers a single layer that has small sinusoidal perturbations of different wavelengths, and attempts to find the perturbation that amplifies faster than all the others

- With some simplifying assumptions, the wavelength λ of the perturbation that amplifies fastest (and hence the dominant wavelength of the fold) is found to be given by

- λ  =  2π d [ η L / ( 6η M ) ]1/3

- where d  is the layer thickness, and η L and η M are the viscosities of the layer and matrix

- It is found that the effect of the folding disappears rapidly away from the folded layer (it is negligible at distances of about one wavelength away)

- The folded layer approximates a Class 1B fold (parallel fold)

**Notes:**

The simplifying assumptions include (a) that the layer and matrix are Newtonian viscous, (b) that the weight of the layer has no effect, and (c) that there is no shortening parallel to the layer that is not accommodated by the buckling (that is, the layer does not get thicker as it is shortened). The analysis is also a two-dimensional one in the profile plane (no movement of material into or out of that plane). More sophisticated models allow these assumptions to be relaxed
A Class 1B fold is a parallel fold – the orthogonal thickness of the layer remains constant around the fold (see Class 1 Slides 20-21)
Photo (Steve Covey-Crump): a folded layer within Moine metasedimentary units, Ross of Mull, Scotland. Note that the folds in the layer just beneath the penny are not present in the vein at the bottom of the photo, thereby highlighting how rapidly the effect of folding disappears away from the folded layer

## Slide 7

### The exception – folded pinch-and-swell

- If the layer thickens and thins as in pinch-and-swell then deformation is concentrated in the thin parts and it is the spacing of these that controls the fold wavelength, not the layer thickness

- The fold wavelength of a boudinaged layer is not, however, controlled by the width of the boudins

**Notes:**

Folds of this kind form when layers that have previously been extended are buckled. In that situation, recognition of this geometry allows one to appeal to two phases of deformation: (1) a phase of layer-parallel extension followed by (2) folding. As we shall see in the next class, these two phases may not be completely separate events but can be early and late stages of a single progressive deformation event
The effect of pinch-and-swell on folds has been investigated experimentally. The folds form such that the thicker parts of the layer (the ‘swells’) always rotate in the same direction so that they stack up in a fashion that looks superficially like imbrication. For further discussion, see Price & Cosgrove, p.426-428

The quartzite is cross-bedded (younging upwards on the photo) but the point of interest for this class is the shape of the folded layer – note how one limb is much thicker than the other. ### Multilayer buckling  1

- Most natural folding involves multilayers (e.g., sedimentary sequences) and so the influence that individual layers exert on each other during folding needs to be considered

- If the competent (high viscosity) layers are close together then they behave collectively as a thick single layer. This produces long wavelength folds  [effective single layer]

- If the competent layers are a little further apart and the layer thickness and spacing are not too variable, then the equation for single layer buckling is modified to

- λ  =  2π d [ Nη L / ( 6η M ) ]1/3

- where  N  is the number of competent layers in the package  [harmonic folding]

## Slide 11

### Multilayer buckling  (accommodation structures  1)

- The resistance to buckling is proportional to layer thickness cubed and so anomalously thick layers control the buckling response of the whole multilayer  →  the fold wavelength of the multilayer is given by that of the thick layer, and all other layers adjust to its shape

- This, and the ptygmatic folding case, are examples of a more general situation in which layers with thickness and/or viscosity characteristics that mean that they start to fold early and come to have limbs that are too long or short for the ultimate fold shape, are forced to adjust when the controlling layer starts to buckle

- The structures that form to allow this adjustment are described as accommodation structures

- Keel-like hinge (hinge-collapse)

**Notes:**

The photo on this slide shows a relatively thick layer that started to fold early but then subsequently had to adjust its shape to conform to the fold shape of the whole multilayer. It did so by forming a keel-like hinge. Note that on the slide I say that the shape of thin layers generally needs to adjust to accommodate the shape of some thick, controlling layer. The case shown in the photo is the reverse situation (shape of a thick layer was controlled by the shape of a package of thin layers) - this arises when chevron folds form (chevron folds are folds with ‘sharp’ hinges, see Class 1 Slide 18) because in chevron folds the dip of the limbs for a given amount of shortening depends upon layer thickness (see Price & Cosgrove, p.319ff, or Ramsay and Huber, vol.2, p.422ff)

## Slide 13

### Buckling anisotropic rocks  1

- Modelling the buckling behaviour of multilayers may be carried out by treating the individual layers as separate entities and by then predicting how the layers interact when the multilayer is compressed along the layering (the perspective of the last few slides)

- Alternatively, one can model buckling by treating the multilayer as a statistically homogeneous but mechanically anisotropic material

- In this case, by anisotropic we mean that the resistance to shearing is different to the resistance to compression

- Materials that are classically anisotropic in this way are thinly-bedded multilayers or rocks with a strongly developed penetrative fabric such as a cleavage (e.g., slates)

- The modelling proceeds by finding the condition for the appearance of an instability that interrupts uniform shortening and leads to the layering becoming non-planar

**Notes:**

A helpful introduction to the buckling of anisotropic rocks is given by Price & Cosgrove, Chapter 13
We will consider more rigorous definitions of terms like compression and shear in Class 3
Mathematically, an instability arises when the solution to an equation is very sensitive to the values of the input parameters, e.g., if the solution to an equation using the value x + x where x is very small, is very different to the solution using the value x

## Slide 21

### The life and times of a buckle fold  3

- Fold initiation: some irregularity in the layer (e.g., some local variation in layer thickness) initiates the onset of folding

- If the competent layers are far enough apart, buckling starts in the different layers independently, until some thick control layer starts to buckle and forces the other layers to readjust their shape (thereby forming accommodation structures)

- Alternatively, if there is no control layer, the multilayer buckles as a whole

- Fold amplification and locking up: as the fold grows, the layers rotate with respect to the compression direction, and ultimately

- become unfavourably oriented for further buckling →  fold locks up

- Post-buckle flattening: continued shortening leads to passive growth of the fold – the fold increases in amplitude (and the interlimb angle decreases) as the whole matrix + layer package thickens

**Notes:**

Locking up occurs prematurely if the deforming rocks become stronger as deformation proceeds. This may arise if rock strength increases with deformation or if something favouring deformation (e.g., pore fluids in the rock) is removed. These points highlight the importance of knowing what processes are actually going on within the rock to accommodate the deformation

- Post-buckle flattening changes the geometry of a buckle fold from Class 1B to Class 1C; dip isogon analysis allows the amount of flattening to be evaluated

- Compression parallel to the axial plane of a Class 1B fold that was formed by buckling changes it into a Class 1A fold

### Passive folding

- Passive folding is said to occur when the folded layer is a marker band that simply records the deformation of the unit in which it is contained

- Good examples occur where layers enter shear zones

- If the folded layer was originally parallel-sided, passive folding produces Class 2 similar folds

## Slide 25

### Folding mechanisms

- Our discussion of folding types (buckling, bending, passive) has not considered the mechanisms by which folding occurs. Folding mechanisms are of three kinds

- Flexural flow/flexural slip and tangential longitudinal strain produce Class 1B folds, and are the folding mechanisms that apply for buckling and bending

- Shear folding produces Class 2 folds. In contrast to the other mechanisms, it is not believed to be a real folding mechanism, but it does serve as an extremely useful approximation in the analysis of Class 2 folds

## Slide 26

### Folding mechanisms  (flexural slip/flexural flow)

- Flexural slip is like compressing a ream of paper parallel to the sheets – individual sheets slide over each other

- Maximum slip is at the inflection points and decreases towards the hinge where it is zero

- There is no distortion within the plane of the layering

- The slip planes are usually bedding or some penetrative fabric such as cleavage

- Flexural flow is when the slip planes are close together so that the layer-parallel shear is uniformly distributed across the folded layer; flexural slip is when the slip planes are more widely spaced (e.g., slip occurs on bedding surfaces in an interbedded succession)

- Secondary features of flexural slip folds are:

- Lineations on the slip plane (grooves or more commonly crystal fibres) oriented at a high angle to the hinge (slickolites)

- En échelon tension gashes indicating the sense of slip

**Notes:**

The absence of distortion within the layering means that fossils and other objects lying in the slip plane remain undeformed. This is not true for other folding mechanisms
Slickolites are asymmetric stylolites (see Class 6)
The significance of the tension gashes (and the following points in this bullet) will be appreciated after Class 4. On the figure bottom left, note that on one limb they point one way and on the other limb the other way. This is consistent with the different sense of shear on each limb during flexural slip folding (dextral on the limb left of the antiform hinge, and sinistral on the limb right of the antiform hinge). Note that their sigmoidal form also becomes more pronounced away from the hinge, indicating increasing slip displacement as one moves towards the inflection point

### Folding mechanisms  (flexural slip/flexural flow)

- One of the characteristic features of flexural flow folding derives from the fact that there is no distortion within the slip planes (e.g., bedding planes):

- a pre-existing lineation on the bedding plane making an angle α  to the future fold axis, remains at angle α  to the fold axis after folding

- Hence on a stereogram, folding spreads the lineation along a small circle about the fold axis

**Notes:**

Figure redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Ramsay, 1967, Folding and Fracturing of Rocks, p.463 and p.466. The diagram on the bottom right side of the figure shows what happens if there is some post-buckle flattening

## Slide 28

### Folding mechanisms  (tangential longitudinal strain)

- Tangential Longitudinal Strain (TLS) folding is like compressing an eraser parallel to its length: the outer arc of the layer experiences extension and the inner arc experiences compression; between them is the neutral surface where the deformation cancels out

- The maximum deformation is at the hinge and decreases towards the inflection points where it is zero

- Secondary features of TLS folds include extension fractures in the outer arc, and intensified cleavage or  conjugate thrusts in the inner arc

**Notes:**

Figure top right redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Ramsay, 1967, Folding and Fracturing of Rocks, p.398. The black shapes are strain ellipses (discussed in Class 3) – they show the shape that an initially circular object deforms into
Figure bottom right from Price & Cosgrove, p.251

## Slide 29

### Folding mechanisms  (tangential longitudinal strain)

- The influence of tangential longitudinal strain folding on pre-existing lineations is more complex than in the flexural flow case; on a stereogram the lineations are spread within a sector, their position within that sector depending upon where within the folded layer they occur

**Notes:**

Figure redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Ramsay, 1967, Folding and Fracturing of Rocks, p.464

## Slide 30

### Folding mechanisms  (shear folding)

- Shear folding is like shearing a deck of cards: the shear planes are parallel to the axial surface of the fold

- The influence of shear folding on a pre-existing lineation is to reorient it within a plane, and so on a stereogram the lineation is spread along a great circle. The intersection of this great circle with the shear plane (axial plane) is the tectonic a-direction. If the folding is caused by shearing on a fault, this is the orientation of the fault slip vector

**Notes:**

Shear folding is frequently the best approximation for folds found in fault zones (shear zones). The axial plane of such folds is parallel to the fault plane (shear zone boundary). Hence measuring the plunge/plunge direction of a lineation as it is deformed into a fault zone and then finding where the great circle containing these lineations intersects the great circle for the fault plane, allows one to determine the orientation of the fault slip vector. This is a very helpful technique and will be a major focus of the exercises in more than one of our practical classes. Anyone undertaking geological mapping in an area containing significant faults, ought to become very familiar with this technique
Figure redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Ramsay, 1967, Folding and Fracturing of Rocks, p.470

## Slide 31

- Non-cylindrical folds

**Notes:**

Most of the discussion over Classes 1 and 2 has been framed in terms of cylindrical folds, that is, folds that have the same shape on all cross-sections that are drawn in the same orientation but at different locations along the fold axis. ## Slide 33

### Sheath folds

- Folds with hinge lines that curve through more than 90° within the axial surface are described as sheath folds

- They are commonly associated with a strong stretching lineation

- Sheath folds form by passive amplification of some initial irregularity within a layer that is subject to layer-parallel shear

- They are a feature of shear zones and so we will return to them in Class 4

**Notes:**

In the extreme case sheath folds take on a tubular shape. Hence the old French name for them – condom folds
Sheath folds have curved hinge lines with an axial surface that is approximately planar. Folds can arise that have straight hinge lines and curved axial planes – these are called polyclinal folds (see notes to Slide 39). Of course, we may also have folds with curved hinge-lines and curved axial planes, and this is commonly the case for soft-sediment folds (see next slide)

### Soft sedimentary folds

- Soft sediment deformation generally produces highly non-cylindrical folds because unlithified and water-saturated sediment is weak enough for it to respond to small changes in the direction of the forces driving the deformation (e.g., as it encounters obstacles to its motion)

**Notes:**

## Slide 35

### Refolded folds

- Folds generated by two (or more) phases of deformation are non-cylindrical except for the special case where the folding events are coaxial and coplanar

**Notes:**

The geometry of refolded folds may be unravelled using the congruent minor structures associated with each folding event because although a second folding event may change the geometry of first folds, it cannot change the asymmetry of its associated minor structures

## Slide 36

### Refolded folds (fold interference patterns)

- The outcrop patterns of refolded folds have been classified into four basic fold interference patterns according to the orientation of the fold axes and the axial planes of the two phases of folding relative to each other

- Type 0 is the coaxial and coplanar case – the 2nd phase of folding merely intensifies the 1st fold structure

- Visualization requires considerable mental dexterity

**Notes:**

Nomenclature: F1, F2 refer to folds from the first deformation event and second deformation event respectively; S1 and S2 refer to the axial planes of the F1 and F2 folds respectively (see Class 1 Slide 35)
Not everyone finds it easy to visualize fold interference patterns. To assist the process, the Fault Analysis Group at University College Dublin have generated some paper models to illustrate the geometry (see www.fault-analysis-group.ucd.ie, use the “Educational Material” tab and go to “Superposed Folds”). ### Refolded folds  (Type 2: dome-crescent-mushroom)

- In Type 2 refolded folds, the fold axes are at 90° to each other, but one set of folds is recumbent and the other is upright. The result is a characteristic crescent or mushroom-shaped outcrop pattern

## Slide 39

### Refolded folds  (Type 3: convergent-divergent)

- In Type 3 refolded folds, the fold axes are approximately coaxial, but one set of folds is recumbent and the other is upright. The result is a wavy form of outcrop pattern

**Notes:**

In Type 3 refolded folds the F1 and F2 fold axes are in approximately the same direction (that’s what coaxial means). However, the axial surfaces for the two sets of folds (S1 and S2) are very different. Hence the end result is approximately straight F1 and curved S1. This is a polyclinal fold (see notes to Slide 33). Polyclinal folds may actually be developed in one single deformation event, that is, the fact that we have a Type 3 fold interference pattern does not automatically mean that we have two folding events. It is not easy to get one’s head around how Type 3 fold interference patterns can be generated in a single folding event but an attempt to show that this is so is described by Carreras & Druguet, 2019, Journal of Structural Geology 125, 195-201, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2018.07.015
Figures redrawn by Steve Covey-Crump from Ramsay, 1967, Folding and Fracturing of Rocks, p.533 and p.531. The top right figure is annotated by Steve Covey-Crump
