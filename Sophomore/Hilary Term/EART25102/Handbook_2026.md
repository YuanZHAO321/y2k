## **Course introduction**

The EART25102 field course to Scotland comprises two parts:

- (a) further training in mapping geological structures – in this case multiply-deformed, moderatelymetamorphosed units at Kinlochleven and Cuil Bay on the north-western coast of Scotland;

- (b) development of field data collection techniques used for sedimentary and igneous units – in this case, Caledonian igneous units at Glen Coe and Kentallen, and Carboniferous sedimentary and Permian igneous units exposed on the coast of Fife, eastern Scotland.

This guide covers the Kinlochleven, Cuil Bay, and Glen Coe/Kentallen part of the field course. The _Geological Background_ section is simply for those who wish to place what they are mapping in geological context but is entirely optional reading; the rest of the field guide may be useful in completing the work.

Our primary aims in this part of the field course (6-field days) are focused on (not in this order):

- (1) producing a geological map of an area 2-3 km<sup>2</sup> in size located on the coast at Cuil Bay – the mapping area is primarily on one limb of a significant fold, has an intrusive igneous body, and has minor structures associated with a later deformation event (3 days);

- (2) illustrating the geometry of folds that crop out along the coast near Kinlochleven by means of a profile plane cross-section with accompanying stereogram constructed from map data (2 days);

- (3) producing a compass-and-pace map (on a blank piece of paper) of a small area (~100 m<sup>2</sup> ) in Glen Coe that contains numerous dykes intruding andesitic and rhyolitic country rock (½ day)

- (4) recording notebook observations of the igneous and metamorphic units seen at Kentallen – these will include both hand specimen descriptions and descriptions of field relations (½ day).

This is an area that is structurally rather complex, but almost all this complexity lies in the interpretation of the structures – completing the exercises is relatively straightforward provided the task of collecting and recording observations is undertaken systematically.

Some of the days will be staff-led – we will show you key localities and provide training in the mapping skills. At other times, however, you will be mapping in pairs or threes – when you are doing this the staff will be doing their own mapping within the mapping area but will be on-hand to provide some guidance if needed. Throughout we will be emphasizing observational/recording skills not skills of interpretation.

## **Geological  Background**

The basement geology of Scotland comprises an assemblage of fault-bound crustal blocks. These blocks were juxtaposed (with subsequent readjustments) during a series of Ordovician to Devonian orogenic events that were associated with the formation and closure of oceans that lay between the continents of Laurentia (to the northwest), Baltica (to the east) and Avalonia (to the south-east). These orogenic events are known collectively as the Caledonian Orogeny.

The rocks that we will be examining on this field course belong to the Central Highlands (Grampian) terrane, which is bounded to the north-west by the Great Glen Fault, and to the south-east by the Highland Boundary Fault.

#### **Dalradian Supergroup**

#### **_Plate tectonic context_**

At ~900 Ma most of the Earth’s land masses were amalgamated in a supercontinent (Rodinia). Subsequently, subduction zones and accretionary orogens developed around the periphery of this supercontinent, while at the same time the continental interior underwent multistage rifting events. The rifting ultimately led to the break-up of Rodinia during the second half of the Neoproterozoic.

The protoliths of the older parts of the metasedimentary Dalradian Supergroup were deposited in extensional basins formed within Rodinia as it underwent rifting. At ~600 Ma this rifting developed into the opening of the Iapetus Ocean, that separated Laurentia to the north-west from Gondwana to the south-east. The younger parts of the Dalradian sequence were deposited on the Laurentian passive margin during the latest Neoproterozoic and early Palaeozoic as Iapetus Ocean opened and then began to close.

#### **_Stratigraphy_**

The Dalradian metasediments are a sequence of marine sands, silts, muds, and limestones that were deposited on a basement of older metamorphic rocks (the Badenoch Group, formerly known as the ‘Central Highland Migmatite Complex’). Dalradian sedimentation post-dates the deformation and mid-amphibolite facies metamorphism of the Badenoch Group that occurred at ~840 Ma, and probably began ~730-700 Ma, at about the same time as the onset of the Sturtian ‘Snowball Earth’ glaciation. The total thickness of the succession was at least ~25 km but the complete vertical thickness was never deposited at one place – rather the depocentre migrated south-eastwards with time as the basin deepened with prolonged crustal stretching.

The Dalradian sequence is subdivided into the Grampian, Appin, Argyll, and Southern Highland Groups. The **_Grampian Group_** is composed primarily of micaceous to quartzose psammites and semi-pelites. These record the initiation of extension, followed by a period of rapid rifting and basin deepening (associated with turbidites), and then shallowing as the basin filled during the transition from active rifting to a more gradual thermally-induced basin subsidence. The **_Appin Group_** comprises quartzites, limestones, semi-pelites and pelites which were deposited on a stable marine shelf. Cyclical alternations of quartzites and muddy sediments are thought to correspond with repeated episodes of rifting and thermal subsidence. Towards the top of the group, limestones and dark mudstones become more prevalent and this is interpreted to indicate a period of low sediment supply during a prolonged phase of thermal subsidence and basin widening. Thickness variations of the units suggest that the Appin Group sediments were deposited in a set of half-grabens. The base of the succeeding **_Argyll Group_** is marked by a tillite (the Port Askaig Tillite). The units immediately above this tillite are shallow water, intertidal, stromatolitic dolomites suggestive of a warm, arid climate. Hence the Port Askaig Tillite has been linked with one of the ‘Snowball Earth’ glaciations (probably the Marinoan glaciation, ~ 645635 Ma). The rest of the Argyll Group succession comprises quartzites (originally thick tidal shelf sands), pebbly psammites (originally fan turbidites), graphitic pelites (originally deep-water organic-rich mudstones), and calc-pelites (originally tidal flat sediments), and appears to record fault-controlled sedimentation. Towards the top of the Argyll Group there are abundant metabasite sills and volcanics (Tayvallich Volcanic Formation) indicative of rift-related basic magmatism. Contemporaneously, the rift-related ‘Vuirich Suite’ of granites were intruded. Together the Tayvallich Volcanics and Vuirich Suite granites mark the onset of the opening of Iapetus Ocean at 605 -590 Ma. The **_Southern Highland Group_** is dominated by feldspathic psammites and hemipelagic mudstones representing steady basin deepening. Near the base of the group there are beds containing dropstones and diamictites (the Macduff Slates) that date to the Gaskiers glaciation (579.8 Ma). At the very top of the Southern Highland Group are deep water sandstones, limestones, slates, cherts and slump breccias, some of which units contain Cambrian trilobites and Early Ordovician conodonts.

_Dalradian units in the mapping areas at Kinlochleven and Cuil Bay (within the Appin Group)_

The Dalradian units at Kinlochleven (Binnein Quartzite, and Binnein Schist) are from the lower part of the Appin Group. The quartzites contain cross-bedding (commonly herring-bone), ripple marks, and erosional features, while occasional mudcracks indicate intermittent emergence. They become less feldspathic towards the north-east, with palaeocurrent evidence indicating NE-SW flow. These units are interpreted as shelf deposits with sediment being supplied from the south-west, the quartzites representing shoaling events in which the deposits were tidally reworked.

At Cuil Bay the units between the Ballachulish Slates and Cuil Bay Slates are from the middle of the Appin Group, and are also interpreted as shelf deposits formed during a period of basin filling between two phases of basin-deepening rifting – the slates are from mudstones deposited when the basin was at its deepest.

#### **Grampian Orogeny**

#### **_Plate tectonic context_**

In the Cambrian and Early Ordovician the Laurentian margin in northern Scotland was a shallow-water carbonate shelf that passed south-eastwards into the deep marine turbidite basins of the Southern Highland Group. Sedimentation was terminated in the Mid-Ordovician by the Grampian Orogeny (478-460 Ma). The Grampian orogenic event was caused by the collision of a volcanic arc with the Laurentian margin. This arc, commonly known as the Taconic Arc and including units now underlying the Midland Valley of Scotland at its eastern end, had developed over an intra-oceanic subduction zone within the Iapetus Ocean. The collision led to the obduction of an ophiolite nappe, together with regional deformation and metamorphism. _~_ To the far south during the Early Ordovician ( 475 Ma), Avalonia, a continental fragment that included southern Britain and Ireland, rifted away from the margins of Gondwana and began migrating northwards. This marked the start of the closure of the Iapetus Ocean and the opening of the Rheic Ocean between Avalonia and Gondwana. At this time, subduction was also occurring along the western margin of Baltica which was rotating anticlockwise and beginning to converge with Laurentia.

#### **_Deformation_**

The Grampian Orogeny affected units in the Northern Highland terrane to the north of the Great Glen Fault as well as units in the Central Highlands (Grampian) terrane. North of the Great Glen Fault it led to widespread NW-directed, ductile thrusting and folding under amphibolite facies metamorphic conditions. South of the Great Glen Fault in the Central Highlands terrane, the Dalradian Supergroup experienced three major ductile deformation events, associated with initial crustal thickening (D1), gravitational spreading and back-thrusting of the thickened pile as it heated up (D2), and differential uplift and exhumation (D3).

D1 involved NW-directed ductile thrusting over distances of at least tens of kilometres, accompanied by NE-SW trending upright folding (F1) on scales of up to tens of kilometres. In micaceous lithologies the folding was associated with the development of an axial-planar, penetrative slaty cleavage (S1) that fans across the Dalradian outcrop from NW facing in the north-west to SE facing in the south-east. These areas are separated by a central zone of upright folding represented by the Loch Awe Syncline and the Geal Charn-Ossian Steep Belt. Associated with D1 is a stretching lineation (L1) which appears as stretched pebble clasts or as a mineral elongation lineation.

D2 involved vertical shortening and SE-directed shearing (back-thrusting). This refolded the earlier folds, forming upright F2 folds in the NW and, through a combination of back-thrusting and extensional collapse of the orogenic belt, a major recumbent fold (the Tay Nappe) to the SE. The associated foliation is typically a crenulation cleavage (S2), but in places this has completely transposed the earlier S1 fabric. The usual lineation is a crenulation (L2) formed by the hinges of F2 micro-folds of the S1 cleavage.

D3 was a series of late, low-strain events, the net large-scale effect of which was to rotate D1 and D2 structures locally into steep attitudes – the present-day distribution of ‘flat belts’ and ‘steep belts’ in the Dalradian is largely the result of D3. D3 produced large-scale upright folds but did not generally produce penetrative fabrics. Gentle crenulations are the most common small-scale D3 structures.

#### **_Metamorphism and Grampian Orogeny intrusions_**

Over much of the Dalradian outcrop the regional metamorphism during the Grampian Orogeny is represented by the classical Barrovian zonal sequence: chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite, sillimanite and sillimanite + K-feldspar. The metamorphic grade increases from greenschist facies in the south-west to mid-amphibolite facies in the north-east. Pressures of 900 -1200 MPa corresponding with depths of burial of 25-35 km are widely found over the South-Western and Central Highlands but these decrease markedly to ~300 MPa (10 km) in the north-east.

The Dalradian units of the north-east were intruded, ~ 470 Ma, by a suite of gabbros (the ‘Newer Gabbros’). Locally the contact aureoles of these overprint the peak regional metamorphic assemblages prior to their involvement in D3. At about the same time, or very shortly afterwards, but before D3, S-type granites (the ‘Grampian Granites’) derived from partial melting of the Dalradian metasediments were intruded into the northeastern part of the Dalradian outcrop.

The thermal peak of the regional metamorphism associated with the Grampian Orogeny occurred after D1 since porphyroblasts overgrow S1. At many locations the porphyroblasts exhibit S-shaped inclusion trails which imply that they grew during D2, and hence that the thermal peak was during or shortly after D2. The chemical zonation of the garnets suggests that they grew as pressure was decreasing, which is consistent with the suggestion that D2, at least in part, represents extensional collapse of an over-thickened (during D1) crust as it heated up. D3 is associated with the development of retrograde minerals and so occurred after the metamorphic peak but before the intrusion of an undeformed pegmatite that has been dated at 462 Ma.

#### **Late to post-Grampian Orogeny events**

The presence at the surface today of Dalradian rocks that were metamorphosed at depths of 35 km, coupled with _~_ the observation that the crust in the Grampian Highlands is currently 35 km thick, implies that at peak _~ ~_ metamorphic conditions ( 470 Ma) the thickness of the Grampian crust was 70 km. Barrovian high-grade garnet detritus is found in mid-Llanvirn (465 Ma) conglomerates to the south of Glasgow, which implies that within 5 million years of peak metamorphism, crust metamorphosed at 35 km depth had reached the surface. This requires local erosion rates of about 7 mm yr<sup>-1</sup> , that is, erosion-rates similar to those in the Nanga Parbat region of the modern Himalaya. The implication is that during the Grampian Orogeny, the D1 crustal thickening phase generated high mountains, and that fast erosion, possibly assisted by extensional collapse during D2, led to rapid exhumation.

_~_ By 455 Ma, in the Southern Uplands terrane to the south, an accretionary prism had formed indicating that by then northward subduction under Laurentia had been initiated. In the Central Highlands terrane at _~_ 455 Ma a suite of post-tectonic S-type granitic intrusions were emplaced. These are interpreted as the products of decompression melting of the over-thickened Grampian crust as it continued to be eroded and exhumed. There is no record of igneous activity in the Central Highlands terrane in period 455-430 Ma. When _~_ igneous activity began again 430 Ma it was as subduction was ceasing. The lack of evidence for active subduction-related igneous activity in this 25 myr interval is attributed to shallow angle subduction under Laurentia, or to the removal of the evidence by the extreme rates of uplift and erosion at this time.

#### **Convergence of Laurentia with Baltica-Avalonia and the final closure of Iapetus**

#### **_Plate tectonic context_**

Southwards subduction of Iapetus Ocean under Avalonia ceased _~_ 450 Ma and from then on Avalonia was brought closer to Laurentia ( _i.e._ , the Iapetus Ocean was narrowed) by continued northwards subduction under _~_ the Southern Uplands accretionary prism. By 430 Ma the ocean was effectively closed and the Lake District on the northern edge of Avalonia had become the site of a foreland basin. Further subduction of Avalonia under _~_ Laurentia was resisted by buoyancy constraints: by 426 Ma subduction had slowed and by 423 Ma orthogonal shortening of the Southern Uplands accretionary prism had switched to sinistral transpression because the collision between Laurentia and Avalonia was oblique and not ‘head-on’.

This _~_ collision was underway by 435 Ma and led to the Scandian Orogeny ( 435-425 Ma) during which episode large scale thrust and nappe deformation occurred in Scandinavia, Greenland, and in north-western Scotland (it was during this event that the major thrust systems in the Northern Highlands terrane, namely the Sgurr Beag, Navier and Moine thrusts, were activated). The outcome of the mutual collision of these three plates was the initiation of substantial sinistral strike-slip movement on the Great Glen Fault. The movement on the Great Glen _~_ Fault, which was perhaps 700 km+, occurred over the period 428 -410 Ma and resulted in the juxtaposition of the Scandian-deformed Northern Highlands terrane with the Central Highlands terrane which shows no Scandian tectonic overprint.

#### **_Silurian to Early Devonian igneous activity_**

From _~_ 430 Ma, as subduction of Iapetus under Laurentia slowed and ceased, there began a period of I-type granite emplacement in the Grampian and Northern Highland terranes which lasted at least 22 million years. These ‘Newer Granites’ were closely associated with hydrous alkali-rich rocks (appinites and lamprophyres). It is presently considered that this period of igneous activity followed failure (break-off) of the subducted slab. The idea is that slab break-off led to a linear domain of mantle upwelling through the slab break. The heating induced by the upwelling hot asthenopheric mantle led to partial melting (at depths of 100-200 km) of previously metasomatized subcontinental lithospheric mantle that lay above the slab break, thereby generating alkali-rich, hydrous appinite magmas. Rapid uplift, thermal weakening, and focusing of deformation along this linear domain may have helped to fix the location of the lithosphere-penetrating Great Glen Fault itself, and this fault then provided an easy upward pathway for the magmas. During their ascent, some of the magmas underwent fractional crystallization to produce alkali-rich intermediate and silicic magmas that were emplaced in the upper crust north-west of the Great Glen Fault. However, the chemistry of most of the Newer Granites cannot be explained by fractional crystallization from appinitic magmas but rather it is inferred that the volatiles and heat that accompanied the rising appinitic magmas promoted partial melting of mafic and intermediate lower crust, thereby generating large volumes of dioritic magma. These dioritic magmas then ascended to their level of buoyancy ( _~_ 10 km depth) where they were modified by _in situ_ fractionation and country rock assimilation to produce the Newer Granites that crop out today. Large volcanic edifices, mostly since eroded away, were formed above these intrusions, with the plumbing systems for these volcanic edifices being represented today by swarms of dykes.

Rapid uplift caused by high heat flow meant that later igneous complexes were emplaced into the deeper levels of older ones. The resulting cross-cutting relationships between the volcanics, hyperabyssal intrusions, and plutons are particularly well displayed immediately to the east of the Great Glen Fault.

Many of these igneous features (the appinites, Newer Granites, volcanic edifices) are exposed close to the mapping area. We will not visit them on this field course but we see several NE-SW trending dykes that belong to the Etive dyke swarm. These form part of the plumbing system of one or more of these volcanic edifices, now eroded away.

#### _Emplacement of the Silurian-Devonian plutons_

Many of the Newer Granite plutons have well developed thermal aureoles, with contact metamorphism clearly overprinting regional metamorphism. The inner aureole rocks contain mineral parageneses of pyroxene-hornfels facies ( _e.g_ ., cordierite + andalusite + biotite + K-feldspar + quartz / corundum in pelites), indicative of _~_ emplacement in the upper crust ( 8-10 km).

The plutons are approximately aligned in NE-trending arrays along major strike-slip faults or lineaments. It has been suggested that high-angle NE-SW trending Caledonian shear zones and high-angle NW-SE trending pre-Caledonian lineaments effectively compartmentalized the lower crust into a set of blocks that rotated relative to one another during sinistral strike-slip movement on the Great Glen and Highland Boundary faults. As these blocks rotated, transtensional zones were set up at the intersections of the two sets of lineaments, locally facilitating the ascent of magma in a series of steep sheets. Assembly of the plutons in the upper crust then resulted from rotational ballooning.

#### **Quaternary**

Scotland was heavily glaciated in the Quaternary but little evidence remains on land for the numerous glacial episodes that occurred before the last one. From _~_ 26 ka glaciers formed in the Scottish mountains and amalgamated to form a large ice sheet. Glen Coe and Loch Leven formed major east to west routes for the ice. After 18 ka the ice was in retreat, albeit a retreat punctuated by several re-advances of varying magnitude. There was a sustained warm period (the Windermere interstadial), 14.7-12.9 ka, although the ice may not have completely disappeared at this time. This was terminated by a major climatic reverse (the _Loch Lomond stadial_ in Scotland but known to the rest of the world as the _Younger Dryas_ ) when a thick ice cap formed

just east of Glen Coe on Rannoch Moor. Ice flowed westwards down Glen Coe and Loch Leven but did not overtop the peaks lining these valleys. This cold period ended abruptly 11.7 ka. Rannoch Moor was ice-free within a few hundred years, and since then slope processes have shaped the landscape.

#### **_Deformation events recognizable in the Kinlochleven area_**

#### _D1 structures_

D1 is responsible for the principal younging confrontations observed across the mapping area – these help to locate a number of mappable F1 axial traces. S1 is a penetrative micaceous cleavage that is everywhere nearly bedding-parallel. It is invariably present in relatively thick pelitic units, and is easily seen in the cores of F2 folds where S2 clearly crenulates it. However, in thin pelitic units it is overprinted by S2, and in localities where S2 is also nearly bedding-parallel (which is common in the mapping area), careful observation is required to distinguish it from S2. S1 is not generally visible in the more quartzitic lithologies. In thin section S1 appears as a strong preferred orientation of muscovite and biotite and of the enclosed quartz and feldspar grains. Minor F1 folds are recognizable as F1 where they are clearly refolded folds, where the dominant cleavage (S2) transects them, or where S2/S0 has a different asymmetry to that of “its” fold. It is challenging to make reliable observations of S1/S0 asymmetry.

#### _D2 structures_

The mapping area lies between two major D2 folds – the Stob Bhan synform to the west and the Kinlochleven antiform to the east. There are no mappable F2 folds in the main part of the mapping area – indeed, in the mapping area, D2 is essentially just a near-bedding-parallel shearing deformation. This shearing is locally intense, particularly in the pelitic lithologies close to the contacts with more quartzitic units, where it is responsible for small scale F2 folds that have a strongly developed axial planar crenulation cleavage and variable plunge.

D2 is responsible for the dominant cleavage (S2) in the sandy pelites to more quartzitic lithologies in the mapping area. S2 is a closely spaced (typically ~ 1 mm) crenulation or composite fabric (a transposition fabric in zones of high D2 strain) that has the appearance of being formed at peak metamorphic conditions, and which is usually steeply dipping and trending NE-SW to N-S. In thin sections of semi-pelitic lithologies it clearly deforms S1 in intense crenulations with recrystallization of the mica on the S2 planes. In quartzitic lithologies it is a strong grain shape fabric. S2 locally contains a mineral lineation (rodding of quartz and feldspar, elongate rusty spots). Minor F2 folds are recognizable as F2 because S2 is axial planar to them. It is difficult to do much with observations of S2/S0 asymmetry but it is worth noting whether the strike of S2 is clockwise or anticlockwise to that of S1.

The growth of biotite on S2 and the appearance of porphyroblasts around which S2 is deflected, both indicate that D2 occurred at about the same time as the metamorphic peak. Garnet porphyroblasts occur locally in all pelitic lithologies, and microcline porphyroblasts in the Binnein and Eilde Schists. The porphyroblasts contain inclusion trails (quartz, feldspar, mica) representing the S1 fabric; in the garnet these are strongly curved while in the microcline they are less so, the implication being that the microcline porphyroblasts grew early in D2 while the garnet porphyroblasts continued to grow well into D2.

#### _D3 structures_

D3 is responsible for ENE-WSW trending, upright folds that cause the main swing in S0 (and S1 and S2) across the mapping area. The cores of F3 folds have a well-developed, spaced (5-10 mm) axial planar crenulation (S3) but S3 is generally weakly developed elsewhere. In thin section, S3 is seen to result from the alignment of mica on the limbs of microfolds of S1 or S2 without new mica growth, that is, it postdates peak metamorphic conditions.

#### _D4 structures_

D4 is represented by a sporadically developed, N-S trending, steeply dipping crenulation (S4). It is spaced with no new mineral growth. There are no major D4 structures.

**_Rule of thumb:_** _relatively high metamorphic grade, tightly spaced fabrics and small-scale structures are D2; lower grade, spaced crenulations and more open folds are D3. D1 minor structures are only recognizable locally, although F1 fold axial traces are mappable from younging information._

#### **Ballachulish Igneous Complex**

The Ballachulish Igneous Complex was emplaced 427 ± 1 Ma (U-Pb dating of zircon) making it one of the earliest of the Newer Granites. The complex comprises a zoned monzodiorite-quartz diorite envelope with flow- and deformation-fabrics, that surrounds a core of variably porphyritic granite. The internal contacts between the granite and quartz-diorite are mostly hybrid, and so it is inferred that the granite was emplaced whilst the older quartz-diorite envelope was still partially molten.

The contacts with the surrounding metasediments are mostly sharp, discordant, and steeply outward dipping. The intrusive complex is approximately cylindrical in shape to a depth of ~4 km below the present land surface. The lack of intrusion-related deformation features in the marginal country rocks, together with the presence of large blocks and a high density of xenoliths in the marginal parts of the intrusion, imply that the

intrusion was emplaced by stoping and block faulting. From the mineralogical composition of the thermal aureole (see below) the emplacement depth is estimated to be ~10 km.

The major element chemistry identifies the monzodiorites as metaluminous (Na2O + K2O < Al2O3 < CaO + Na2O + K2O) I-type granites, the quartz-diorites as transitional to peraluminous (Al2O3 > CaO + Na2O + K2O) crustal-derived magmas, and the granites as S-type granites derived from crustal melting. The rare earth element composition of the monzodiorites and granites is similar implying that the geochemical

- 35 -

signature of both was imposed by the source region and is not the result of fractional crystallization of one large magma batch – the interpretation is that the magmas contained a significant component derived from melting of lower crustal intermediate granulites or gneisses. The monzodiorites and quartz diorites have a greater range of differentiation index compared with the granites, and this has been interpreted as indicating that the former underwent greater differentiation/ fractionation whereas the latter crystallized from a crystal mush with limited separation of cumulate phases or intercumulus liquids.

##### **_Intrusion story_**

1. Dry, alkaline, convecting monzodioritic magma was emplaced at a depth of ~ 10 km at ~1100°C to form a small elliptical stock. During cooling and crystallization, a sub-vertical crystal mush zone slowly advanced from the margins inwards. It was this phase of the intrusion that was primarily responsible for the contact metamorphism

2. In response to crystal fractionation, flow differentiation, and assimilation of country rock, a quartz-diorite melt evolved from the monzodioritic magma at 1050°- 900°C. Ongoing regional faulting and block stoping may have led to enlargement of the pluton. Continued fractionation and crystallization led to the accumulation of a residual rhyodacitic magma in the centre of the intrusion

3. At 900°-850°C a granite magma was emplaced. When this reached its buoyancy level, the granite diapir expanded and mixed with the partially consolidated quartz-diorite envelope magmas. With further cooling, hydrous fluids were released at 700°-680°C. Consolidation proceeded inwards leaving a residual, hydrous leucogranite melt, which crystallized rapidly to fine grain size when it suddenly lost volatiles. The thermal event (time above ~ 550°C) lasted ~ 500 kyr

#### **The Kentallen intrusion**

_Map of the Kentallen intrusion (after Platten, 1999, In: Stephenson et al. (editors), Caledonian Igneous Rocks of Great Britain, Geological Conservation Review Series 17, Chapter 8, Site: Kentallen)_

Associated with the Ballachulish Igneous Complex are several small intrusive complexes, collectively known as the Appinite Suite. These comprise ultramafic (peridotite, pyroxenite, hornblendite), mafic to intermediate (olivine monzonite, biotite- and pyroxene-diorite) and subordinate felsic (granodiorite) bodies in association with explosion breccias. The lithologies are texturally and mineralogically highly variable.

Geochemically, the Appinite Suite lithologies have high K and volatile (H2O, CO2, S, Cl) contents, and are enriched in Mg, Ni, Cr, Co, Ba, Sr, and LREE. On chemical variation diagrams, the lithologies clearly lie on a differentiation trend from a primary K-rich, calc-alkaline basaltic magma.

They are interpreted as a suite of plutonic rocks emplaced in sub-volcanic pipes within the upper crust under conditions of high vapour pressures. Their textural variability is attributed to crystallization under highly variable fluid pressures that reflect differences

- a) in the fluid pressure required to breach obstacles during the ascent of the magmas,

- b) the amount of crystallization that had occurred at the time of breaching,

- c) the rate of gas release when breaching occurred

Hence some members of the suite are cumulates, some are the crystallization products of residual liquids, and some are mixtures produced during explosive emplacement.

#### **_The main body of the intrusion_**

The interior of the Kentallen intrusion comprises a melanocratic olivine monzonite (‘kentallenite’). The olivine and clinopyroxene crystals are typically 2-8 mm but rarely the olivine crystals may be up to 20 mm. The crystals are euhedral to subhedral and form a densely packed, nearly crystal-supported fabric. The olivine gives the impression of being corroded, and it is densely charged with magnetite. Plagioclase occurs as small interstitial laths. Phlogopite (bronze in hand specimen) forms large (10-30 mm) crystals that poikilitically enclose the plagioclase and mafics. K-feldspar either overgrows the plagioclase or forms poikilitic crystals. Apatite is a common accessory.

Towards the margin, the phlogopite decreases in grain size (> 10 mm to < 1 mm) and changes from poikilitic to interstitial. The olivine becomes less abundant, the olivine and pyroxene crystals are less densely packed, and the colour index decreases. Some enclaves of a porphyritic kentallenite facies with very fine-grained matrix are present close to the outer margin.

##### **_Intrusion story_**

1. Formation of a volatile-rich, high-K, calc-alkaline basaltic magma by small degree partial melting of phlogopiteperidotite (metasomatized subcontinental lithospheric mantle)

2. Crystallization of olivine at ~1800°C and 25 kbar (70 - 80 km) but resorption of these crystals during ascent of the magmas; rapid magma ascent was facilitated by the deeply penetrating Great Glen Fault

3. Explosive emplacement of magmas at high crustal levels:

   - → formation of the chilled porphyritic kentallenite of the early contact facies (this porphyritic kentallenite is representative of the primary magma)

   - → wall crystallization from the convecting magma produced the pyroxenite and peridotite cumulates of the early contact facies; as the olivine, clinopyroxene and some phlogopite crystallized (1125°- 1000°C), fluid pressures increased leading to crystallization (960°-770°C) of amphibole and further phlogopite; cataclasis of the phlogopite in these cumulates indicates internal deformation whilst these aggregates contained interstitial fluids and were only supported on one side (the wall)

   - → temporary cessation of the convection allowed the crystallization of the pyroxene-olivine diorite of the early contact facies

4. A new batch of more mafic magma was introduced into the pipe, displacing the existing magmas and triggering partial collapse and removal of the wall cumulates and chilled rocks:

   - → emplacement of the marginal intrusions may have occurred during the collapse of the wall cumulates

   - → the main magma body quenched against the walls of the pipe, trapping enclaves of the porphyritic kentallenite and forming the relatively fine-grained outer parts of the main intrusion

   - → settlement and accumulation of olivine and clinopyroxene phenocrysts in the centre of the pipe and crystallization of interstitial liquids led to the formation of typical kentallenite

5. Compaction of the main intrusion expelled some interstitial residual liquids to form biotite-feldspar veins and lamprophyre dykes

6. Subsequent gas streaming led to hydration and carbonation of some of the products of crystallization

#### **Rapid ascent of fluids/melts (mobile hydrofracturing)**

<!-- Start of picture text -->
Principles behind mobile hydrofracturing (after Bons et al.,<br>2012, Journal of Structural Geology 43: 33-62)<br><!-- End of picture text -->

Rapid ascent of fluids from great depths may be achieved by mobile hydrofracturing. When a fracture is filled with stagnant fluid the vertical fluid pressure gradient ( _pfluid_ = _ρfluid g z_ ) is ~10 kPa/m (for H2O) while the lithostatic pressure gradient in the rock ( _plith_ = _ρrock g z_ ) is ~27 kPa/m. Hence if the fluid and lithostatic pressure are in equilibrium at one level in the fracture then the fluid is over-pressured above this level, and under-pressured below it. Above a critical height (~10s of metres) the rock cannot sustain the over-pressure and the crack propagates upwards closing its lower end as it does so – the fracture moves with its containing fluid. The propagation rate is rapid

(~1 ms<sup>-1</sup> ~ 11 km hr<sup>-1</sup> ). The fractures move preferentially up pre-existing planes of weakness, merge, and then ascend as even bigger and faster fluid batches. In this way ascent of volatile-rich magmas across the full crustal thickness is achieved in a few hours without the need to invoke large fluid fluxes.

#### **On lamprophyres**

‘Lamprophyre’ is a field term for hypabyssal rocks of wide-ranging composition but which are usually mesocratic or melanocratic (occasionally ultramafic) and comprise phenocrysts of biotite and/or amphibole in a groundmass of feldspars and/or feldspathoids. Those which are geochemically calc-alkaline (typically, containing no feldspathoids) are classified on the basis of whether they are biotite- or amphibole-bearing and on the relative proportions of plagioclase and K-feldspar:

||_biotite_|_hornblende_|
|---|---|---|
|_orthoclase > plagioclase_|minette|vogesite|
|_plagioclase > orthoclase_|kersantite|spessartite|

Appinites are therefore essentially plutonic equivalents of vogesites and spessartites.

Post-orogenic (415-395 Ma) lamprophyric dykes and sheets occur across Scotland in sufficient numbers for them to account for 10-20% of the total Caledonian magmatism. They have two distinct chemistries: those in the Northern Highlands terrane north of the Great Glen Fault are predominantly minettes, and those in the Central Highlands terrane south of the Great Glen Fault are predominantly spessartites.

Calc-alkaline lamprophyres have primitive compositions ( _i.e._ , the magmas came to the surface rapidly with little assimilation). The hydrous nature of the rocks reflects volatile retention via crystallization at high fluid pressures. Their rare earth chemistry suggests that the magmas originated at depths of over 100 km, and so the presence of lamprophyres with two different chemistries within a few kilometres on either side of the Great Glen Fault implies that this fault is near vertical and extends deep into the sub-continental lithospheric mantle.

On the field course we may encounter some Permo-Carboniferous lamprophyres. These are typically alkaline lamprophyres (containing feldspathoids and/or alkali-rich glass). Two of the most common lithologies among these lamprophyres are **_camptonites_** (feldspar > feldspathoid with the feldspars being predominantly plagioclase) and **_monchiquites_** (feldspar-free with a groundmass that may be glassy). In both of these types, the ferromagnesian minerals are typically sodic, Ti-rich amphiboles, Ti-augite, olivine, and biotite.

## **GLEN COE**

In the Glen Coe area the roots of a large Silurian-Devonian volcano are exposed. The geology is primarily an intra-caldera succession of silicic pyroclastic rocks and intermediate / silicic lavas with intercalated alluvial deposits, which overlies Dalradian metasedimentary rocks and pre-caldera andesitic sills. The succession is intruded by a wide variety of igneous rocks.

Most of the intra-caldera succession comprises seven thick ignimbrites which represent major calderaforming explosive eruptions that were associated with volcano-tectonic subsidence and caldera collapse. The subsidence was piecemeal, involving movement on numerous cross-cutting faults, many of which tapped the underlying magma chambers. After the early stages of caldera formation, a ring-fault system developed, a large part of the caldera succession foundered, and voluminous silicic magmas (now comprising the Clach Leathad Pluton) were emplaced.

#### **Some remarks on calderas**

Catastrophic eruption of tens to thousands of cubic kilometres of magma from a chamber beneath a volcano typically leads to collapse of the chamber roof and subsidence of the overlying volcanic pile. This subsidence forms the surface depression that is described as a caldera.

Most caldera-forming eruptions are explosive, involving sustained jets and gas-rich flows of vitric ash particles, pumice fragments, and crystals with some included rock fragments. Much of the erupted mixture is denser than air and so falls to the ground and then moves away from the vent as a pyroclastic flow. The resulting deposits are described as ignimbrites. Commonly the fragmented magmatic material in ignimbrites retains temperatures above 550° to 600°C and so the glassy constituents deform in ductile manner and become fused together. Fallout, both from the eruptive fountain and the associated pyroclastic flow, produces blanket-like deposits of pumice and ash. The magma that erupts is usually of intermediate to silicic composition, commonly dacite, rhyodacite or rhyolite.

Since the eruptive vents are mostly around the margins of the caldera, pyroclastic material usually only partially infills the topography. The topographic depressions are liable to flooding by water, and so eruptions commonly involve the explosive interaction of water and magma. When the interaction occurs beneath the ground surface in, for example, an aquifer, the resulting phreatomagmatic explosions typically build low profile tuff cones composed of ash and fragments of the aquifer.

#### **C.  Hand specimen scale observations**

Hand specimen scale observations lead to the point where an igneous rock may be named/classified. Note that naming comes _after_ making these observations and not before. The following is a recommended strategy for hand specimen inspection.

**1.** **_Weathering._** Examine a weathered surface noting its colour and any textural features. Sometimes weathered surfaces show up features not easily visible in fresh surfaces.

Colour index estimates are more reliable for coarse-grained samples.

## **Describing metasedimentary rocks in the field**

The metasedimentary units that you will see on this field course are of sufficiently low metamorphic grade for you to describe them at the outcrop and exposure scales in the same way as you would a sedimentary unit. Bed geometries have been modified by the deformation but remain recognizable, and many of the sedimentary structures ( _e.g._ , cross-bedding, graded bedding, erosive contacts) have been preserved. However, at the hand specimen scale, deformation, recrystallization, and new mineral growth have heavily overprinted the sedimentary textures and so the observations at this scale need to reflect this. An accurately drawn stratigraphic column showing up-stratigraphy variations in lithology, unit thicknesses, the nature of contacts, and sedimentary structures should be drawn, and if this is combined with stratigraphic columns drawn in other parts of the area, lateral variations in the stratigraphy can be depicted. Locally, where the variability of the lithologies warrants it ( _e.g._ , particularly near contacts between the mapping units), short lengths of properly measured section are the best method of communicating your lithological observations. Such measured sections ought to be primarily diagrammatic with annotations and comments reserved to convey points not easily communicated in a diagram. They should cover the following matters:

- (a) Contacts

   - Orientation of the contact

   - Nature of the contact ( _e.g._ , sharp/ gradual, transitional, faulted)

- (c) Geometry of the units

   - Shape of unit ( _e.g._ , tabular, lenticular, wedge-like)

   - Thickness of unit

- (c) Within unit variations in sedimentary structures

   - Changes in grain size ( _e.g._ , pebbly bases, any hints of graded bedding)

   - Indicate the present and scale of sedimentary structures ( _e.g._ , cross-bedding, slumping)

   - Show any sedimentary features associated with the contacts ( _e.g._ , erosive bases, loading)

- (d) Hand specimen scale features

   - Mineral assemblages with criteria used to identify the minerals ( _e.g._ , colour, cleavage, habit, hardness)

   - Comment on metamorphic textures: grain size, porphyroblasts, porphyroclasts, presence of any segregations

   - Note the nature of the tectonic fabrics providing orientations of cleavages and lineations in the area covered by the measured section, and with observations relevant for inferring the timing relationships of deformation and mineral growth when possible

- (e) Details of any later minor structures and their orientations ( _e.g._ , fractures, veins)

## **Writing descriptions of formations**

The content of a written (and illustrated) description of a geological formation depends upon the nature of that formation, for example, whether it is igneous (plutonic, hypabyssal, volcanic), metamorphic (low grade / high grade, _i.e._ , the extent to which features in its protolith are recognizable), or sedimentary ( _e.g._ , siliciclastic, carbonate, evaporitic). For igneous and metasedimentary units the key ingredients are summarized above.

Although the details of the content may vary, any good description should (a) be heavily focused on observations, (b) keep observation and interpretation separate as far as is practicable, and (c) be organized in something like the following way:

**1.** **_Formation name_** with a short (perhaps only one-line) description and indication of stratigraphic context to serve as an introduction

**2.** **_Outcrop scale observations:_** detailing the location and areal extent of the outcrop with a comment on the nature/quality of the exposure; describing any subdivisions (if any) and their locations and areal extents; indicating the total apparent thickness and apparent thicknesses of any subdivisions

**3.** **_Exposure-scale observations:_** primarily conveying information (quantitative whenever appropriate) on the nature of contacts, the geometry of units, and internal features ( _e.g._ , layering, sedimentary structures)

**4.** **_Hand specimen-scale observations:_** primarily conveying information on mineralogy/composition and on textural features

**5.** **_Microscopic observations (if thin section work has been carried out):_** amplifying the hand specimen scale compositional and textural observations with those features that require a microscope to be seen

**6.** **_Interpretation:_** a concise interpretative synthesis of the preceding observations, typically incorporating explicitly made deductions about the processes by which the unit was formed and was subsequently modified.

In general, point 2 above will be **_illustrated_** with a simplified map showing the outcrop and any contacts or features within it, point 3 will almost always be illustrated by a measured section to show up-stratigraphy and lateral variations, and points 4/5 will be illustrated by synoptic sketches illustrating textural relationships.

## **Field Notebook**

On this field course your notebook is not being marked. However, be aware that the quality of your field notebook is generally a good indicator of how you are approaching the task – if it is well-organized and tidy, what you are doing probably illustrates those qualities too. In order to keep it well-organized and tidy, you have to think about what you are doing, and the major benefit of this is that it helps you to come up with more effective and efficient mapping strategies. Keeping a good notebook takes effort but after a few days it becomes progressively easier.

#### **Some preliminary remarks**

The field notebooks of professional geologists belong to their employer and not to the geologist who compiles them. Hence, they must be fully intelligible to a third party, and contain all your data and any other relevant information. In principle, it should be possible to reconstruct your map and the observational part of your report using your notebook alone.

A field notebook that falls apart is incredibly depressing to the one who is compiling it. Hence it is worth getting a good one. It should be hard-backed, robust ( _i.e_ ., reasonably weather-proof), and of manageable size ( _e.g._ , A5). The purist takes the view that by recording your observations systematically in the field, only one version of the notebook is needed; in practice, some might like to keep a fair-copy notebook that is written up **_every_** evening. If you adopt the latter strategy, your fair copy notebook should be with you at all times in the field even if you are actually writing into another notebook when actually collecting your observations.

Notes should be written using a good quality pencil, with a good quality pencil sharpener and a good quality eraser to hand. There is no particular need to ink-in unless there is a risk of the writing becoming smudged or faint through rubbing. Colouring pencils can be used to advantage on sketches. Above all, be neat, and make effective use of terminology to keep your text concise.

#### **Layout**

You should write your name and permanent (or University) address inside the front cover as well as your local address. Provide information about the map grid and the magnetic declination used (see p.67). Before going into the field, many find it convenient to compile a few pages of notes about their mapping area that they have obtained from the literature. This may include a stratigraphical column with some details of the mapping unit lithologies, published maps, notes on key features to look out for, details of the deformation history, _etc_ . – essentially a set of quick reference facts that might come in handy while in the field.

Use a systematic layout for the notebook. Begin the entry for each day with the date, and with brief remarks about the weather and anything else that might affect the quality of that day’s observations ( _e.g._ , if you feel that you have illness coming on). ## **Field Map**

You are producing an **_exposure map_** , that is, one that shows the exposures as well as the readings. In contrast, an **_outcrop map_** shows the readings but does not show the outlines of the exposures. Arguably, marking exposures is unnecessary now that GPS systems make it easy and quick to locate oneself accurately at all times without sighting other exposures. However, showing exposure does indicate how much of it there is.

In general, the more geological information that you can put on your map without confusion the better; geological information is most easily assimilated and interpreted in its correct geographical position.

#### **Precision**

The base maps that you will be using are at a scale of 1:10000, that is, 1 mm on the map = 10 m on the ground. Thus a 0.2 mm thick line on the map has a ‘ground’ thickness of 2 m. This represents the limit of precision of your mapping and it is the precision that you should be aiming for when you locate features on the map. In order to achieve this precision, you must work as neatly and accurately as possible. Use fine lines when marking information on your map, and take care when locating yourself.

#### **Putting information on your map**

In the field, mark information on your map in pencil. Make sure that your pencil is well sharpened. It will take a day or two before you establish how much data can be accommodated on the map. The following should be recorded on the map while ‘on location’: locality number, mapping unit (using colour to show the extent of exposure), all structural readings, bedding trends (form-lines), and contacts. Most (including me) are sparing in how many readings we actually put on the map in the field but you **_absolutely must shade the extent of the exposure in the field_** – you cannot do this accurately in the evening. Normally, you should show topographic features that help to constrain contacts but this is not really necessary at Kinlochleven.

In the evenings, you should ink- and colour-in **_all the data_** collected in exposed areas. It is best to leave unexposed boundaries in pencil until you have all your data. Take care when inking in: (1) to use the recommended symbols and line colours, and (2) not to obscure any areas on the map that you have not yet visited. Lettering and symbols should be neat and small, but still legible.

#### **Data to be recorded**

Data recorded on the map should include things actually _observed_ in exposed rocks as well as those _inferred_ therefrom in unexposed areas.

**1.** **_Outlines of exposures at clearly defined numbered localities._** When finally inked in, locality numbers should be neat and legible but unobtrusive. Use a green pen so that the numbers are not confused with readings. Exposures should be outlined with a thin green line. Outlines should be accurate; stylized circles or ellipses will be marked down.

**2.** **_Lithologies._** The mapping units are **formations** not lithologies. Formations are defined on the basis of stratigraphical position as well as lithology. A formation is a mappable unit characterized by a particular lithology or group of lithologies. Where a sedimentary or meta-sedimentary unit comprises two or more lithologies, these may be inter-layered on a scale too small to be mapped individually; thus, for mapping purposes, the formation has to be defined on the basis of the _proportions_ of different

lithologies present and the _scale_ on which they are inter-layered. A formation is also defined by its stratigraphical position – two different units, each containing the same proportion of lithologies interlayered on the same scale but in different parts of the stratigraphical column, are assigned to different formations. Within a formation, an abnormally thick layer (> 5 m) of one of the constituent lithologies, or a thick layer of an uncharacteristic lithology, should be mapped as a separate member. Each exposure on the map should be coloured to indicate the formation or member present. A lighter tone of the same colour can be applied to those unexposed areas of the map where the same unit is inferred. Colours should be selected so that there is clear contrast across contacts. Bearing in mind the limits to precision, the thinnest unit that you can colour in accurately is about 4 m thick.

It is an internationally agreed convention that formations should be named on the basis of geography, not lithology ( _e.g_ ., “Mam Beag Formation”, not “Mam Beag Schist Formation”).

**3.** **_Geological boundaries._** Primary contacts and faults should be mapped with three categories of confidence. Within exposures, boundaries can be mapped with certainty. In unexposed ground, boundaries are either located with confidence ( _e.g_ ., where the two units straddling the boundary are both exposed, or if there is a tell-tale break in slope), or else are speculative ( _e.g_ ., if correlations are uncertain). When estimating the trend of an inferred boundary, remember that it will be influenced by both the topography and the dip of the contact. Wherever possible, trends should be estimated from topography and dip using stratum contours.

A form-line can be a powerful constraint on the position of an unexposed concordant sedimentary contact as the two features should be parallel. (A concordant sedimentary contact can be considered as a special kind of form-line that separates two formations.) Form-lines should be drawn wherever there is a _large_ exposure within which bedding has variable attitude. Formlines may be expected to bend at changes of slope; however, on a flat exposed surface, strongly curving form-lines pin-point fold hinges.

Other symbols ( _e.g_ ., lineations) should be plotted as close as possible, preferably along strike from their true position.

## **Constructing Cross-Sections**

Whereas maps illustrate the geometry of the geological structures in plan view, that is, on a nearly horizontal cut through the structures, cross-sections illustrate those structures on a more optimally oriented surface. In the Kinlochleven section drawing exercise you will be drawing a profile plane section to illustrate the geometry of the F1 folds along the coastline in the mapping area.

#### **The data to be shown on the cross-section**

- Ideally, the cross-section should be treated just like a field map: all the data used in its construction should be recorded on the section, that is, all the planar orientation measurements (bedding, cleavages) should be presented as apparent dip arrows, and younging information shown where it was observed (note that the younging symbol should be at right angles to the apparent dip arrow). Ideally, the exposure should also be shown projected on to the plane of section but we won’t ask you to do that for this exercise.

- The cross-section should be completed with the interpretation, that is, the positions of the mapping unit boundaries, fold axial traces, and any faults should be shown using the same solid/dashed line conventions as on the map. Show form-lines of bedding and cleavage.

- As this is not a vertical section, it is not necessary to show topography.

#### **The orientation of the cross-section**

- In an area of folded rocks, the ideal orientation for a cross-section is the fold profile plane, that is, a section drawn such that one is looking along the fold axis. The section may be drawn looking up-plunge or downplunge, whichever is the more convenient.

- Hence the first step is to plot a stereogram to determine the strike/dip of the fold profile plane. It is good practice to present a fully interpreted stereogram illustrating the geometry of the folds (complete with simplified profile plane sketch section) to accompany the section.

#### **Constructing the section**

Once you have chosen a cross-section plane orientation, putting the data on to the section involves two steps: (a) the apparent dip of each planar reading (bedding, cleavages) on the cross-section needs to be determined; (b) the projected position of each planar reading on the section needs to be determined.

The easiest way to determine apparent dips is by using a stereonet. Note that for planes with a strike close to perpendicular to the line of section, the difference between true dip and apparent dip is small.

## **Sir Edward Bailey (1881-1965)**

The following is taken (with minor editing) from A. Maltman (1998) _Geological Maps: An Introduction_ (2<sup>nd</sup> edition). For a more detailed biography see C.J. Stubblefield (1965), Edward Battersby Bailey, 1881-1965, _Biographical Memoirs of Fellows of the Royal Society_ 11:1-21.

Edward Bailey was born 1 July 1881 at Marden in Kent. At school he was picked on by bullies, and took some cruel beatings, but his reaction to this was to ‘toughen himself up’. As an undergraduate student he would go for marathon walks at night, took up boxing, and would sleep all year with his bedroom window wide open and only a cotton sheet for a cover. He graduated from Cambridge University in 1902 and applied to join the Geological Survey. Only two people were appointed that year, but Bailey was one of them – despite having broken the nose of the Director’s nephew in an undergraduate boxing match.

Bailey was assigned to the West Highland unit. He joined a team comprising three of the finest geologists the BGS has ever employed. Ben Peach and John Horne were fresh from their brilliant unravelling of the Moine Thrust region of northwest Scotland, and the field maps of the equally gifted C.T. Clough have often been cited as the standard against which others should be judged. The mapping training that Bailey received, and his sudden exposure to ‘a jostling crowd of problems awaiting solution’, exhilarated him. By his twenty-first birthday he was mapping

independently for the BGS and went on to be involved with many of the superb geological maps of the Scottish western seaboard.

Yet for Bailey the maps alone were not sufficient. The accompanying memoirs were vital channels for him to expound his interpretations of the geological histories of the surveyed areas. The memoir accompanying the map of Glencoe, for example, allowed the first detailed three-dimensional analysis of a volcanic caldera. It was a characteristic of Bailey’s writing that terms were used exactly, and if no suitable terms existed, he would invent them – antiform and synform, for example. In addition, Bailey began to publish the results of geological studies carried out in his own time – evenings, weekends, holidays – in journals other than official Survey publications. The liberty to do this was to become to Bailey a fundamental principle.

All was not straightforward in these early days – Bailey’s eccentricities were raising eyebrows. Why was some of his fieldwork being done barefoot? And was he really conducting official Survey work wearing shorts? The hierarchy reeled, and informed Bailey that he was not wearing ‘sufficiently formal attire’. Hearing this, Bailey promptly resigned, which rattled his senior supervisors even more. A special dispensation was produced which allowed him to continue his work in shorts.

At the start of World War I, Bailey volunteered for duty. He was posted to an island off Plymouth, but this did not stop him doing geology for he immediately began surveying the island, the results of which he published after the war. He was eventually sent to the front line and was badly wounded during the Battle of the Somme and later at Ypres. He sustained a badly damaged arm and lost an eye. He was later knighted for gallantry in battle.

72

After the war, Bailey was able to return to the survey of western Scotland and was put in charge. He was thereby enabled to complete a project that he had been involved with off and on since 1907 – the mapping of the island of Mull and nearby areas. The completed one-inch map appeared in 1924, and the following year the accompanying two-volume explanatory memoir was published. The map has been called a ‘landmark in the history of geological cartography’, ‘one of the most wonderful chapters of the geology of Britain’, and when the map was published it was said that ‘new lustre had been added to the Geological Survey’. The precision with which the intricate geology is portrayed, including over 40 kinds of igneous rocks, is remarkable. Bailey was clearly entranced, both with the routine surveying, and the gradual deciphering of the volcanic story that was hidden in the rugged mountains. His commitment to geology and oblivion of danger are notable. On one occasion of prolonged heavy rain, the survey team was camped in a meadow which became flooded. The team was forced to leave during the night, only to realise the next morning that Bailey had not joined their evacuation. One of them waded back to Bailey’s tent, to find the water lapping just inches below the level of the canvas camp bed. On the bed lay Bailey, happily reading a textbook on physics.

Nevertheless, despite this success, Bailey was continuing to annoy his superiors with his machismo activities. He would soak his boots at the start of a day in the field, so that further wettings would go unnoticed. He was renowned for eating nothing more than a chocolate bar for lunch, or, if he had been supplied with more, eating it first thing to ‘get it over with’. On one occasion Bailey received an official reprimand for leading his new Assistant Director through a chin-deep river, when he knew there was a perfectly good bridge just out of sight upstream. But much more significantly than all of this, in 1920 a director was appointed to the BGS who felt that all his staff’s scientific writings, whether or not they were related to survey work, had to be vetted by him. This was completely contrary to the principle of scientific liberty cherished by Bailey. He became increasingly uneasy with the new regime. In turn, his director grew paranoid about enforcing his new rule. Things eventually came to a head when Bailey was banned from looking at any of the Survey’s field maps, even though they were available for consultation by the general public. Bailey resigned, taking up the life of a university professor, at Glasgow.

At Glasgow Bailey clearly enjoyed himself, travelling far and wide, coming up with innovative interpretations, and letting his geological imagination roam. He was one of the first in the UK to embrace a wild new theory which involved the continents actually drifting apart. He seized on the significance in rocks of ‘way-up’ indicators, was one of the first to recognize submarine slumping in rocks and anticipated the concept of growth faulting. Bailey had an unrivalled grasp of the three-dimensional aspects of map work. He began to apply his skills to geometrically difficult regions such as the Swiss Alps. The convoluted forms of the sections he constructed became nicknamed ‘Bailey’s bicycles’. He, himself, enjoyed a play on his first name in his paper entitled ‘Eddies in mountain structure’. He formulated some of the methods for dealing with the outcrop patterns of structurally complex areas, and, following a visit to the Appalachians, helped promote the ‘down-structure’ viewing technique.

However, despite the scientific liberty which a university offered, it seems that the Geological Survey was still Bailey’s first love. In 1936 he was invited to return – as Director. Bailey accepted, but on his own conditions regarding research. As it transpired, he had little time to put his ideals into action before once again he and his Survey were plunged into supporting the national war effort. In World War II, BGS geologists were not involved in the fighting, but rather in all kinds of strategic support work. Geological surveying was required for underground vaults, new airbases, temporary water supplies, _etc._ , as well as for sources of raw materials which could no longer be imported. These ranged from coal and iron to less obvious but vital needs – sand for sandbags, mica for electrical equipment, silica for optical glass, even sapphires for aircraft compass pivots. Bailey threw himself into his new responsibilities. He was later to be

73

proud of the BGS war effort and was eager to point out that its duties had been greatly facilitated by the fact that highstandard geological maps of the UK already existed.

During the war, the BGS office was evacuated, becoming the Civil Defence Headquarters. The geological maps and materials were shipped to North Wales for safety; the geologists, too, were moved. Except Bailey. He found space sufficient for a temporary office on the topmost gallery of the Survey building and worked there throughout the London blitz. True to form, he seemed oblivious of the danger, even after he was ‘temporarily buried by a V1 or Doodlebug’.

At the end of the war Bailey saw the need for the Survey to have a fresh look. Reorganization was necessary, and Bailey felt that it was time for him to make way for new blood. He retired, although until his death 20 years later he continued actively in geology, publishing research papers, and attending geological meetings – invariably wearing shorts.

In 1948, there came to fruition one of his pet projects. Back in 1942 Bailey had been appointed to look after the geological aspects of a ‘National Atlas’. He had arranged for the BGS to produce a map at the innovative scale of 1:625000, or ‘about ten miles to an inch’. The scale was selected in order that the land mass could be accommodated on ‘two not over-large sheets’. The map was to carry a new kind of 10 km grid. This was to help unify the two sheets and give easy correlation with larger-scale maps which from then on were to carry a kilometre grid. This ‘National Grid’ would also provide a convenient and precise reference system for locations ‘much more easy to employ than that given by longitudes and latitudes’. Most of the detailed survey data were already available on the one-inch sheets; it was largely a matter of compilation and reorganization to fit the new scale. Bailey took the project to heart. The north sheet covered the Scottish Highlands and Islands, and he saw this part, especially, as his own.

On its publication, the map was hailed a great success. Since then, it has remained the standard geological map of Great Britain, and a model for many other national maps. Without a great deal of alteration, a second edition was published in 1957, and a third in 1979. The north and south sheets are widely available today for purchase. Also still sold are the one-inch maps of the Scottish western seaboard which Bailey helped produce. They remain the definitive maps of those areas (some now photographically enlarged to 1:50000), especially the celebrated Mull map. The legacy to geological maps of Sir Edward Bailey remains very tangible.

Bailey produced the first comprehensive geological map of the Loch Leven area, and while the interpretation of the structures has evolved since he did the work, his map remains a fine statement of what is there on the ground.

## **Health and Safety / Behaviour**

### **Nationally agreed Code of Safety and Behaviour (relevant parts for this course)**

##### **To:  All students attending a geological field course**

- It is important that we know if you have any disability which may influence your work, _e.g_ ., diabetes, epilepsy, _etc_ ., so that appropriate action can be taken should you be affected while on a field course. You should therefore have filled in the Medical Questionnaire provided prior to the field course. This will be kept by the field course leader.

- **_All information will be treated with strict confidence._**

- If for any reason you are prevented from attending a prescribed field course, or have to withdraw during such a course, please inform the course leader at the earliest opportunity. _If the reasons are medical, you will be expected to produce a doctor’s certificate._

##### **I. General**

Geological fieldwork is an activity involving some inherent special risks and hazards, _e.g_ ., exploring coast exposures, quarries, and mountains. Severe or dangerous weather conditions may also be encountered at any season, especially on mountains or the coast. In accordance with the Health and Safety at Work Act, leaders will have been advised by the School to take certain safety precautions and every reasonable care concerning the safety of members of their parties. _It is imperative that students should cooperate by behaving reasonably in order to reduce the risk of accidents_ . **_Each individual is responsible for his or her own safety._**

##### **II.  In particular you must:**

1. Observe all safety instructions given by party leaders or field advisers. Anyone not conforming to the standards required may be dismissed from the field course (with financial and examination liabilities as a result).

2. Stay with the party, except by clear arrangement with the leaders. Assemble _where_ requested in order to receive specific instructions regarding likely hazards. Observe instructions for reporting back after completion of work.

3. Report any injury or illness to the field course leader as soon as possible.

4. Carry identification of yourself and the School.

##### **III.  Equipment**

<u>Note:  leaders are advised by the School to refuse to allow ill-equipped students on their field courses.</u>

1. Wear adequate clothing and footwear for the type of weather and terrain likely to be encountered. Shirt, loose-fitting trousers, warm sweater, and brightly coloured rain-proof jacket with hood, are desirable in Scotland. A woollen hat (in addition to a coat hood) is useful on high ground. Waterproof over-trousers are necessary for wet weather. Jeans generally do not give sufficient protection when wet and subject to cold wind but can suffice if waterproof overtrousers are worn as well. Walking boots with rubber mountaineering soles are normally essential. Sports shoes/trainers, etc., are unsuitable for mountains, quarries or rough/wet country. Wellington boots are generally best reserved for streams, peat bogs, _etc_ ., but are inadequate and usually uncomfortable on rocky ground.

2. Always wear a safety helmet, preferably with chin strap, where there is a risk from falling objects.

3. Wear safety goggles when hammering rocks or using chisels. **_Do not use_** a geological hammer as a chisel while hitting on it with another hammer; use only a soft steel chisel, preferably with a plastic guard. Avoid hammering near another person. Avoid looking towards another person hammering.

4. Carry at all times a survival bag, emergency food supplies, whistle, small first aid kit.

##### **IV.  Behaviour**

1. Be aware of traffic when examining road cuttings. While there, avoid hammering, and in any event do not leave rock debris on the roadway or verges.

2. Take great care when walking or climbing over slippery rocks on rock shores, particularly below high water mark. More accidents to geologists, including fatalities, occur along rocky shorelines than anywhere else.

75

3. Do not climb cliffs, rock faces or crags unless this had been approved **_in writing_** as an essential part of the work.

4. Take special care near the edges of cliffs and quarries, or any other steep or sheer faces, particularly in gusting winds.

5. Ensure that rocks above are safe before venturing below. Avoid working under steep overhangs. Avoid loosening rocks on steep slopes and _never_ roll rocks down slope for amusement. Beware of landslides and mudflows occurring on clay cliffs and in clay pits, or rockfalls from any cliffs.

Leaders of parties will follow the general guidance contained in: _A Code for Geological Field Work_ issued by the Geologists' Association; _Mountain Safety – basic precautions_ published by Climber and Rambler; _Guidelines for visits to quarries –_ laid down by the British Quarrying and Slag Federation.

##### **V.  To: all students undertaking geological fieldwork alone, in pairs, or small groups (section relevant for this**

##### **field course):**

##### **Qualitative Assessment of Risk**

- Student misbehaviour is one of the most significant sources of risk in the field. Act sensibly, and take care not to put yourself or people near you at risk. Do not fool around, and do not throw stones.

- The weather in Scotland in April can be hot and sunny but is more commonly, cool, windy and wet. If it is cold there is a real risk of exposure, particularly on high ground. Even if the weather does not seem to be very cold, windy or wet in the morning, be prepared by packing spare warm and waterproof (preferably brightly coloured) clothes. Even if you doubt that it will be needed, pack sun-screen to guard against sun-burn.

- The mapping area involves walking across featureless moorland or hilly terrain in which one can easily get lost. Take care not to get separated from the rest of the group. Guard against this by ensuring that you have the mobile phone numbers of fellow students but be alert to the possibility that mobile phone reception may be poor.

- Shore /river platforms and boulders are often extremely slippery when wet, particularly when covered in green algae and seaweed. A bad fall can result in a broken arm, leg or collar-bone or a fractured skull.

- Some shore exposures have irregular surfaces on which it is easy to twist an ankle.

- Grassy / mossy hillsides can be very slippery in the rain. Even in dry weather, the ground can be saturated in water, and hence treacherous under-foot, especially if there has been a lot of rain in recent weeks.

- The tidal range in Loch Leven is small, and there are no localities on the itinerary where one can get cut off by the tide. Even so, you should always be aware of tidal conditions when working along the coast.

- Road cuttings/crags present a risk of falling rocks. Be alert to this.

- The main road through the mapping area carries fast-moving traffic. You must always walk along the pavement or verge in single file.

##### **Site-Specific Hazards**

- A. _Shore at Loch Leven:_ Rock surfaces are slippery when wet and extra care must be taken at low tide on seaweedcovered areas. Take care not to trip or twist your ankle on irregular rock surfaces. Be aware of the tide at all times.

- B. _Upland areas at Kinlochleven:_ Be prepared for bad weather at all times. Take care to guard against getting exposure. Take care not to get separated from the rest of the group or your mapping partner(s).
