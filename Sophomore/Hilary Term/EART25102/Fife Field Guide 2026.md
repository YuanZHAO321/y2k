## **Part 2 - Aims and assessment**

This field course aims to develop your competence in the techniques that are essential for describing and interpreting sedimentary and igneous rocks.

You will develop skills in describing sedimentary rocks at outcrop and bed scale; interpreting sedimentary processes; and reconstructing environments and environmental change. To a lesser degree you will interpret basin-scale sedimentary sources and routing patterns. During the field course you will learn to collect datasets and use your data to address some of the fundamental questions that geologists ask themselves when mapping sedimentary rocks, such as:

- What sort of depositional environment were the sediments deposited in and what were the major processes operating in that environment?

- How does the depositional environment vary both laterally and through time?

- What sort of rocks were the sediments derived from and where was the parent rock located?

- What are the major controls on deposition within sedimentary basins?

You will develop skills in describing igneous rocks at outcrop scale, describing and interpreting igneous contacts, interpreting intrusion and extrusion processes, and assessing post-emplacement modification and alteration. You will learn to make observations and collect measurements appropriate for quantitative description of igneous rocks, and use your data to address some fundamental questions that geologists think about when mapping igneous rocks, such as:

- What is the relationship between igneous intrusions and the surrounding country rock?

- How did the igneous activity change over time, in terms of geochemical composition and intrusion/eruption style?

- What was the location or source of the igneous activity – particularly relevant for lava flows and pyroclastic deposits?

##### **_Intended learning outcomes_** :

At the end of the field course you should be able to:

- Collect quantitative descriptive data of sedimentary rocks, such as clast-counting and logging measured sections.

- Reconstruct sedimentary rock source area(s) and transport distances.

- Define sedimentary facies from a consideration of the types of processes responsible for the sedimentary structures, contact relations and bedforms observed.

- Group facies coherently into facies associations as a basis for inferring depositional environments.

- Analyse facies association stacking patterns to reconstruct environmental change in space and time, and provide insight into the larger scale controls on deposition within sedimentary basins such as relative and eustatic sea level changes, and the influence of tectonics on subsidence and sediment supply.

- Collect qualitative and quantitative descriptive data of igneous rocks, such as thickness of lava flows; vesicle, phenocryst or xenolith size distributions; jointing patterns.

- Reconstruct the direction to source area(s) of extrusive igneous rocks.

- Interpret mechanisms of intrusion, e.g. passive vs forceful, using observations of igneous contacts with country rock.

- Evaluate the volcanic and magmatic context of igneous activity.

## **Part 3 - Geological Context**

##### **Introduction**

The Devonian and Carboniferous rocks of Angus and Fife were deposited in a relatively narrow WSW-ENE-striking basin known as the Midland Valley Basin (MVB), which broadly corresponds to the central lowlands of Scotland (the “Central Belt”). The Midland Valley Basin was bounded to the north and south by two major master faults: the Highland Boundary Fault to the north, and the Southern Upland Fault to the south (Fig. 3.1, 3.2). Today, the terrain of the basin is characterised by an undulating or rolling landscape with rich arable land. The Carboniferous rocks of the Midland Valley contain the main key ingredients for steel-making (iron ore and coal – the main resource for hot furnaces, and limestone – for removing impurities) and energy generation. In the industrial revolution, beginning in the 19<sup>th</sup> Century, the Midland Valley became one of the most densely populated parts of the United Kingdom. Today it is home to close to 75% of Scotland’s population (c. 4 million persons [2023]).

##### **Geological history and stratigraphy**

Detailed treatment of the geological history of the MVB can be found in Bluck (2002), Trewin and Thirlwall (2002), Read et al. (2002), and Underhill et al. (2008). This summary is derived from these and other sources.

The Midland Valley Basin is an c. 80 km-wide, >150 km-long WSW-ENE trending Late Palaeozoic fault-bounded basin, and likely extends in the subsurface westwards towards Northern Ireland and eastward into the North Sea (Fig. 3.1, 3.2). The Midland Valley Basin is bounded by Neoproterozoic rocks to the north and Lower Paleozoic rocks to the south, and contains largely an upper Palaeozoic fill. The basin as a whole has the appearance of a rift graben (Fig. 3.1, 3.2).

The Caledonian Highlands formed as a consequence of the closure of the Iapatus Ocean during the Caledonian Orogeny at the end of the early Palaeozoic. Though peak metamorphism in the orogeny occurred in what is now the Grampian region, the mountain belt extended southward as far as what is now central Wales. Beginning in the latest Silurian, a (punctuated) episode of post-orogenic extension began in the Midland Valley that initiated a protracted episode of rapid crustal subsidence and the formation of the basin. There is long-standing uncertainty concerning the plate tectonic driving mechanisms and kinematics behind this episode of extension and subsidence. The basin was originally thought to conform to a classic rift valley developed into continental crust, bounded to the NE and SE by major normal faults: the Highland Boundary Fault (HBF) to the northeast, and the Southern Upland Fault (SUF) to the southwest (e.g. George, 1960). This model was contested by Leeder (1982), who interpreted the subsidence as resulting from latest Silurian-Devonian N-S back-arc extension above the northward-subducting Gonwanan lithosphere, followed by late Carboniferous thermal subsidence. Unquestionably there is evidence of major strike-slip deformation on the major basin-bounding HBF and SUF, and along more minor intrabasinal faults, implying that the basin has a relatively complex origin and kinematic history.

At the time of early extension in the latest Silurian, Scotland lay within the arid subtropics, drifting from c. 30° south of the equator and reaching c. 20° south by the end Devonian. Initially, in the lower Devonian, the MVB was broadly segmented into two distinct elongate, parallel sub-basins, the Strathmore and Lanark basins, separated by a central chain of developing volcanoes (Fig. 3.1, 3.3,

3.4). The general picture of sedimentation in the lower Devonian was of transverse alluvial fans, delivering more locally derived sediment from the Highlands flanking the MVB to the north and south, and to a lesser extent, reworking of the volcanics that formed the sill separating the two sub-basins (Fig. 3.3). Flooring the centre of the sub-basins were broad fluvial plains, crossed by major SW-draining axial rivers probably originating in Scandinavia, and ephemeral lakes. The transverse alluvial fans were probably minor tributaries to the major axial rivers.

Abundant volcanism at around 410 Ma in Scotland is related to subduction along the northwest margin of the Iapetus (Thirwall, 1988). In the northern part of the Midland Valley, 300-1800 m of basalts, andesites and rhyolites are exposed (Fig. 3.7). Lavas and tephras intrastratify with the predominantly alluvial/fluvial clastic sediments of the **_Stonehaven Group_** and **_Strathmore Group_** (Fig. 3.5, 3.6). The Midland Valley igneous rocks are calc-alkaline in character, and include high-K shoshonitic basalts and basaltic andesites, such as the lavas of the Crawton Volcanic Formation. Some of the Midland Valley lavas show strong signatures of subducted greywacke, suggesting at least some degree of sediment melting within the subduction zone.

In the mid-Devonian, an episode of NW-SE-directed compression occurred, briefly (c. 10 Ma) inverting the MVB. Deformation was localised on the major basin-bounding faults (reverse faulting on the HBF), but also diffusely strained the lower Devonian succession through open folding (e.g. the Strathmore Syncline in the footwall of HBF; Fig. 3.4). The locus of uplift was to the south of the MVB, as evidenced by a greater lacuna in deposition to the south, and the continuation of sedimentation in the Orcadian Basin in NE Scotland and the Northern Isles (Fig. 3.3).

The Midland Valley Basin underwent renewed subsidence in the Upper Devonian, continuing into the Carboniferous. Resumption of deposition occurred via braided rivers and alluvial fans, passing upward into fluvial channel and floodplain successions characterised by the development of caliche palaeosols (the **_Stratheden Group_** ; Fig. 3.5, 3.8). In the Upper Devonian, major axial river systems appear to have been directed towards the NE, in direct contrast to Lower Devonian times. Aeolian dune systems formed on these floodplains too (Fig. 3.9). The entire Upper Devonian succession rests unconformably on the folded Lower Devonian succession (Fig. 3.5). In the Arbroath area there is perhaps 100 m of relief on the unconformity.

By Carboniferous times, Gondwana had fully docked with Laurentia to form Pangaea. A major new uplifting mountain belt, the Variscan Orogen, lay several hundreds of kilometres to the south of the MVB; the margin of the Variscan deformation lies across South Wales and Southern England. By this time the former Caledonian highlands formed much more subdued topography (but topography nonetheless). During the Carboniferous, Leeder (1982) proposes that basin subsidence in the MVB changed from fault-driven rifting to a regime of post-rift thermal sagging with lower rates of subsidence (Fig. 3.3). Nonetheless, volcanism in the basin remained active, peaking in Viséan to Namurian times. Two aspects of local and global palaeogeography are significant to deposition in the MVB. Firstly, the area drifted into the intertropical convergence zone (“the tropics”), around 10° south of the equator. This significantly altered the climatic regime in the Carboniferous to one of wet, warm climatic conditions lacking in, or with subdued, seasonality. Secondly, the Gondwanan sector of Pangaea had drifted over the palaeo-south pole, where major continental glaciation developed on a similar scale to Antarctica today. Waxing and waning of the continental icecap, possibly at Milankovic timescales, resulted in major sea level fluctuations exceeding 100 m.

The Carboniferous succession of the MVB (Fig. 3.5, 3.10) is characterised by cyclothemic deposition. Cyclothems are cycles a few metres to tens of metres thick, characterised by an upward transition through one or more of the following: (1) marine or lake mudstones, limestones and ironstones; (2) deltaic heterolith and sandstones; (3) fluvial heterolith and sandstones; and (4) coal. The idealised cycle described here would represent, at its base, a rapid deepening event (flooding), followed by gradual infilling of the bathymetry by sediment supply ultimately delivered by rivers. The base of the next cyclothem represents renewed flooding of the land surface. Cycles are rarely complete, and formations within the **_Inverclyde to Coal Measures Groups_** (Fig. 3.10) are defined according the proportion of marine, deltaic and fluvial facies present. Researchers disagree on the precise driving mechanisms for the formation of this highly cyclic sedimentation style, some invoking glacio-eustatic sea level fluctuations; others autocyclic delta switching in an overall subsiding regime; others pulses of subsidence; and yet others a combination of one, two or three of these drivers.

The rifting associated with the opening of the Midland Valley generated significant magmatic activity (Fig. 3.11). Alkaline (silica-undersaturated) and tholeiitic (silica-saturated) magmas were generated by variable degrees of partial melting of the asthenspheric mantle (Upton et al., 2004). In the Late Carboniferous, significant volumes of tholeiitic lava were emplaced, especially in the southern Midland Valley (e.g. the **_Bathgate Group_** ; Fig. 3.12). Alkali basaltic magmatism persisted into the Early Permian. The alkali basalts and dolerites are variously composed of plagioclase, clinopyroxene, olivine, amphibole, and analcite (a feldspathoid mineral). As the sedimentary basin fill increased, sill intrusion tended to dominate over lava extrusion. The sills, diatremes and volcanic plugs intruding the Carboniferous sedimentary succession form prominent topographic relief in the modern landscape of the Midland Valley, most famously expressed by Arthur’s Seat in Edinburgh. Some of the igneous rocks are only a little younger than the sediments they intrude; in some locations there is evidence that they perhaps intruded into almost unconsolidated and still water-saturated sediment. The locations of the Fife volcanic vents were to some extent controlled by the existing basement structure: for example, along the Ardross Fault. The remains of the plumbing systems of these volcanoes remain in the rock record as discrete volcanic plugs and necks, such as at the Rock and Spindle. Many of the Fife volcanic plugs contain xenoliths of mantle peridotite and pyroxenite, as well as lower-crustal granulites and meta-igneous rocks.

The final chapter in the story of the Midland Valley Basin is of its Permian inversion. By the end Carboniferous, the basin had drifted north of the equator, and once again into the arid subtropics. This is expressed by the absence of coal and renewed development of red-beds in the **_Upper Coal_**

However, the Upper Devonian to Carboniferous succession of the MVB was folded and thrusted, and a clear mechanism for the deformation other than the far-field effects of Variscan stress is lacking. ##### **3. Genetic classification of tephra deposits**

Pyroclastic deposits may be classified according to their formation into: **pyroclastic fall** deposits or **pyroclastic density current** deposits (also called pyroclastic flow deposits). This is the most interesting approach, but if the rock is wrongly classified it can lead to problems interpreting the volcanic processes that formed the rock.

**Pyroclastic fall deposits** contain pyroclasts that are coarsest near the vent (bombs and coarse lapilli) and become increasingly finer-grained away from the vent (fine lapilli and ash). The thickness and dispersal of the pyroclasts depends on the type and magnitude of the eruption that formed the deposit. Two end-member genetic types of fall deposits are recognised:

<u>Scoria fall deposits derive from Strombolian eruptions of scoria cones. Composed of basaltic to ande-</u> sitic vesiculated pyroclasts (scoria). Deposits do not usually spread far from the vent.

<u>Pumice fall deposits derive from Plinian eruptions of stratovolcanoes. Composed of highly vesicu-</u> lated dacitic to rhyolitic pyroclasts (pumice). Deposits may be distributed for hundreds of square kilometres away from the vent.

**Pyroclastic density current deposits** (also called pyroclastic flow deposits) are classified according to the density of the individual pyroclasts, and the concentration of the current that carried them.

Ignimbrites are the classic “pyroclastic flow deposit”. They have distinctive ignimbritic textures that can be recognised in hand specimen and thin section. Many ignimbrites are strongly compacted and welded, so that the rock takes on a preferred orientation of flattened pumice lumps (fiammé) and/or stretched-out shards, resulting in a eutaxitic texture. Most ignimbrites are rhyolitic or dacitic in composition.

Ignimbrite deposits tend to be massive and poorly sorted. Pyroclastic surge deposits often exhibit cross-bedding.
