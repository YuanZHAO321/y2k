## **III. Sample description**

#### **Checklist for carbonate rock descriptions (field/hand sample and thin section)**

#### <u>Color</u>

- both weathered and fresh

- may indicate mineralogy – dolomites commonly yellowish/brownish, red in presence of Fe - may be better visible on weathered surfaces

#### <u>Lithology</u>

- limestone, dolomite, marl, mixed carbonate-clastic (NB: use adjectives such as ‘quartzose’, ‘shaley’…. when describing carbonate-clastic mixtures, but avoid calcarenite, which should be restricted to grain size)

- quick check with HCl

#### <u>Bedding and sedimentary structures</u>

- thickness of beds (laminated, thin, thick, massive)

- lateral extent of beds (compare also bioherm/biostrome for organic accumulations)

- bedding surfaces, contacts (sharp, gradational, erosive, wavy, presence of clay seams, diagenetic features such as stylolites…..)

- sedimentary structures in beds (lamination, cross-bedding/-lammination, ripples, graded beds….; includes massive beds without structures – note this can indicate primary absence of structures or secondary destruction due to bioturbation or diagenesis)

- base-of-bed structures: load casts, backfilled channels, gutter casts…..

- any features useful for paleocurrents (ripples, aligned shells in coquinas…)

- very early diagenetic features such as mudcracks, fenestrae, edgewise conglomerates as direct indicators for processes and environments…..

- look for geopetal structures as indicators of stratigraphic up, convex/concave up of shells as indicators of in-situ vs transported nature, life position of fossils such as corals

- cyclicity?

#### <u>Grain size and texture</u>

- calcilutite, calcisiltite, calcarenite, calcirudite are useful field descriptions

- sorting, grading, roundness, presence/absence of micrite (mud) – grain-/mud-supported texture

- packing of fossils as indicators for transport processes/in-situ accumulation

- note that grain size/shape/sorting in carbonates are controlled primarily by original size and shape of biogenic skeletons, abrasion processes during (limited) transport, but also energy level

#### <u>Components</u>

- identify and quantify grains, micrite, sparite and porosity if present (use percentage chart or point counting)

- grain types (skeletal/bioclast, non-skeletal), order them according to abundance

- any characteristic faunal associations

- note degree of grain preservation, in particular for bioclasts: entire or broken, original shell preserved or replaced/dissolved (mold)

- micrite: appears pasty in hand sample, fine-grained & dense in thin section; various sedimentary and diagenetic origins - not equivalent to siliciclastic mud

- sparite: appears clear, like glass in hand sample, identifiable crystals/crystal boundaries in thin section; cement and product of diagenetic recrystallization

9

#### <u>Biogenic structures</u>

- bioturbation: degree, type

#### <u>Diagenetic fabrics</u>

- replacement fabrics, dolomitization

- pressure solution (mechanical/chemical)

- nodular fabrics or beds, mineralogy of nodules (silica, Fe….)

- fractures, orientation, width, open/cemented

- type of porosity, estimate amount (none, low, fair, good, excellent; or use percentage chart)

<u>For classification schemes (Dunham in particular)</u>

- consider whether rock derives from activity of benthic sessile organisms (autochthonous – bound together during deposition), or consists of grains, mud and/or cement (allochthonous – not bound together during deposition)

- autochthonous carbonates:

- sessile organisms form low-relief, tightly packed ‘crusts’ or accretion structures- _bindstone_

- sessile organisms form vertical structures separated by sediment – _bafflestone_

- sessile organisms form framework with intervening incrustations - _framestone_

- allochthonous carbonates: Dunham works on the basis of 3 simple questions: presence/absence of mud?, grain- vs mud-supported fabric?, percentage of grains?

- apply the Embry and Klovan classification for autochthonous carbonates when the role of sessile benthic organisms can be demonstrated; otherwise use _boundstone_

- integrate the dominant or ecologically important fossil group, or dominant grain type into the name, e.g. (branched) coral framestone

- for grainstones, additional clues as to sorting and/or grain size should be included when possible, as this is relevant for porosity evolution

- specific concepts are required for reefal, non-marine and recrystallized carbonates

- and finally -  “A classification is simply a tool for organizing information, not the source of a conclusion” (Blatt, 1972)
