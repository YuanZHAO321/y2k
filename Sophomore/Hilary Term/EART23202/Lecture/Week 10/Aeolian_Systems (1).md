- What are aeolian environments?

- The adjective, aeolian describes genetic processes and products of, erosion, entrainment, transportation and deposition by the wind.

- The term derives from the Latin Æolus, god of the winds (itself borrowed from Greek Αἴολος, lord of the winds).

## Slide 5

- Aeolian transport processes

- Aeolian sediment entrainment occurs when and where wind forces are able to overcome the effects of surface stabilisation.

- For grains of sand and finer grade, ballistic impacts and aerodynamic lift promote entrainment, whereas particle weight, friction, drag, and cohesive inter-particle forces retard entrainment.

- Drag and lift are generated by fluid flow over and around particles, whereas weight, friction and cohesion are determined by particle size, density, shape, packing, moisture content, mineralogy and degree of inter-particle cementation (Lancaster, 1995).

- L = lift, C = cohesion, D = aerodynamic drag, W = weight, I = impact.

- Image: Mountney et al., 2023

## Slide 6

- Aeolian transport processes

- The construction of dunes and sandsheets requires the availability of abundant sand grains, which are mostly transported as bedload prior to deposition.

- By contrast,  silt and clay fractions are mostly transported aloft in suspension.

- Under most wind conditions, gravel and coarser sediments are not moved, and are left in situ as a deflation lag deposit: a reg or serir.

## Slide 7

- Aeolian transport processes

- L = lift, C = cohesion, D = aerodynamic drag, W = weight, I = impact.

- Sand carried as bedload may be transported by one or more of the following processes:

- (1) rolling or creep due to drag directly on the bed

- (2) saltation whereby particles are temporarily lifted into the airflow and then collide with other particles in the flow, thereby inducing a chain reaction of interparticle collisions

- (3) reptation whereby saltating particles that return to the bed impact and nudge other particles and cause them to hop short distances downwind.

## Slide 9

- Where do aeolian processes operate?

- Aeolian processes operate in a variety settings:

- Hot and cold climatic deserts, coastal region, fluvial and glacial outwash plains, volcanic regions and aeolian systems supply sediment to the deep ocean.

- Although aeolian processes operate in various climate regimes, they are currently most active and widespread in arid, hot and cold deserts.

- In these settings, dry sandy substrates that lack significant vegetation cover are prone to sediment erosion, transport and deposition via the wind.

- Aeolian processes and resultant deposits are most widespread in the world’s climatic desert regions.

## Slide 16

- Desert aeolian environments

- Both present-day aeolian systems and their preserved successions are commonly classified as dry, wet or stabilising types (Kocurek & Havholm, 1993).

- Dry aeolian systems develop where accumulation is controlled solely by aerodynamic configuration. Down-wind airflow deceleration causes sediment-laden winds to deposit some of their load.

- Wet aeolian systems develop where accumulation is controlled by progressive water-table rise concomitant with ongoing aeolian activity – notably, dune migration.

- Stabilising aeolian systems develop where physical, chemical or biogenic agents promote aeolian system construction and accumulation. For example, where vegetation or surface cements act to restrict the transport capacity of the wind.

## Slide 19

- Non-desert aeolian environments

- Aeolian processes are also widespread in many humid settings where there exists a surfeit of sediment available for transport and   the wind velocity is sufficiently great to overcome resistive forces induced by the presence of moisture on sediment surfaces.

- Notable this includes pro-glacial regions (McKenna-Neuman & Gilbert, 1986).

- In many humid settings, however, the presence of vegetation can have a stabilizing effect on depositional surfaces, thus inhibiting aeolian processes.

- In the example here, high wind speeds enable significant aeolian sand transport, despite the overall humid climate setting and a generally high water-table level.

## Slide 21

- Aeolian loess environments

- Loess is composed of fine, wind-blown silt and clay particles

- Loess deposits are found in various regions around the world, but they are most commonly associated with areas that have experienced extensive glacial activity during past ice ages.

- Prominent loess regions include the American Midwest, the Loess Plateau in China, the Russian steppes, and parts of Europe.

- Loess formation typically occurs in areas where strong winds carry fine-grained sediment from exposed surfaces, such as glacial outwash plains, river valleys, and deserts.

- Loess is known for its high fertility due to its mineral-rich composition and ability to retain moisture. Despite its fertility, loess can be vulnerable to erosion, especially when exposed by agricultural practices or construction activities.

## Slide 22

- Aeolian contributions to other systems

- Aeolian transport of dust occurs where strong winds lift and carry large amounts of dust in the atmosphere, typically in arid or semi-arid regions with loose, dry soil or sandy terrain.

- Dust can reach atmospheric concentrations in excess of 6000 μg m−3 in severe events (Song et al ., 2007).

- Dust storms can have significant societal impacts, including reduced visibility and transportation disruptions, reduced productivity in agricultural regions, and dust-related respiratory diseases.

- Sand and dust storms play an important role in ocean fertilization (Han et al., 2008), carrying significant amounts of nutrient-rich dust particles into the ocean, which can stimulate the growth of phytoplankton – the primary producers in the oceanic food chain. This process is known as ocean fertilization.

- Dust storm from the Namib Desert. Image from Pfiz et al. (2010)

## Slide 23

- Aeolian contributions to other systems

- Aeolian transport of volcanic ash can occur over 100s – 1000s km, depending on eruption magnitude, wind patterns, and atmospheric conditions.

- Fine ash particles (<0.1 mm) can remain airborne for extended periods and be transported over vast distances, while coarser particles tend to settle closer to the source of eruption.

- Particle density and shape influence the settling velocity and suspension characteristics of volcanic ash.

- Lighter, more irregularly shaped particles have greater potential for prolonged suspension and long-distance transport.

- Ash plumes can spread across entire regions or even continents, affecting air quality, visibility, and human activities.

## Slide 26

- Aeolian Bedforms

- Aeolian ripples, dunes and megadunes (draa) form a hierarchy of equilibrium bedforms and one or more of these types are present in the great majority of depositional aeolian environments

- Bedform size provides a basis for initial bedform classification and description.

- Almost all dunes and megadunes have ripples present on many parts of their sloping surfaces, and ripples are also common on sandsheet surfaces and in interdunes.

- Three morphologically distinct types of small-scale ripple structure occur on wind-blown surfaces: impact ripples, megaripples, adhesion ripples.

- Each are also recognised in vertical section, where they produce different types of aeolian ripple lamination or stratification.

- Badain Jaran Sand Sea; from Dong et al. (2022)

## Slide 27

- Aeolian bedform Migration

- Image: Mountney et al., 2023

- Ripples, dunes and megadunes (draas) that are migrating under the influence of the airflow comprise the sediment that is in transport. Although such bedforms may be large and slow moving, over longer time scales (months, years, or longer) they can move significant distances.

- Ripples, dunes and megadunes commonly develop into a train of regularly spaced and repeated bedforms that records the response of a sand bed to the shearing action of the wind. A train of bedforms records a stage towards  dynamic equilibrium in response to a varying but consistent fluctuation in the flow pattern (e.g., Jimenez et al., 1999).

## Slide 30

- Aeolian wind-ripple strata

- Wind ripples are the most widespread ripple type developed in aeolian environments (Hunter, 1977a, b; 1981).

- They have low relief, and form preferentially from the coarser-grained fraction of the sand in transport.

- They typically have wavelengths of 50 to 200 mm and heights of 5 to 10 mm, rarely up to 30 mm.

- They exhibit a high ripple-form index (ratio of wavelength to height), typically between 12 and 50.

- They may have straight or curved (in some cases sinuous), continuous crestlines, usually oriented transverse to the local wind direction.

- Wind ripples are slightly asymmetrical in profile, though with their lee faces inclined at relatively low gradients (<20°), below the angle of repose for loose dry sand.

- Impact ripples develop through a combination of saltation and reptation.

- Left: Wind ripples on crescent-shaped sand dunes (Barchans) in Southwest Afghanistan (Sistan).

## Slide 31

- Aeolian wind ripple strata

- Image: Mountney et al., 2023

- Perturbations on a bed expressed as surface roughness influence how grains landing from saltation arrange themselves on the bed. This initiates the onset of ripple development.

- Impact ripple spacing is determined by the saltation path length, which is a function of grain size, shape and density, and mean wind velocity and gustiness close to the bed.

- Aeolian impact ripples have upwind-facing stoss slopes subject to impact of incoming grains and downwind-facing lee slopes where shadow zones develop. Modified after Anderson (1987).

## Slide 34

- Associated aeolian facies: grainflow strata

- Image: Mountney et al., 2023

- From: https://www.pamhage.com/post/2016/11/22/building-your-world-dunes

- The gravitational collapse of dry, loose sand on aeolian dune and megadune lee slopes inclined at the angle of repose (typically 32-36°) generates grainflow sand avalanches. Grainflows gather momentum as they pass down the lee slope and may run out onto lower-angle-inclined bedform plinths and flanks. Grainflow avalanches generate sediment units that are inclined at 18-36° within cross-bedded foreset deposits (the inclination is reduced by sediment compaction in ancient strata).

## Slide 42

- Aeolian bedforms: dunes

- Modified after Cain (2009)

- In situations where the net aeolian sediment budget is positive, accumulation occurs such that bedforms may climb over one another at various angles, thereby accumulating sets and cosets of cross strata, and associated bounding surfaces (Brookfield, 1977; Rubin & Hunter, 1982; Rubin, 1987a).

- These sets and cosets are themselves internally composed of smaller-scale facies units of wind-ripple, grainflow, grainfall and plane-bedded strata, the relative proportions and distributions of which are determined by how processes operated upon the formative bedforms.

- Thus, dune elements are larger-scale stratal units composed of a variety of arrangements of smaller scale bodies.

## Slide 43

- Aeolian bedforms: dunes

- Where bedforms migrate over one another, typically as part of a bedform train, they commonly climb at low angles such that the lowermost cross-bedded deposits of one bedform are accumulated as a second bedform overrides them.

- Cross strata generated by the first bedform are partly truncated by the passage of the second. The level of truncation (cut-off) generates a set of cross-strata bounded at its top by an erosional surface, a so-called bounding surface (Brookfield, 1977; Rubin & Hunter, 1982; discussed later).

## Slide 50

- Dune Migration

- Image: Mountney et al., 2023

- The accumulation of aeolian sequences does not automatically ensure their long-term preservation. Indeed, evidence from the Quaternary record (e.g., Kocurek et al., 1991a; Kocurek, 1999) argues that entire ergs may be constructed and accumulated to considerable thicknesses, only to be later deflated to leave no record. Long-term preservation requires that the body of strata is placed below a regional baseline, beneath which erosion does not occur. Thus, the rate of generation of accommodation and the rate at which aeolian accumulations fill that space is a fundamental control on preserved architectural style (Havholm & Kocurek, 1993; Howell and Mountney, 1997; Mountney   & Howell, 2000; Cosgrove et al., 2021c).

## Slide 52

- Dune migration and preservation

- The principal agent that generates accommodation and promotes preservation is subsidence.

- Long-term preservation of an aeolian succession can be achieved in many ways, but two mechanisms are widely identified.

- First, where an accumulation occurs within a subsiding basin in which accommodation is available to be filled, it can be placed beneath a so-called baseline of erosion – the level down to which deflation can proceed due to aerodynamic configuration (Kocurek & Havholm, 1993).

- Second, where an accumulation is placed beneath the water table, thereby protecting the aeolian accumulation from later deflation. This can occur either via subsidence through a static water or, on shorter geological timescales, via an absolute rise in water-table level.

- Summary

- Aeolian processes operate in a variety of erosional and depositional settings, notably hot and cold climatic deserts, coastal regions, fluvial and glacial outwash plains, and volcanic regions, and even supply sediment to the deep ocean.

- Although aeolian processes operate in various climate regimes, they are currently most active and widespread in arid, hot and cold deserts.

- Aeolian deposits occur at a variety of scales, from smallest to largest these are ripple-, dune-, and mega-dune scale

- At the smallest scale: wind-ripples, grainflow, grainfall and adhesion strata

- Aeolian dunes are associated with interdunes (dry, damp, wet), sandsheets and zibars

- Aeolian dunes and interdunes accumulate by climbing over one another.

- Overlying bedforms truncate underlying bedforms leaving cross-bedded sets in geological record
