## Slide 3

- Deep-water depositional systems

- Sedimentary systems lying predominantly below the storm wave base (10s – 100s metres).

- Topographically low – gravity wants to move particles there.

- Difficult for particles to get out once they are there.

- Therefore – deep-water depositional systems form the ultimate resting place for much of the worlds sediment, organic matter, and increasingly pollutants.

- Submarine channels around salt topography offshore Angola

**Notes:**

Sediment gravity flows entering deep marine or lacustrine basins may be confined, unconfined, or a combination of the two. Typically, through time, flows with sufficient energy will develop some degree of confinement, either through degradation of the slope profile, or through aggradation, i.e., levee development. The dynamic equilibrium profile of the slope will dictate the behaviour of the channel profile along its length (e.g., Pirmez et al., 2000; Kneller, 2003). Channels which are considered to be ‘at grade’, neither incise, nor aggrade, and are largely constrained to migrate laterally across the slope (Kneller, 2003). The interaction of sediment gravity flows with the slope over which they flow therefore has significant implications for the resultant large scale architecture of sand-bodies.
	Local topography may also exert a strong control on flows, confining flows in some cases, dictating the course of channelised flows in others (e.g. Rowan & Weimer, 1998; Weimer et al., 1998; Mayall et al., 2006; Nelson et al., 1999; Pirmez et al., 2000; Prather, 2000;). Topography generated by gravity tectonics is particularly important in many areas, such as the Gulf of Mexico (Rowan & Weimer, 1998; Gee & Gawthorpe, 2006), slump induced topography may be influential in some areas (Cronin et al., 1998), whilst folding and faulting, both in the form of pre-exisiting and syn-depositionally active features, are important in others (e.g., Soreghan et al., 1999; Broucke et al., 2004; Mayall et al., 2006).

### Turbidity Currents

- Definition: A turbidity current is a flow driven by the action of gravity upon the density excess of a sediment laden fluid within an ambient fluid. Sediment is maintained in suspension by the upwards (and chaotically) directed turbulent flow cells.

- Flows have a dominantly Newtonian rheology

- In the absence of turbulence, particles will settle from suspension

- In the absence of sediment, the flow will stop (unlike a river)

- Deposit character is directly linked to the rate of deposition, which, in turn is linked to the steadiness of the flow.

- The deposits of turbidity currents are termed Turbidites.

## Slide 36

### Debris flows

- Definition: A debris flow is a high concentration (typically >40%) mixture of fluid and sediment which flows downslope due to gravity. Particles are supported in the flow by the sediment and pore pressure in the flow.

- Flows have a dominantly non-Newtonian rheology

- Particles are supported by the flow matrix strength and pore fluid pressure which creates a buoyancy force

- Deposit character is directly linked to the flow content. Flows freeze ‘en-masse’ and the deposit represents what the flow was at that time.

- The deposits of debris flows are termed Debrites.
