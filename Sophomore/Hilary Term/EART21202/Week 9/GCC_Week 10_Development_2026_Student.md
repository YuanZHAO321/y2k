## Slide 7

- What can we do?

- We are already committed to some level of climate change, responding to climate change involves a two-pronged approach:

- Adaptation: adapting to life in a changing climate.

- The goal is to reduce our risks from the harmful effects of climate change

- It also includes making the most of any potential beneficial opportunities associated with climate change (for example, longer growing seasons or increased yields in some regions).

- Mitigation: reducing climate change.

- involves reducing the flow of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, either by reducing sources of these gases (for example, the burning of fossil fuels for electricity, heat, or transport) or enhancing the “sinks” that accumulate and store these gases (such as the oceans, forests, and soil).

## Slide 8

- Climate Engineering

- Greenhouse Gas Removal (GGR) and Solar Radiation Management (SRM) are terms describing a range of technologies that aim to counteract human-caused climate change

- This is done through deliberate large-scale intervention in the Earth’s natural systems.

- They are sometimes referred to as ‘geo-engineering’ or ‘climate engineering’.

- GGR technologies actively remove greenhouse gases from the atmosphere.

- Those that specifically remove carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere are also known as Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) technologies.

- SRM technologies reflect some of the Sun’s energy that reaches Earth back into space.

- While these would be likely to reduce the Earth’s temperature, they would not reverse ocean acidification (unlike GGRs).

## Slide 13

- Scattering, Absorbtion, and Extinction

- As EM radiation passes though the atmosphere, some is absorbed, and some is scattered.

- Scattering is when the light is redirected by reflection, refraction, and diffraction!

- There is no change in wavelength with (elastic) scattering, but it can affect radiative forcing by redirecting radiation (think reflecting back into space).

- Extinction is the sum of scattering and absorbtion.

## Slide 20

### Cloud droplets form on condensation nuclei
If a parcel of air is super-saturated, then water molecules will start to condense on any surface within that area.
If there is a nucleus for this water vapour, a cloud droplet is formed.
If there is a limit of nuclei, then water vapour will condense on existing water droplets

- Water Vapour

- Aerosol particle as condensation nuclei

## Slide 21

### Small number of nuclei means all the water vapour is concentrated on a small number of particles.
Leads to a smaller number of bigger drops, which causes more transmission and absorption of light.

- Large number of nuclei means the water vapour is more spread out, which leads to a large number of smaller droplets.

- Leads to more reflection of light, as discussed earlier.

## Slide 22

### Marine cloud brightening (MCB) exploits this interaction of EM theory with cloud physics in order to modulate the brightness of clouds.
John Latham et al (formerly part of UMIST, merged with UoM) originally proposed this in 1990.
Picture to the right is from his original paper and is an artistic impression of the concept.
The idea is to seed new clouds over the ocean, and increase the bightness of existing ones.
This is done over the ocean because there is lots of water vapour, and marine stratocumulus clouds often contain large droplets with low number concentration. There is also a good source of aerosol in the form of sea salt spray!

- Marine Cloud Brightening

## Slide 23

- Unforeseen Consequences #1!

- The potential consequences of MCB are mostly generally that of [SRM] geoengineering generally as opposed to physical and scientific constraints with the methodology. This is because small scale testing can be conducted and quickly stopped if there are issues.

- There are issues surrounding the social perception of geoengineering generally.

- Modifying the clouds in this way leads to lower precipitation rates, because clouds need the larger droplets to trigger rain. This is less of an issue over the ocean but worth keeping in mind for later!

- It is also possible that MCB could be used as an excuse to continue greenhouse gas pollution, and enhance a relliance on the technology.

- Should MCB be stopped for any reason, the step change in radiative forcing would be huge and immediate, and likely not give ecosystems any chance for change.

- It should be stressed that a reduction in radiative forcing is not exactly equal to a reduction in greenhouse gas emmissions, mearly a mitigation thereof.

## Slide 24

- Case Study: Global Shipping

- In 2020, fuel regulations abruptly reduced the emission of sulfur dioxide from international shipping by about 80%.

- These regulations were put in place because sulphur emmissions are the cause of a large number of deaths per year.

- This reversed an accidental SRM geoeingineering effect.

- The result of this is a net radiative forcing impact of about +0.2W/m2 (Yuan et al 2024).

- There are other reasons why you would want to ban sulphur emmissions (human health impact).

### The stratosphere is the region of the atmosphere above the troposphere.
Due to the absorption of em radiation by Ozone, the temperature of the stratosphere increases with altitude.
Inversions will keep aerosol suspended within them since the cold air cannot rise and disperse the aerosol.

## Slide 30

### Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) is a proposed method whereby aerosol (usually SO2) is dispersed into the stratosphere above the stratospheric inversion.
The origins of SAI are debated but the earliest solid reference is P.J. Crutzen in 2006 https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-006-9101-y where it was postulated as a worst-case scenario if GHGs cannot be controlled.
The stratospheric inversion keeps the aerosol suspended within it, keeping the cost of SAI very low.
Compared to MCB, this is far less of an engineering challenge.

## Slide 31

- Unforeseen Consequences #2!

- Unlike with MCB, a lot of the issues with SAI are scientific in nature, in addition to the general issues with [SRM] geoengineering.

- SO2 will react with Ozone in the stratosphere and destroy it, potentially leading to a larger hole in the Ozone layer.

- The SO2 aerosol will eventually descend from the stratosphere, causing huge human health impact.

- SO2 will also interact with clouds and have a brightening effect and resultant rainfall reducation, however this rainfall reduction might not be limited to over the ocean.

- Aerosol in the stratosphere could nucleate ice, which would have the opposite effect since ice clouds have positive radiative forcing.

- Aerosol above the stratospheric inversion takes a long time (approximately 5 years) to return to the surface, complicating small scale tests and limiting them to lab and model based studies.

- Promises of future carbon removal later instead of reducing emissions now are essentially a huge gamble with future generations paying the cost

## Slide 40

- Forests and better management

**Notes:**

The first method I want to talk about is growing forests and woodlands. This is perhaps the simplest of the removal methods.

When trees grow they naturally absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.
It takes a forest about 10 years to ramp up to absorbing the maximum amount of carbon it can
And after 20-100 years the forest reaches what we call maturity, and the amount of carbon it can absorb starts to slow down

We can grow more forests and woodlands – and we can manage our forests better.
e.g. there are ways to remove some tree growth to make room for other trees to grow more successfully. This allows you to increase the overall amount of carbon that can be stored in that forest

**Notes:**

The second method I want to talk about is peatlands and wetlands. Like forests this is another natural way of doing greenhouse gas removals.

Peatlands and wetlands are the damp, wet natural habits that we find all around the UK. These habits naturally absorb a lot of carbon. But if they dry out then they release that carbon back into the atmosphere.

What that means is that if we want to use these habitats as a way of removing greenhouse gases from the atmosphere then we need to keep them wet. Often that means restoring these peatlands and wetlands because agricultural practices have deliberately dried them out over time to create farmland.

**Notes:**

The next method I’m going to talk about is enhancing the storage of carbon in the soil.

Again, like with forests, peatlands and wetlands, the soil naturally stores carbon.

How much carbon the soil can store is affected by:
How often we disturb the soil (e.g. how often we plough it with machines)
How much organic material is in the soil. Organic material is things that were living, like plants, that are now dead. So those of you who have gardens may have a compost heap where you put garden waste (hedge trimmings etc). You let that decay a bit and then you dig that back into the soil. That’s organic material.

If we want the soil to be able to store more carbon, we need to disturb it less and it ensure it has more organic materials in it. This is really about changing farming practices:
Changing ways that crops are grown
Reducing how often we plough the soil with machines
Using natural fertilizers rather than artificial ones

## Slide 52

- Lecture Summary

- Climate engineering involves large-scale interventions to counter climate change.

- Two main approaches:

- GGR/CDR: removes CO₂ (addresses root cause)

- SRM: reflects sunlight to cool the planet (does not reduce CO₂)

- Techniques like MCB and SAI can lower temperatures by increasing albedo, but come with major uncertainties.

- Key issues:

- Environmental risks

- Termination shock

- Ethical and governance challenges

- Climate engineering may help limit warming, but cannot replace emissions reductions.

- Mitigation must remain the priority, with climate engineering only as a potential supplement.
