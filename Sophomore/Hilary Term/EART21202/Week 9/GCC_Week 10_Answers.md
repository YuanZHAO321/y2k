## Slide 3

- Q1.

- Is it more effective to prioritise immediate emissions reductions or invest heavily in future technologies like carbon removal? Why?

## Slide 4

- Q1. Answer

- Is it more effective to prioritise immediate emissions reductions or invest heavily in future technologies like carbon removal? Why?

- In most cases, prioritising immediate emissions reductions is more effective because it directly limits cumulative CO₂ emissions, which determines long-term warming.

- Delaying action increases reliance on uncertain future technologies that in many cases are not yet fully developed at the required scale or cost. However, some investment in carbon removal is still necessary to deal with residual emissions (e.g. aviation, agriculture) that cannot be reduced to zero. The strongest approach is a combination, but with clear priority on cutting emissions now.

## Slide 5

- Q2.

- Should Solar Radiation Management (SRM) technologies be researched or deployed, even though they do not address root causes like CO₂ emissions or ocean acidification?

## Slide 6

- Q2. Answer

- Should Solar Radiation Management (SRM) technologies be researched or deployed, even though they do not address root causes like CO₂ emissions or ocean acidification?

- SRM should be researched but not deployed at scale (yet). We have however seen some small scale deployments that have had positive impacts, for example in the Great Barrier Reef.

- Research helps us understand risks, governance, potential emergency use or long-term reliance. However, deployment is controversial because SRM does not solve underlying problems and could create unintended climate side effects or political/social tensions. It risks becoming a “quick fix” that reduces motivation for mitigation.

## Slide 7

- Q3.

- Which NETs (e.g. afforestation, BECCS, DACCS) do you think are most viable for large-scale deployment in the UK, and why?

## Slide 8

- Q3. Answer

- Which NETs (e.g. afforestation, BECCS, DACCS) do you think are most viable for large-scale deployment in the UK, and why?

- Afforestation/ecosystem restoration: Most immediately viable due to low cost and existing knowledge but limited by land availability and slow carbon uptake.

- BECCS: Potentially significant in the UK due to existing infrastructure but constrained by land use and sustainability/safety concerns.

- DACCS: Promising long-term due to scalability and minimal land conflict, but currently prohibitively expensive and energy-intensive.

- Overall, a suite of approaches is most realistic and beneficial rather than relying on a single solution.

## Slide 9

- Q4.

- Should governments invest heavily in NET development now, despite high costs and uncertainty, or wait until technologies mature?

## Slide 10

- Q4. Answer

- Should governments invest heavily in NET development now, despite high costs and uncertainty, or wait until technologies mature?

- Governments should invest now, but cautiously and strategically with a particular focus on research.

- Early investment helps drive innovation, reduce costs, and build infrastructure. However, this should not come at the expense of immediate mitigation efforts.

- The key is to avoid overreliance—NETs should complement, not replace, emissions reductions.
