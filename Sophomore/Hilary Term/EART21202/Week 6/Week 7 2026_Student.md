## Slide 9

- Global Climate Models

- We have covered the main concepts that feed into computer models to simulate and predict climate change. We now look at how these models work and their results.

- The concept of radiative forcing is central to climate models.

- Climate models project climate (the average weather over a long period of time, e.g., a 30-year period), not weather (what an area experiences on an hourly or daily basis).

- Models help us understand how our actions can affect the future climate.

- They allow modelers to look at different scenarios of increased greenhouse gas emissions and see how increases may affect us

## Slide 11

- Global Climate Models

- The size and complexity of the Earth makes this challenging

- Different places have different responses to energy (e.g., land warms up and cools down faster than oceans).

- We divide Earth into a 3D grid across the surface, up into the atmosphere, and down into the ocean.

- Each cube is analysed individually over time while receiving information (inputs) from and passing information (outputs) to neighbouring cubes.

- The size of the cubes varies among models

- A higher resolution model has more, smaller cubes and provides a more accurate picture of how changes to the climate impact an area.

## Slide 13

- History of Climate Models – 90s – 00s

- Atmosphere only Model

- Earlier models calculated physical processes for the atmosphere, land, ocean, and sea ice components separately.

- Initially GCMs treated atmosphere and ocean models as separate entities.

- Atmosphere models focused on simulating atmospheric dynamics, while ocean models represented oceanic circulation and thermodynamics.

- These separate models were run independently and their outputs were not directly interconnected.

- Coupling atmosphere and ocean models allowed for the interaction to be simulated.

- These now allow feedback loops between the atmosphere and ocean, such as the influence of ocean currents on atmospheric circulation patterns and vice versa.

- Thus capturing complex interactions and feedback mechanisms.

- Can capture phenomena such as El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) which are crucial for understanding global climate variability.

- GCMs can now produce more realistic climate projections on both regional and global scales.

## Slide 17

- Resolution of Global Climate Models

- Initially, GCMs had resolutions of 250 to 600 km horizontally, with limited vertical layers.

- Improved computing power allowed for higher resolutions, enabling better representation of small-scale processes.

- Higher resolutions are crucial for capturing regional climate variations accurately.

- Challenges remain, such as parameterizing small-scale processes like clouds, leading to uncertainties in future climate projections.

- GCMs play a vital role in understanding and predicting the Earth's climate response to increasing greenhouse gas concentrations.

- This also requires a huge amount of computational power, not possible on personal computers

## Slide 20

- The Hadley Centre – Third Coupled Model

- The Hadley Centre Third Coupled Model (HadCM3) was developed by the UK Met Office's Hadley Centre for Climate Prediction and Research.

- HadCM3 was a pioneering global climate model that integrated atmosphere, ocean, sea ice, and land surface components.

- It played a significant role in climate research and was widely used in studies assessing the impacts of greenhouse gas emissions and anthropogenic activities on global climate.

- HadCM3 contributed to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessments, providing valuable insights into future climate scenarios and informing international climate policy discussions.

## Slide 22

- Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change

- The First Assessment Report (FAR) was published by the IPCC in 1990.

- It represented the first comprehensive assessment of climate change science, impacts, and potential mitigation strategies.

- FAR played a crucial role in raising global awareness about the issue of climate change.

- The Second Assessment Report (SAR) was released in 1995.

- It provided an updated and more detailed understanding of climate change science

- The SAR also highlighted emerging issues such as the potential impacts of climate change on vulnerable regions.

- important material for governments to draw from in the run-up to adoption of the Kyoto Protocol in 1997

- The Third Assessment Report (TAR) was published in 2001

- assessments of observed changes in climate, projections of future climate scenarios, and the potential impacts of climate change on various sectors such as agriculture, water resources, and human health.

- The TAR contributed significantly to international climate negotiations and policy discussions.

- The Fourth Assessment Report (AR4) was released in 2007.

- Provided an updated assessment of climate change science, impacts, adaptation, and mitigation strategies.

- The AR4 incorporated advancements in climate modeling, observational data, and understanding of climate feedback mechanisms.

- laid the ground-work for a post-Kyoto agreement, focusing on limiting warming to 2°C

## Slide 24

- Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs)

- Anthropogenic GHG emissions are mainly driven by population size, economic activity, lifestyle, energy use, land use patterns, technology and climate policy.

- The Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), which are used for making projections based on these factors, describe four different 21st century pathways of GHG emissions and atmospheric concentrations, air pollutant emissions and land use.

- The RCPs include a stringent mitigation scenario (RCP2.6), two intermediate scenarios (RCP4.5 and RCP6.0) and one scenario with very high GHG emissions (RCP8.5). Scenarios without additional efforts to constrain emissions (’baseline scenarios’) lead to pathways ranging between RCP6.0 and RCP8.5.

- RCP2.6 is representative of a scenario that aims to keep global warming likely below 2°C above pre-industrial temperatures. The RCPs are consistent with the wide range of scenarios in the literature as assessed by WGIII5.

## Slide 27

- Modelling Anthropogenic Impacts

- How would such a model simulation be carried out? The following protocol is typical.

- 1. A GCM model is run for many decades with no external forcing

- 2. The statistics of the run (mean climate, annual cycles and variability on a range of time scales) are then compared with observation to assess performance.

- 3. The model is then re-run with changes in forcing (such as greenhouse radiative forcing).

- 4. The difference in the two climates provides an estimate of the consequent climate change arising form that forcing factor.

## Slide 33

- Impacts: Increase in Natural Disasters

- Global warming has been linked to an increase in the frequency, intensity, and severity of various types of natural disasters.

- Heatwaves: Rising temperatures have led to more frequent and intense heatwaves. Heatwaves can have serious health implications, leading to heat-related illnesses and deaths, particularly among vulnerable populations.

- Extreme Weather Events: GW can contribute to the intensification of extreme weather events such as hurricanes, typhoons, and cyclones. Warmer ocean temperatures provide more energy to fuel these storms, leading to increased wind speeds and heavier rainfall

- Flooding: Higher temperatures accelerate the melting of glaciers and ice caps, leading to rising sea levels. This, coupled with increased precipitation intensity, can result in more frequent and severe flooding events

- Droughts: Changes in precipitation patterns and increased evaporation rates due to higher temperatures can contribute to more frequent and prolonged droughts. Droughts can have significant impacts on agriculture, water resources, and ecosystems and loss of biodiversity.

- Wildfires: Warmer temperatures, coupled with changes in precipitation patterns, can create conditions conducive to the spread of wildfires. Drier vegetation and increased fire risk can lead to larger and more frequent wildfires, posing threats to human health, property, and ecosystems.

## Slide 35

- Lecture Summary

- Global Climate Models (GCMs) simulate the Earth’s climate by representing interactions between the atmosphere, oceans, land, and sea ice to understand long-term climate behaviour.

- The Earth is divided into a 3-D grid, where variables such as temperature, pressure, wind, and water vapour are calculated and exchanged between grid cells. Higher resolution improves accuracy but requires computational power.

- Modern models are coupled Earth system models, linking atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, biosphere, and atmospheric chemistry to capture important climate feedbacks.

- Models are tested using hindcasting, where simulations of past climate are compared with observations to assess reliability.

- Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) describe possible future greenhouse-gas emission scenarios used to project warming and changes in precipitation and extreme events.

- Future climate models are expected to use higher resolution, improved observational data, and approaches such as AI and machine learning to better represent complex processes and reduce uncertainties in climate projections.
