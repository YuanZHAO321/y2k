## Slide 5

- Climate Change

- Climate change is one of the most complex issues facing us today.

- We have so far focused on the environmental impact

- It involves many dimensions – science, economics, society, politics, and moral and ethical questions – and is a global problem, felt on local scales

- Carbon dioxide has a lifetime of hundreds of years, so the planet (especially the ocean) takes a while to respond to warming.

- So even if we stopped emitting all greenhouse gases today, global warming and climate change will continue to affect future generations.

- In this way, humanity is “committed” to some level of climate change.

## Slide 6

- How much climate change?

- That will be determined by how our emissions continue and exactly how our climate responds to those emissions.

- Despite increasing awareness of climate change, our emissions of greenhouse gases continue to rise

- In 2013, the daily level of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere surpassed 400 parts per million for the first time in human history.

## Slide 7

- Representative Concentration Pathways

- Anthropogenic GHG emissions are mainly driven by population size, economic activity, lifestyle, energy use, land use patterns, technology and climate policy.

- The RCPs include a stringent mitigation scenario (RCP2.6)

- two intermediate scenarios (RCP4.5 and RCP6.0)

- very high GHG emissions (RCP8.5).

- Scenarios without additional efforts to constrain emissions (’baseline scenarios’) lead to pathways ranging between RCP6.0 and RCP8.5.

- RCP2.6 is representative of a scenario that aims to keep global warming likely below 2°C above pre-industrial temperatures.

## Slide 23

- Projections of Future Change: Sea Level

- Warming temperatures contribute to sea level rise by:

- expanding ocean water (well understood)

- melting mountain glaciers and ice caps (well understood)

- the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets to melt or flow into the ocean (less well understood, account for 1.2 to 8 inches of sea level rise)

- Since 1870, global sea level has risen by about 7.5 inches

- Global sea level for the next century is expected to rise at a greater rate than during the past 50 years.

- Studies project global sea level to rise by another 1 to 4 feet by 2100

- Large uncertainty about how glaciers and ice sheets will react to climate change

- Regional and local factors will influence future relative sea level rise for specific coastlines around the world.

- Relative sea level rise also depends on local changes in currents, winds, salinity, and water temperatures, as well as proximity to thinning ice sheets.

## Slide 24

- Projections of Future Change: Sea Level

- Sea level rise is the dominant driver of increased coastal flood risk, both historically and in future projections of climate change.

- Individual UK flood events are usually associated with extreme weather conditions, particularly the generation of storm surges, which can be compounded by the action of wind-generated surface waves.

- However, projections of changes in frequency and severity of future coastal extreme water levels are dominated by the long-term increase in coastal water levels associated with climate change

- One of the main outcomes of the research was the demonstration that UK sea levels will continue to rise well beyond 2100 under all future emissions scenarios

## Slide 25

- Projections of Future Change: Ocean Acidification

- Ocean acidification is a consequence of increasing carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions

- When CO2 dissolves in seawater, it forms carbonic acid (H2CO3)

- Acidity plays a key role in many biological mechanisms, including calcification.

- Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is crucial for organisms which need calcium to develop, build and maintain their shells and skeletons, such as certain types of plankton, oysters, crabs, sea urchins, shrimps and lobsters.

- Ocean acidification makes it harder for them to maintain these calcified structures. This can cause disruptions within food chains.

- Ocean acidification can also impact the ability of coral reefs to recover from warming and other stressors, by making it harder for corals to build their calcium-based skeletons.

- If CO2 concentrations continue to rise at their current rate, the combination of climate warming and ocean acidification could slow coral growth by nearly 50% by 2050.

- Ocean acidification impacts both small coastal communities and big industries. By harming aquaculture and tourism, communities that rely on the ocean as a source of food and income will be particularly affected.

## Slide 26

- Projections of Future Change: Sea Ice

- Arctic sea ice is already declining.

- The area of snow cover in the Northern Hemisphere has decreased since about 1970.

- Permafrost temperatures in Alaska and much of the Arctic have increased over the last century.

- It is expected that sea ice will continue to decline, glaciers will continue to shrink, snow cover will continue to decrease, and permafrost will continue to thaw.

- The coastal sections of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are expected to continue to melt or slide into the ocean.

- If the rate of this ice melting increases in the 21st century, the ice sheets could add significantly to global sea level rise.

- Glaciers are expected to continue to decrease in size. The rate of melting is expected to continue to increase, which will contribute to sea level rise.

## Slide 40

- What can we do?

- Adaptation: adapting to life in a changing climate.

- involves adjusting to actual or expected future climate.

- The goal is to reduce our risks from the harmful effects of climate change (like sea-level rise, more intense extreme weather events, or food insecurity). It also includes making the most of any potential beneficial opportunities associated with climate change (for example, longer growing seasons or increased yields in some regions).

- Mitigation: reducing climate change.

- involves reducing the flow of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, either by reducing sources of these gases (for example, the burning of fossil fuels for electricity, heat, or transport) or enhancing the “sinks” that accumulate and store these gases (such as the oceans, forests, and soil).

## Slide 41

- What do we mean by mitigation?

- From IPCC WG3 introduction.

- “Climate change mitigation is achieved by limiting or preventing greenhouse gas emissions and by enhancing activities that remove these gases from the atmosphere.”

- And on solar radiation management:

- “SRM contrasts with climate-change mitigation activities, such as emission reductions and CO2 removal, as it introduces a ‘mask’ to the climate change problem by altering the Earth’s radiation budget, rather than attempting to address the root cause of the problem, which is the increase in greenhouse gas emissions in the atmosphere.”

## Slide 44

- Regional Climate Models

- ‘Regional downscaling’

- Run a climate model only over a specific region, but at a higher spatial resolution

- Global Climate Models (GCM) offer future climate projections, serving as a primary driver for international decisions on climate mitigation.

- Despite GCM projections, the impacts of climate change and necessary strategies will manifest at regional and national scales.

- Downscaling methods address the spatial scale disparity between GCMs and the granularity required by decision-makers and impact assessors.

- GCMs provide reliable predictions covering vast landscapes with diverse topographies and varying risks of extreme events.

- Downscaling derives local to regional scale information from larger-scale models, enhancing the specificity and applicability of climate projections.

## Slide 53

- The Decision-making Context

- Iterative risk management recommended for complex situations

## Slide 54

- Examples where this has worked…

- Tulsa, Oklahoma

- In 1974, victims of a major flood in Tulsa, Oklahoma, began organising to pressure the city government to reduce flood damage.

- Gradually the city improved its technical capabilities, organizational structure and funding for flood control.

- After another major flood in 1984, the city banned development in floodplains and began relocating more than a thousand buildings away from them.

- By 1992, Tulsa had earned the lowest flood insurance rates in the US

- In 1998, Tulsa Partners began creating public-private partnerships for the mitigation of other extreme events such as droughts, ice storms and tornadoes.

- Since then, the city has continued to make progress toward its long-term goal, a disaster-resistant and sustainable community.

## Slide 55

- Maladaptation

- Maladaptation is where your adaptation approach:

- Increases emissions of greenhouse gases

- Disproportionately burdens the most vulnerable

- Has high opportunity costs

- Reduces incentives to adapt or mitigate

- Sets paths that limit the choices available to future generations

## Slide 56

- Maladaptation

- In early 2012, the UK government offered incentives to motorists to encourage them to switch to diesel vehicles as part of their strategy.

- The Department for Transport (DfT) acknowledges that diesel vehicles have significantly lower CO2 emissions per kilometre travelled due to their higher efficiency.

- Trade-off: CO2 vs. NOx Emissions: While new generation diesel vehicles emit lower levels of carbon dioxide compared to petrol engines, they tend to emit higher levels of nitrogen oxides (NOx), particularly in urban areas.

- Impact on Air Pollution: Diesel engines, especially older models, are often cited as a significant contributor to roadside air pollution in built-up urban areas, primarily due to their emissions of NOx.

- Government Measures to Discourage Diesel Use: In response to environmental concerns, the government has introduced extra charges and initiatives aimed at weaning the public off diesel vehicles, reflecting a broader strategy to reduce air pollution and mitigate climate change.

## Slide 57

- Lecture Summary

- Climate change is already driving global impacts on temperature, precipitation, sea level, ecosystems, and human health.

- Impacts vary by region, with some areas experiencing more extreme heat, rainfall, or vulnerability than others.

- Future climate change depends on emission pathways (RCPs) and how the climate system responds.

- Higher emissions lead to more severe and widespread impacts, including extreme weather events.

- Adaptation focuses on managing risks locally using projections, risk assessment, and planning.

- Mitigation targets the root cause by reducing greenhouse gas emissions and enhancing carbon sinks.

- Both approaches are needed together to effectively respond to current and future climate risks.
