### **Eccentricity**

- Eccentricity refers to the shape of Earth's orbit around the Sun, specifically how elongated or circular it is.

- Earth's orbit is not perfectly circular but slightly elliptical.

- Eccentricity varies cyclically over time, with periods typically ranging from **tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years.**

- They are primarily driven by the gravitational interactions between Earth and the gas giant planets, particularly Jupiter and Saturn.

### **Eccentricity**

- When Earth’s orbit is at its most elliptic, about 23 percent more incoming solar radiation reaches Earth at our planet’s closest approach to the Sun each year than does at its farthest departure from the Sun.

- Currently, Earth’s eccentricity is very slowly decreasing and is approaching its least elliptic (most circular), in a cycle that spans about 100,000 years.

- The minimum and maximum eccentricities during each 100,000 years also vary.

- Because variations in Earth’s eccentricity are fairly small, they’re a relatively minor factor in annual seasonal climate variations.

### **_Obliquity_**

   - Obliquity refers to the tilt of Earth's axis relative to its orbital plane around the Sun.

-

   - Earth's axis is currently tilted at an angle of approximately 23.5 degrees relative to its orbital plane.

-

   - This is not constant; it varies between approximately 22.1 and 24.5 degrees

-

- Over a cycle lasting about 41,000 years.

   - Changes in obliquity affect the distribution of solar radiation across Earth's surface, leading to changes in the intensity and seasonality of climate patterns.

### **_Obliquity_**

The obliquity of the Earth’s axis of rotation is the angle of the axis relative to the line perpendicular to the plane of the Earth’s orbit around the sun Greater tilt = greater difference between seasons Lesser Tilt  = less of a difference between seasons

### **_Obliquity_**

At the moment the obliquity is 23.5<sup>o</sup> , in the middle of the cycle.

When the obliquity is low (22<sup>o</sup> ) more sunlight intercepts the equator, increases the N-S temperature contrast.

When the obliquity is high (24.5<sup>o</sup> ) more sunlight reaches higher latitudes, reducing temperature contrasts.

The obliquity therefore affects the seasons and temperature at each latitude.

The change in Earth's obliquity, or axial tilt, is primarily driven by gravitational interactions between Earth and the Moon

### **_Milankovitch Cycles – The Theory_**

- Milankovitch developed a mathematical model combining orbital cycles to calculate changes in radiation by latitude and resulting surface temperatures

- The model works like a _climate time machine_ ,

- allowing climate conditions to be explored backward and forward in time

- Early calculations suggested Ice Ages occurred roughly every **41,000 years**

- Ice Age pacing later shifted (~800,000 years ago) to a **100,000-year cycle** , matching Earth’s eccentricity

- • The reason for this shift is still not fully understood

### **_Milankovitch Cycles – Verification_**

- Initially overlooked, Milankovitch’s theory gained recognition after his death

- **1976 Science study (Hays et al.)** linked orbital cycles to climate change using deep-sea sediment cores

- Temperature records reconstructed for the past **450,000 years**

- Ice Ages coincided with specific stages of Earth’s orbital variation

- Ice-core data from Greenland and Antarctica further confirmed the cycles

- Milankovitch cycles are now widely accepted as drivers of glacial–interglacial timing

### **_Is the sun causing global warming?_**

Since 1978, we have been tracking the amount of solar energy that hits the top of the atmosphere using sensors on satellites, which tell us that there has been no upward trend in the amount of solar energy reaching our planet.

If the Sun were responsible for global warming, we would expect to see warming throughout all layers of the atmosphere, from the surface to the upper atmosphere (stratosphere).

But what we actually see is warming at the surface and cooling in the stratosphere. This is consistent with the warming being caused by a buildup of heat-trapping gases near Earth's surface, and not by the Sun getting “hotter.”

Since 1750, the warming driven by greenhouse gases coming from the human burning of fossil fuels is over 270 times greater than the slight extra warming coming from the Sun itself over that same time interval.

##### **_The ENSO (El Nino/Southern Oscillation)_**

What is ENSO?

A coherent, large-scale fluctuation of ocean temperatures, rainfall, atmospheric circulation and air pressure across the tropical Pacific. It is a coupled ocean-atmosphere phenomenon.

ENSO is one of the most important climate phenomena on Earth due to its ability to change the global atmospheric circulation, which in turn, influences temperature and precipitation across the globe.

##### **_The ENSO (El Nino/Southern Oscillation)_**

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is a recurring climate pattern involving changes in the temperature of waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean.

On periods ranging from about three to seven years, the surface waters across the tropical Pacific Ocean warm or cool by anywhere from 1°C to 3°C, compared to normal.

This oscillating warming and cooling pattern, referred to as the ENSO cycle, directly affects rainfall distribution in the tropics and can have a strong influence on weather across the United States and other parts of the world.

El Niño and La Niña are the extreme phases of the ENSO cycle; between these two phases is a third phase called ENSO-neutral.

The scale of these fluctuations is vast, changes in sea-surface temperatures often span a distance of more than one-quarter the circumference of the globe, and changes in tropical rainfall and winds span a distance of more than one-half the circumference of the earth.

##### **_The ENSO (El Nino/Southern Oscillation)_**

• **_<u>El Niño:</u>_** A warming of the ocean surface in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. Over Indonesia, rainfall tends to become reduced while rainfall increases over the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. During a strong El Niño episode, ocean temperatures can locally average 2°C - 5°C above normal

- **_<u>La Niña:</u>_** A cooling of the ocean surface. Over Indonesia, rainfall tends

- to increase while rainfall decreases over the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. During a strong La Niña episode ocean temperatures locally average 1°C – 4°below normal

- **_<u>Neutral:</u>_** Neither El Niño or La Niña. Often tropical Pacific ocean temps

- are generally close to average. However, there are some instances when the ocean can look like it is in an El Niño or La Niña state, but the atmosphere is not playing along (or vice versa).

During a strong El Niño episode, ocean temperatures can locally average 2°C - 5°C above normal between the date line and the west coast of South America, resulting in nearly uniform surface temperatures across the entire equatorial Pacific.

### **_The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI)_**

**The SOI** is one measure of the large-scale fluctuations in air pressure occurring between the western and eastern tropical Pacific (i.e., the state of the southern Oscillation) during El Niño and La Niña episodes. This index has been calculated based on the differences in air pressure anomaly between Tahiti and Darwin, Australia.

### **_Key Points of Natural Variability_**

**1. Solar Variability** : Changes in the sun's energy output can influence Earth's climate. Solar cycles, which include variations in the sun's radiation and magnetic activity, can affect the amount of solar energy reaching the Earth.

**2. Orbital Variations** : Changes in Earth's orbit and axial tilt over long timescales, known as Milankovitch cycles, can affect the distribution and intensity of solar radiation received by the Earth, leading to changes in climate over tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years.

**3. Volcanic Activity** : Large volcanic eruptions can inject significant amounts of aerosol and sulphur dioxide into the atmosphere, leading to temporary cooling by blocking sunlight. These eruptions can cause short-term fluctuations in global temperatures.

**4. Ocean Circulation:** Ocean currents play a crucial role in redistributing heat around the Earth. Variations in ocean circulation patterns, such as El Niño and La Niña events in the Pacific Ocean, can have significant impacts on regional and global climate patterns.

### **_Key Points of Natural Variability_**

#### **Lecture Summary**

- Earth’s climate varies naturally over **short (years–decades)** and **long (thousands–100,000s of years)** timescales

- Natural variability arises from interactions within the **Sun–Earth system, atmosphere, oceans, cryosphere, and biosphere**

- Key drivers include **orbital variations (Milankovitch cycles), solar variability, volcanic activity, and ocean circulation**

- Milankovitch cycles (eccentricity, obliquity, precession) change the **distribution and seasonality of insolation** , driving ice ages

- **Ice cores and marine sediments** provide evidence of past temperature and greenhouse gas changes over hundreds of thousands of years

- Solar variability influences climate on **decadal timescales** , but shows no long-term increase in recent decades

- Volcanic eruptions cause **short-term global cooling** through sulphate aerosols in the stratosphere

- **ENSO** drives major interannual climate variability, influencing global weather patterns

- Natural variability cannot explain the **magnitude or rate of modern global warming** , which is dominated by anthropogenic forcing
