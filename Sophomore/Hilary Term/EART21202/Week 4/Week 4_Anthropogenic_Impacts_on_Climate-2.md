**Notes:**

Aerosols influence the climate system through multiple pathways.
Firstly, aerosols can directly affect the Earth’s energy balance by interacting with solar radiation. This process is known as aerosol-radiation interaction or the direct effect.
Non-absorbing aerosols, like sulfate, nitrate and most of the organic aerosols, can scatter sunlight back into space. This increases the Earth's albedo (reflectivity) and has a cooling effect on the climate.
Some aerosols can absorb solar radiation, warm the surrounding air and contributes to a warming climate effect. The best-known type of absorbing aerosol is black carbon. Certain types of organic aerosols are also absorbing, which is called brown carbon.
The overall direct effects of aerosols depend on their size, composition and optical properties. Their location in the atmosphere also plays a crucial role. For example, if an elevated absorbing aerosol layer is above low-lying clouds, it will have a stronger warming effect compared to being over a clear sky. This is because the absorbing aerosol layers can also absorb the solar radiation reflected by the underlying clouds.

- Emission of particles,

**Notes:**

Aerosols also influence climate by interaction with clouds. Clouds cover roughly two-thirds of the Earth’s surface. They can modify Earth’s top of the atmosphere (TOA) radiation budget by reflecting incoming solar radiation, thereby enhancing the earth’s albedo
Aerosols can affect climate system by modifying cloud microphysics and lifetime, which is called “indirect effect”. They can act as Cloud Condensation Nuclei (CCN), providing surfaces for water vapor to condense and form cloud droplets. The aerosol ability to become a CCN is dependent on its hygroscopic nature, which is affected by aerosol size and composition. Larger aerosol particles are generally more efficient CCN. Inorganic aerosols like sulfate and nitrate are generally more hygroscopic than carbonaceous aerosols.
More aerosols or CCN typically lead to more cloud droplets with smaller droplet size. In this way, clouds become more reflective and enhance their cooling effect. The smaller size of cloud droplets also increases their time remaining in the cloud, suppressing the precipitation. This can extend cloud lifetime, further enhancing their cooling effect.
The rapid adjustments to atmospheric thermodynamics due to aerosols, is commonly referred as “semi-direct effect”. This can also affect cloud amounts. For example, when absorbing aerosols, such as black carbon, immersed in or surround cloud droplets, they can absorb lights, heat the surrounding air and evaporate clouds, thus reducing cloud cooling effect.
Above is a brief introduction of how aerosols influence the climate system through interaction with radiation and clouds. the next question is how we can assess their climate effects.

**Notes:**

A key metric used to assess the climate effects of a forcer is Effective Radiative Forcing (ERF), which quantifies the change in the net energy flux at the top of the atmosphere (TOA) due to a climate forcer. It accounts for the instantaneous change in radiation, for example, the direct scattering and absorption of incoming solar radiation by aerosols. It also accounts for the subsequent rapid adjustments. Here, rapid adjustments represent immediate changes in the atmosphere and surface in response to a climate forcer, normally taking place over short timescales from days to months. These adjustments involve processes such as changes in clouds and water vapor. For example, when the increased aerosols lead to more cloud droplets with smaller sizes, this delays precipitation, leading to an increase in cloud fraction, Such adjustments could enhance the cooling effect. Additionally, the semi-direct effects we talked earlier, the absorbing aerosols change the thermodynamics, heat the air, and dry the clouds, Such adjustments could reduce the cooling effect. By considering both instantaneous radiative effects and rapid adjustments, ERF provides a more comprehensive assessment of how aerosols and other climate forcers alter the Earth’s energy balance. Positive ERF values indicate a warming effect and negative ERF values indicate a cooling effect.
In the recent IPCC report, the assessment of aerosol ERF employs observation- and model-based estimates. Observational estimates include satellite -based measurements. Model estimates include simulations using global climate models and Earth System models. The ERF relative to the Year 1750 is regarded as anthropogenic influence over the industrial era. From the IPCC report, the global average ERF for aerosol-radiation interaction and aerosol-cloud interaction for 2014 relative to 1750 are assessed to be –0.3 W m–2 and –1.0 W m–2, respectively. The total aerosol ERF is assessed to be –1.3 W m–2. There is strong evidence for a substantial global cooling effect of anthropogenic aerosols.

Where are the uncertainties from?
We have introduced that aerosol are short-lived species and unevenly distributed. It is challenging to capture spatial and temporal variations in aerosol concentrations accurately.
We have also introduced that aerosol interaction with clouds and radiation are dependent on aerosol physicochemical properties. However, characterizing these properties across different environments remains uncertain.
There are also limitations with existing observational and modelling techniques.
Improving our understanding of aerosol climate effects requires continued observational and modelling research in the future.

## Slide 38

- Lecture Summary

- Human activities alter Earth’s climate by changing the balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing infrared radiation through aerosols, ozone, and land-use change.

- Aerosols (days–weeks lifetime) mostly cool the climate via increased albedo (direct reflection and cloud effects), making them the largest non-CO₂ anthropogenic forcing.

- Tropospheric ozone (weeks lifetime) acts as a short-lived greenhouse gas, warming the climate by absorbing both UV and infrared radiation.

- Land-use change modifies surface albedo, generally producing a net cooling with strong regional variation.

- Overall, these “other” anthropogenic forcings are shorter-lived than CO₂ but exert significant and, to varying extents, uncertain, influences on Earth’s energy budget and climate.
