## Slide 22

- Climate Change Vs Air Quality

- We know that air quality is important

- We know that climate change is important

- We know that these are largely talked about separately

- There are significant benefits from tackling poor air quality and climate change in parallel

- Like carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane, air pollutants come from burning fossil fuels, whether from driving cars, generating electricity, or operating industrial plants.

- The impacts of climate change exacerbate air pollution. For example, extreme heat increases the number of wildfires which, in turn, release more polluting gases and particulate matter into the atmosphere.

- Extreme heat can increase pollution levels and exacerbates the health effects of breathing polluted air.

## Slide 28

- CO2 Measurements

- Ice cores (100’000s years ago)

- Ice cores preserve past atmospheric CO₂ in air bubbles trapped as snow compacts into ice, providing a direct record of past atmospheres.

- These records extend back ~800,000 years, allowing long-term CO₂–climate relationships to be studied.

- Temporal resolution decreases with depth as ice flow and firn diffusion smooth the signal, averaging CO₂ over longer time periods.

## Slide 35

- Understanding Units

- Units:

- For Particulates (e.g., PM): Micrograms per Cubic Meter (µg/m³).

- For Gases: Parts per Million (ppm), Parts per Billion (ppb), Parts per Trillion (ppt).

- Explanation:

- Micrograms per Cubic Meter (µg/m³): Measures the mass of particulate pollutants in micrograms (one-millionth of a gram) per cubic meter of air. For example, a concentration of 43 µg/m³ signifies the presence of 43 micrograms of a pollutant in each cubic meter of air.

- Parts per Million (ppm): Indicates the number of units of mass of a gaseous pollutant per one million units of the total mass of the air.

- Parts per Billion (ppb): Represents the number of units of mass of a pollutant per one billion units of the total mass of the air.

- Parts per Trillion (ppt): Denotes the number of units of mass of a pollutant per one trillion units of the total mass of the air.

- Understanding these concentration units is vital for evaluating air quality, monitoring pollutant levels, and assessing the environmental and health impacts of pollutants within the atmosphere.

## Slide 38

- Modelling Anthropogenic Impacts

- The following protocol is typical.

- 1. A GCM model is run for many decades with no external forcing

- 2. The statistics of the run (mean climate, annual cycles and variability on a range of time scales) are then compared with observation to assess performance.

- 3. The model is then re-run with changes in forcing (such as greenhouse radiative forcing).

- 4. The difference in the two climates provides an estimate of the consequent climate change arising form that forcing factor.

- Indicators of GCC: Ocean

- Ocean acidification impacts both small coastal communities and big industries. By harming aquaculture and tourism, communities that rely on the ocean as a source of food and income will be particularly affected.

## Slide 52

- Today’s Lecture Summary

- Human Activities as Drivers: Human activities, including fossil fuel combustion, land-use changes, and industrial processes, are the primary drivers of greenhouse gas emissions.

- CO₂: Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is the most significant greenhouse gas emitted due to human activities, with other contributors like methane (CH₄) and nitrous oxide (N₂O) also playing major roles.

- Non-CO₂ Influences: Short-lived climate forcers such as aerosols, black carbon, and methane have complex and varied climate impacts.

- Trends in Emissions: Emissions of CO₂, CH₄, and other greenhouse gases have risen sharply over the past century, with future pathways depending on mitigation efforts.

- Atmospheric Concentrations: Greenhouse gas concentrations have significantly increased since pre-industrial times, with CO₂ levels now at their highest in millions of years.

- Radiative Forcing: Anthropogenic activities are the primary source of positive radiative forcing, which is driving global warming. Aerosols contribute a negative forcing, partially offsetting warming.

- Future Projections: Without significant mitigation, global temperatures are projected to rise well beyond 1.5°C and 2°C, leading to severe climate impacts.

- Regional Climate Change: The effects of global warming, such as temperature changes, sea level rise, and altered precipitation patterns, will vary regionally but will universally affect ecosystems and human systems.

- Climatic Impact Drivers: Key impacts include changes in temperature and precipitation patterns, leading to extreme events, biodiversity loss, and threats to food and water security.
