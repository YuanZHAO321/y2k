## Slide 12

- Measurements

- Greenhouse gases absorb radiation at specific wavelengths (unique spectral “fingerprints” in the infrared).

- Absorption strength is proportional to concentration (Beer–Lambert law), allowing precise quantification of gas amounts.

- Instruments measure how much radiation is absorbed along a known path length

- Different gases absorb at different wavelengths, allowing CO₂, CH₄, H₂O and others to be measured independently.

## Slide 13

- Measurements

- In situ ground measurements

- Air is drawn into an instrument cell.

- The gas passes through a chamber of fixed length (e.g. 10–100 cm).

- Infrared light passes through that chamber.

- The instrument measures how much light is absorbed.

- Because the chamber length is known precisely, concentration can be calculated directly.

- Here the path length is the length of the measurement cell.

- Open-path / remote sensing from ground

- A beam may travel:

- From instrument → reflector → back to detector

- The distance between them is measured accurately, so total path length is known.

- Satellites

- Radiation travels from Earth’s surface → through the atmosphere → to the satellite.

- Radiative transfer models account for pressure, temperature, and atmospheric layering.

- The effective path length is calculated

## Slide 15

- CO2 Measurements

- Ice cores (100’000s years ago)

- Ice cores preserve past atmospheric CO₂ in air bubbles trapped as snow compacts into ice, providing a direct record of past atmospheres.

- These records extend back ~800,000 years, allowing long-term CO₂–climate relationships to be studied.

- Temporal resolution decreases with depth as ice flow and firn diffusion smooth the signal, averaging CO₂ over longer time periods.

- The age of the gas is younger than the surrounding ice, creating a gas–ice age offset that must be corrected for

- What is a climate proxy?

- An environmental indicator that can be related to a climatic variable.

- Individual proxies are noisy and uncertain, but taken together they robustly show that atmospheric CO₂ was much higher in the past and has declined over tens of millions of years, closely linked to long-term climate cooling.

- Climate Proxy Examples

- Phytoplankton (red diamonds) -CO₂ inferred from carbon isotopes in marine phytoplankton; reflects how carbon uptake varies with ambient CO₂ but is influenced by growth rate and nutrient availability.

- Stomata (green squares) -Based on the density of stomata on fossil leaves; plants form fewer stomata at higher CO₂, but results depend on species and environmental stress.

- Boron isotopes (blue triangles) -Uses the pH sensitivity of boron isotopes in marine carbonates to infer seawater pH, and hence atmospheric CO₂; sensitive to assumptions about ocean chemistry.

- Paleosols (grey circles) - CO₂ estimated from carbon isotopes in ancient soils; integrates atmospheric CO₂ over long timescales but is influenced by soil respiration and climate.

## Slide 35

- Measurements Summary

- All climate measurements contain uncertainty, arising from instrument limits, calibration, and interpretation.

- Uncertainty increases further back in time:

- Instrumental records (last ~150 years): high accuracy, high temporal resolution

- Historical records: lower accuracy and lower temporal resolution

- Temporal resolution decreases with age:

- Ice cores: annual layers near the surface → multi-decadal to millennial averaging at depth

- Marine & lake sediments: often integrate signals over centuries or longer

- Climate proxies have intrinsic uncertainties:

- Proxies (e.g. tree rings, ice cores, corals, sediments) record indirect climate signals

- Each proxy responds to multiple environmental factors, not just temperature e.g. tree rings reflect moisture, nutrients, CO₂, and disturbance as well as temperature

- Earth/Atmosphere is delicate balance, largely dictated by incoming and outgoing radiation

- Slight changes in balance can cause large changes in global climate

- These changes can be enhanced or diminished by positive or negative feedbacks

- Positive feedback: initial change reinforced by another process.

- Negative feedback: initial change counteracted by another process.

## Slide 46

- Radiative Forcing

- Definition of radiative forcing:

- The change in radiation balance at the tropopause between the incoming solar radiation and the outgoing earth’s radiation (net irradiance).

- A positive forcing warms the climate system

- A negative forcing cools the climate system

- Significance:

- Increase in greenhouse gases has reduced the amount of longwave radiation leaving the troposphere –positive radiative forcing.

- In the real atmosphere there are a number of positive and negative feedbacks that will occur and interact non-linearly with each other.

## Slide 47

- Climate Feedback – Positive Examples

- Climate feedbacks describe how components of the Earth system respond to an initial forcing, either reinforcing or counteracting the resulting climate change.

- (Processes that amplify an initial climate change)

- Ice–albedo feedbackMelting ice and snow reduce surface albedo, causing more solar radiation to be absorbed and further warming.

- Water vapour feedbackWarmer air holds more water vapour, a powerful greenhouse gas, which enhances the greenhouse effect.

- Ocean carbon feedbackAs seawater warms, the oceans’ capacity to dissolve CO₂ decreases, leaving more CO₂ in the atmosphere.

- Vegetation–albedo feedback (regional)Expansion of darker vegetation (e.g. forests into high latitudes) lowers surface albedo, increasing heat absorption.

## Slide 48

- Climate Feedback – Negative Examples

- Climate feedbacks describe how components of the Earth system respond to an initial forcing, either reinforcing or counteracting the resulting climate change.

- (Processes that counteract an imposed climate change)

- CO₂ fertilisation effectHigher atmospheric CO₂ can enhance plant growth, increasing carbon uptake through photosynthesis.

- Increased terrestrial carbon storageWarmer temperatures in some regions lengthen growing seasons, allowing greater carbon storage in soils and biomass.

- Chemical weathering feedbackHigher temperatures and increased rainfall enhance silicate rock weathering, which removes CO₂ from the atmosphere over long timescales.

## Slide 51

- Lecture Summary

- Multiple independent datasets (instrumental records, satellites, ice cores, and proxies) consistently show rising CO₂ concentrations and global temperatures across timescales from decades to millions of years.

- Modern CO₂ levels (~420 ppm) are unprecedented for at least 800,000 years and likely far longer, with isotopic evidence confirming a fossil-fuel (anthropogenic) origin.

- Temperature increases since the mid-20th century are best explained by human greenhouse gas emissions; natural drivers (solar variability, volcanoes, internal variability) cannot account for the observed long-term warming.

- Radiative forcing theory links increased greenhouse gases to warming, while feedbacks (especially water vapour and clouds) amplify or modulate the response.

- Uncertainty increases backward in time and at smaller spatial scales, but confidence is very high that human activity is the dominant cause of recent global warming.
