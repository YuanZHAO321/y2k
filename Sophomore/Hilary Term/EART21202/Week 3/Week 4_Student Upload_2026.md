## Slide 4

- Last Week: Natural Variability

- Solar Variability: Changes in the sun's energy output can influence Earth's climate. Solar cycles, which include variations in the sun's radiation and magnetic activity, can affect the amount of solar energy reaching the Earth.

- Orbital Variations: Changes in Earth's orbit and axial tilt over long timescales, known as Milankovitch cycles, can affect the distribution and intensity of solar radiation received by the Earth, leading to changes in climate over tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years.

- Volcanic Activity: Large volcanic eruptions can inject significant amounts of aerosol and sulphur dioxide into the atmosphere, leading to temporary cooling by blocking sunlight. These eruptions can cause short-term fluctuations in global temperatures.

- Ocean Circulation: Ocean currents play a crucial role in redistributing heat around the Earth. Variations in ocean circulation patterns, such as El Niño and La Niña events in the Pacific Ocean, can have significant impacts on regional and global climate patterns.

## Slide 38

- Radiative Forcing

- Definition of radiative forcing:

- The change in radiation balance at the tropopause between the incoming solar radiation and the outgoing earth’s radiation (net irradiance).

- Significance:

- Increase in greenhouse gases has reduced the amount of longwave radiation leaving the troposphere –positive radiative forcing.

## Slide 39

- Greenhouse Effect - Summary

- Some solar radiation absorbed by earth’s surface and atmosphere.

- Earth and atmosphere emit infrared radiation.

- Greenhouse gases absorb some of this infrared radiation.

- Greenhouse gases (water, CO2 + others) give off infrared radiation in all directions.

- Earth absorbs downward directed infrared radiation

- Result: warmer surface temperature = 288K rather than 255K

## Slide 41

- Atmospheric Composition – Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)

- Dominant contributor to anthropogenic radiative forcing

- Primary sources: fossil fuel combustion (energy, transport, industry)

- Cement production via calcination (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)

- Land-use change and deforestation reduce long-term carbon sinks

- Atmospheric lifetime: centuries (with long tail effects)

- Co-benefits of reduction: CO₂ reduction via fossil fuel phase-out also lowers emissions of PM, SO₂, and NOₓ, improving urban air quality and reducing the health effects of air pollution

## Slide 42

- Atmospheric Composition – Methane (CH₄)

- Second most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas

- Major sources: fermentation in ruminants, rice paddies

- Fossil fuel extraction, processing, and distribution losses

- Landfills and anaerobic waste decomposition

- Short atmospheric lifetime (~12 years)

- Co-benefits of reduction: Similar to CO2 but methane controls can reduce ground-level ozone (O₃) formation, improving respiratory health and crop yields.

## Slide 43

- Atmospheric Composition – Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)

- Strong greenhouse gas with significant stratospheric ozone impacts

- Primarily emitted from agricultural soils due to nitrogen fertiliser use

- Microbial nitrification and denitrification processes

- Additional sources: manure management and industrial chemical production

- Very long atmospheric lifetime (~120 years)

- Co-benefits of reduction:  Improved fertiliser efficiency reduces ammonia (NH₃) emissions, lowering secondary particulate matter formation.

## Slide 48

- Lecture Summary

- All objects with a temperature above 0 K emit electromagnetic radiation, and both the Sun and Earth behave approximately as blackbody radiators.

- Radiation laws (Stefan–Boltzmann and Wien’s Law) explain why hotter objects emit more energy and at shorter wavelengths than cooler objects.

- Earth’s energy balance is set by incoming solar radiation and outgoing terrestrial radiation; assuming blackbody equilibrium gives an effective Earth temperature of ~255 K (–18 °C).

- The observed global mean surface temperature (~288 K, +15 °C) is higher because of the greenhouse effect.

- The atmosphere is largely transparent to shortwave solar radiation, but absorbs and re-emits longwave infrared radiation emitted by the Earth.

- Greenhouse gases (e.g. H₂O, CO₂) absorb infrared radiation at specific wavelengths and re-radiate energy in all directions, warming the surface and lower atmosphere.

- Differences between planetary temperatures (e.g. Earth, Mars, Venus) illustrate how atmospheric composition and pressure control the strength of the greenhouse effect.

- Increasing greenhouse gas concentrations enhance infrared absorption, leading to positive radiative forcing and further warming.
