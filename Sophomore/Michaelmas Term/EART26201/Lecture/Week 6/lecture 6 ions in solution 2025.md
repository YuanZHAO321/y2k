### **Basic revision: Stoichiometry**

A **mole** of a substance equals the **gram-formula mass**

**Molarity (M):** the number of moles of a solute dissolved in a litre of solution.

A molar solution is made by placing 1 mole of a solute into a 1 litre volumetric flask. Water is then added to the volumetric flask up to the one litre mark. For a 1 molar solution of NaCl we would weigh out 58.35 g

### **Basic revision: Stoichiometry**

**Molality (m)** : Molality is the number of moles of solute dissolved in one kilogram of solvent.

Be careful not to confuse molality and molarity. Note that the solvent must be weighed. Because one litre of water has a specific gravity very close to 1.0 at room temperature and weighs nearly one kilogram; one can measure out one litre of water and add the solute to it. When the solvent is water molarity and molality are very similar.

_To make a one molal aqueous (water) solution of sodium chloride (NaCl), measure out one kilogram of water and add one mole (58.35g) of NaCl to it._

### **Water as a Solvent**

#### Water - most abundant liquid on Earth

- key role in most geochemical and biological processes

- the medium in which chemical reactions take place in nature

##### _Special properties of Water_

- Higher boiling point than other hydrides of higher molecular weight (e g H2S)

   - liquid at most surface conditions

- Very high heat capacity relative to common liquids and solids

   - means oceans are an important heat sink for the Earth’s climate

- Maximum density of water is at 4 deg-C

   - means ice floats on the surface and does not sink to the bottom of the ocean – **what would happen if it did?**

- An excellent solvent for ionic compounds i.e. compounds that dissociate into negatively and positively charged ionic species in water.

### **Water as a Solvent - Ions**

- Pure water is a strong dielectric = an electrical insulator

- The high dielectric constant of water is a measure of its polarity and is responsible for the remarkable solvent properties of water.

- Solute ions are hydrated by the dipolar water molecules preventing them from re-combining.

### **Electrolyte solutions**

When NaCl dissolves in water it dissociates into ions:

NaCl + Cl<sup>-</sup> (s) = Na<sup>+</sup>

Results in hydrated cations and anions

###### **High capacity of water to dissolve ionic compounds means:**

- Water in nature almost always contains dissolved ionic species (an aqueous solution)

- Dissolved ions are charged particles or electrolytes (conduct electricity) so aqueous solutions are electrolyte solutions

#### **Activity Coefficient**

- In very dilute aqueous solutions, ions do not interact with each other.

- This is the standard state (ideal solution) for ions in aqueous solution.

- With increasing concentration, ions start to interact with each other (non-ideal solutions). The chemical potential of an ion will depend upon the concentration of the other ions.

- Ion-ion interactions may be specific (e.g. ion pairs or complexes) or non-specific (long-range coulombic interactions).

- The net results is that chemical reactions among ions and molecules in aqueous solution are often not a direct function of just their concentrations.

### **Dissociation of Acids and Bases**

###### Arrhenius definition of acids and bases

- **Acid** – a H-bearing compound that dissociates when dissolved in water

   - releasing H<sup>+</sup> ions (protons)

   - lowering pH

   - H<sup>+</sup> ions combine with water molecules to form hydronium ions H3O<sup>+</sup> (aq).

- **Base** – a compound bearing the OH group that dissociates in water

   - releasing OH<sup>-</sup> ions (hydroxide ions).

   - raising pH

   - OH<sup>-</sup> combine with water to form hydrated ions

### **Strong and Weak Acids and Bases**

- Strong Acids (e.g. HCl) dissociate almost completely in water.

- Weak acids (e.g. CH3COOH – acetic acid) dissociate to a small extent (Ka<<1)

- Bases are also strong or weak depending on how much dissociation occurs when dissolved in water

- Do not confuse the **strength** of an acid or base (the extent to which it dissociates) with **concentration** (which is the amount dissolved in water).

- The degree of dissociation of an acid or base is given as:

- pKa measures the strength (dissociation) of the acid (pKb for bases)

### **Summary - 1**

- The polarity of water results from hydrogen bonding

- The hydrogen bonding gives water properties that are vital for life

- When ionic compounds dissolve in water they dissociate into cations and anions.

- The ability of the dissolved ions to interact with each other is diminished by the presence of other ions and by interactions with the solvent molecules (hydrogen bonding).

- Therefore the molar concentrations are corrected for by factors known as activity coefficients.

- Activity coefficients decrease with ionic charge and increasing ionic strength of the solution

- Ionic strength is a measure of the concentration of ions in a solution

### **Summary - 2**

- The Law of Mass Action can be used to obtain the equilibrium constant during dissolution.

- The equilibrium constant simply expresses the ratio of concentrations (or activities) of products/reactants for a chemical reaction at equilibrium.

- Acid and bases are compounds that dissociate in water to produce H<sup>+</sup> and OH<sup>-</sup> ions respectively.

- The equilibrium constant for acids and bases is known as the dissociation constant K . a

- The pH of a solution and Ka of the acid or base are related by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

- Most minerals are the salts of a strong acid and weak base.
