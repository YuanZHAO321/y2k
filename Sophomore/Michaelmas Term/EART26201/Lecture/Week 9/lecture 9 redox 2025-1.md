## Reduction–oxidation reactions (Redox)

## **Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) reactions**

- Reactions involving a change in oxidation state are redox reactions.

- Oxidation state of an element determines its mobility and toxicity in the environment (e.g As<sup>3+</sup> reducing conditions and more toxic to humans than As<sup>5+</sup> ).

- Natural waters vary widely in redox conditions; constantly changing an element’s chemical speciation and mobility.

- Understanding redox in natural waters (groundwaters) is important for applied geochemistry related to drinking water quality, landfill leachates, organic pollutants, acid mine drainage and radioactive waste containment.

- The major elements which show redox behaviour include C, S, N, and Fe (and Mn in some subsurface systems).

- There are many minor elements which show multiple oxidation states in nature. Some of the important ones include As, Pb, Cu, Cd, Hg, Se, and Cr.

## **Redox or oxidation state**

- Oxidation is electron loss, reduction is electron gain: OIL RIG

- Oxidizing agents accept electrons, and reducing agents give-up electrons.

- The oxidation state (or number) or redox state is the charge that the ions would have if the molecule of species dissociated.

- The oxidation state of an atom in its elemental form is zero (e.g. O2).

- When bonded to something else, oxygen is in oxidation state 2<sup>-</sup> e.g. -

- SO42-, CO32-, NO3 (electron acceptors = oxidising agents)

- Hydrogen is in oxidation state of 1<sup>+</sup> e.g. H2O, H2S

## **Redox systems**

- Free electrons do not exist in solution. Any electron lost from one species in solution must be immediately gained by another.

Ox ⇋ Red 1 + Red2 1 + Ox2

- Ox1-Red1 and  Ox2-Red2 pairs are known as _redox couples_

- Redox couples are written in the following form:

Cu<sup>o</sup> (s) + Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) ⇋ Cu<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + Zn<sup>o</sup> (s)

- oxidation half-reaction: Cu<sup>o</sup> (s) ⇋ Cu<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e

- reduction half-reaction: Zn<sup>2+</sup> (aq) + 2e ⇋ Zn<sup>o</sup> (s)

# **Redox potential**

- Can calculate the potential for any reaction, e.g. to calculate E<sup>0</sup> for the reaction:

Zn + Cu<sup>2+</sup> ⇋ Zn<sup>2+</sup> + Cu

- Consider E<sup>0</sup> for each half reaction:

Zn = Zn<sup>2+</sup> + 2e = -0.76 volt

Cu = Cu<sup>2+</sup> + 2e  = +0.34 volt

- Subtract: -0.76-0.34 = -1.10 volt

- Any reaction with a negative potential will go to the right.

- Any reaction with a positive potential will go to the left.

## **pE, electron Activity**

- If pE is **low** , the solution provides a **source** of electrons and is **reducing** .

- If pE is **high** , the solution acts as a **sink** for electrons and is **oxidizing** .

- pE is important for predicting the solubilities of minerals

#### **Example: Mam Tor**

- The iron concentration of an aqueous solution, at equilibrium, will be much greater if iron is present in oxidation state 2<sup>+</sup> instead of 3<sup>+</sup> .

- Ground water, in contact with iron bearing soils, will dissolve iron as Fe<sup>2+</sup> (ferrous) ions. When this water, containing Fe<sup>2+</sup> , contacts air the iron is oxidized to Fe<sup>3+</sup> (ferric) ions and precipitates at Fe(OH)3.

- This is a problem whenever mining operations expose ground water to iron. Ultimately, the ground water will be exposed to an oxidizing environment, which causes the dissolved metals to precipitate. The orange colour of many streams in mining regions is due to the precipitation of ferric hydroxide.

## **pE/pH in natural environments**

- pE and pH are both important environmental variables; pE determines oxidation state and both determine species solubility and formation of insoluble oxides (and hydroxides).

- In general, environments in contact with the atmosphere (e.g. rainwater, oceans, streams) have higher pE than environments isolated from the atmosphere (e.g. peat bogs).

- This makes sense if we think about the actual partial pressure of oxygen in the atmosphere (PO2 = 0.21) – this is much nearer to the upper stability limit of water than the lower stability limit.

- In most natural waters redox conditions are controlled by oxygen, sulphur and iron.

## **Eh and pH –waters natural waters**

## **Summary - 1**

- Oxidation is _loss_ of electrons, reduction is _gain_ of electrons.

- Redox reactions are written in the form of half-reactions for each redox couple.

- Oxidation potential (as measured by Eh or pE) is the redox equivalent of pH. Both Eh and pH are master variables in environmental systems.

- Eh is measured as a voltage (potential or emf) for redox reactions relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE).

- pE is electron activity and is used to represent the redox equilibrium state of natural waters.

- The Nernst equation is used to speciate metals in solution.

## **Summary - 2**

- Plots of Eh (or pE) vs pH are also known as Pourbaix diagrams and are widely used to describe  the chemical behaviour species in the hydrosphere, of natural waters and soils.

- Redox is critically important for controlling subsurface conditions that affect everything in the subsurface from deep biosphere to aquifer/reservoir behaviour.
