# **Buffering, alkalinity and silica**

- **Acidity** of a solution is its _capacity to donate protons_ . It can be determined by titrating the solution with a strong base (e.g. NaOH).

- **Alkalinity** of a solution is its _capacity to accept protons_ . It can be determined by titrating the solution with a strong acid (e.g. HCl).

- Acidity is an important property because high acidity enables minerals and metals to be dissolved and is often accompanied by high **_total dissolved solids_** (TDS) which means the water is unsuitable for drinking.

- Strong Acids more readily release H<sup>+</sup> into water, they more fully dissociate

- Strong Bases more readily release OH<sup>-</sup> into water, they more fully dissociate

- Remember that strength DOES NOT EQUAL concentration.

- Acids with more than one proton dissociate stepwise with more than one pKa

# **Alkalinity**

- **Alkalinity** is the capacity of a solution to neutralize or “buffer” acid.

- 2- -

- In most natural waters carbonate [CO3 ] and bicarbonate [HCO3 ] anions are most common, so **_total alkalinity_** is approximately equal to the **_carbonate alkalinity_** .

- The alkalinity (and acidity) for many natural waters can be expressed in terms of carbonic acid species and strong acids and bases (which dissociate completely).

   - Calk = [HCO3-] + 2[CO32-] + [OH-] – [H+]

- Alkalinity can increase the pH (more basic). When CaCO3 dissolves in water, the carbonate can react with water to form bicarbonate, which produces hydroxide:

CaCO3 (s) ⇋ Ca<sup>2+</sup> + CO32CO 2- + H O ⇋ HCO - + OH3 2 3

# **Importance of alkalinity**

- Low alkalinity of a water can result in rapid changes to pH, if an acid enters the water.

- Alkalinity is important for aquatic organisms because it protects them against rapid/transient changes in pH (“acid shock”).

- Alkalinity is important for two of the most severe environmental impacts of **acid rain** and **ocean**

- industrialization linked to rising CO2 in the atmosphere – **acidification** .

- Carbon dioxide and water are converted to carbonic acid, this can then dissociate to provide bicarbonate and carbonate:

CO + H O ⇋ H CO H<sup>+</sup> + HCO - ⇋ 2H+ + CO 2- 2 2 2 3 ⇋ 3 3

- Notice in the reaction that as carbonic acid (H2CO3) reacts to form carbonate (CO32-), **two** hydrogen ions H+ are released into the water.

- Conversion of carbon dioxide to carbonic acid causes a **decrease in pH (increase in H**<sup>**+**</sup> **).**

# **Solubility of silica**

- Silicon dioxide (SiO2) is a solid that can be amorphous or have a variety of crystalline forms including quartz (a very abundant mineral in the Earth’s crust), cristobalite or tridymite.

- Amorphous silicon dioxide (silica) reacts with water to form silicic acid

- Most silicic acid forms during weathering of common rock-forming aluminosilicates such as feldspars and micas. Very little is formed by the dissolution of quartz itself.

- When its solubility is exceeded it forms amorphous silica which precipitates out as a gelatinous material. The water is slowly expelled and the silica crystallises as **_chert._**

## **Precipitation of silica from solution**

- A relatively small decrease in pH of a basic solution containing silicic acid may reduce its solubility to form a supersaturated solution from which amorphous silica will precipitate.

- e.g. a decrease in pH from 8.5 to 8.4 can result in precipitation of 1.37 mg L<sup>-1</sup> of silica. This amount magnifies rapidly in the natural environment because we are often dealing with relatively large volumes of aqueous fluids (groundwaters) and high flow rates.

- Quartz is a very common mineral in the Earth’s crust but it is not an important source of silicic acid because it is highly insoluble (about 20x lower solubility than amorphous silica)

- Quartz solubility = 0.0059 g L<sup>-1</sup> = 5.9 ppm.

- For amorphous silica the corresponding value is 109 ppm

# **Summary - 1**

- Acidity and alkalinity are the capacity to donate or accept protons respectively.

- Alkalinity is an important measure of a natural solutions ability to buffer pH during addition of acid solution.

- A pH buffer is the capacity of a solution to “resist” changes in pH when and acid or base is added.

- The changes in pH during buffering can be approximated using the Henderson-Hasselbach equation and buffering is strongest within +/- 1 pH unit of the pK value of the acid or base.

- pH buffering in seawater is controlled mainly by the dissolution and precipitation of calcium carbonate

- and OH<sup>-</sup>

- In pure water the concentration of H<sup>+</sup> ions are equivalent at 1 x 10<sup>-7</sup> mol L<sup>-1</sup> and the -log of this is the pH = 7.

# **Summary - 2**

- The solubility of some important geological compounds  are sensitive to changes in pH.

- Silica is much more soluble in alkaline solutions (pH>7) due to the stepwise dissociation of silicic acid.

- Precipitation and dissolution of the mineral quartz from supersaturated and undersaturated solutions respectively, is kinetically unfavourable.

- In the oceans, precipitation of silica is biologically mediated.

- Mineral stability diagrams can be used to define the environmental conditions under which each phase (mineral) is stable and what happens when a mineral is exposed to conditions outside of its stability field.
