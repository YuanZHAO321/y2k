Question 1

- The crystallisation behaviour of mantle melts can be summarised using a phase diagram of the forsterite (Fo; Mg2SiO4)-quartz (Qz; SiO2) system. Cristobalite (Cr) is a high-temperature polymorph of SiO2; quartz is a low-temperature polymorph. Enstatite (En) has an intermediate composition between Fo and Qz.

- Quantitatively describe the equilibrium crystallisation of a mantle melt analogue with a composition of Fo80Qz20 from 1900 to 1500 °C.			[15%]

Once the melt cools to ~1750°C Fo will start to form. Crystallisation will then continue, with the melt becoming progressively richer in Qz; the composition of the Fo will remain unchanged. At ~1570°C, the liquid will reach the peritectic, defined by the reaction Fo + L = Qz. At this point the liquid will have the composition Fo68Qz32. Because the system is evolving by equilibrium crystallisation, Fo will be consumed by the peritectic reaction to produce En. Because the melt has a lower Qz content then En, the peritectic reaction will stop once the melt has been exhausted, leaving a solid containing Fo and En in proportions of 67% and 33%, respectively, which is compositionally equivalent to the composition of the initial melt.

- How and why would the end products of fractional crystallisation differ from the end-products of equilibrium crystallisation for the same composition (Fo80Qz20)?

- If the system evolved by fractional crystallisation the peritectic reaction could not occur because there would be no Fo to react with the remaining melt. The liquid composition would thus continue to evolve from ~1570 °C to ~1540 °C by En crystallisation until the eutectic defined by the reaction En + Qz = L, where the last solid would eventually form with solid containing En and Qz in proportions of ~84% and ~16%, respectively.

- How can radiogenic isotopes be used to determine the absolute ages of ancient volcanic rocks? What are the limitations of using radiogenic isotopes for dating?	Use sketches and examples of radiogenic isotope systems to illustrate your answers as appropriate			[15%]

- Absolute ages of ancient rocks from isochron dating in which ratios of parent to stable isotopes are plotted against ratios of daughter to stable isotopes such that samples with different amounts of the parent isotope evolve over time to produce more of the daughter isotopes. The different ratios of parent to stable isotopes result from partitioning differences during the formation of the rock, with different mineral hosting different proportions of the parent and daughter/stable isotopes. Assuming that the samples were in isotopic equilibrium at the time of formation and the system has remained closed (no resetting or weathering), the slope of a line fitted to the isotopic composition of the samples is related to their age via the decay constant for the system; the intercept gives the initial daughter to stable isotope ratio. This line itself is called an isochron and gets progressively steeper as time passes. As well as showing a sketch of an isochron diagram, students may refer to key systems that have been discussed in detail such as Rb-Sr or Sm-Nd.
