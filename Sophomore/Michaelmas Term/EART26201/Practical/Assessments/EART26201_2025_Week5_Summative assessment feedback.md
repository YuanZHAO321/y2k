Thermodynamic describes the equilibrium state of a system under a given set of intrinsic conditions (pressure, temperature and composition). The equilibrium state is defined by the phase or phase assemblage that has the lowest Gibbs free energy under the conditions of interest. Phase diagrams provide a way of visualising how the equilibrium state (i.e. phase assemblage) changes as conditions are changed, though are restricted to looking at changes in only two conditions at a time because of graphical limitations; phase diagrams are typically isochemical, isothermal or isobaric projections. However, reaching equilibrium takes time as reactants will typically need to move within the system, whether by advection or diffusion, in order for reactions to occur. As a result, phase diagrams may not adequately describe the behaviour of chemical systems that are subject to kinetic limitations.

When basalt cools slowly in large magma reservoirs, chemical equilibrium is maintained throughout crystallisation and kinetic effects are absent. The first crystal to form from liquid basalt with a composition XDi=0.7 will be augite with a composition of XDi~0.92. As the system cools, both the augite and the liquid will become progressively poorer in Di poor until the liquid reaches the eutectic, at which point the composition of the liquid will be XDi~0.45 and the composition of augite will be XDi~0.77. Eutectic crystallisation will then produce augite (XDi~0.77) and pigeonite (XDi~0.17) in roughly equal proportions until the system is solid and contains about ~90% augite and ~10% pigeonite. Because equilibrium is maintained throughout cooling, Di-poor pigeonite will progressively exsolve from Di-rich augite and vice versa, producing exsolution lamellae. This exsolution will continue until the pigeoniteorthopyroxene phase transition is reached. Before the phase transition, the system will contain about ~78% augite and ~22% pigeonite with compositions of XDi~0.88 and XDi~0.10, respectively; after it will contain ~80% augite and ~20% orthopyroxene with compositions of XDi~0.88 and XDi~0.05, respectively. As the system cools further exsolution will continue, with orthopyroxene eventually reaching a very Di-poor composition of XDi~0.02 and augite reaching a Di-rich composition of XDi~0.98; the system will contain ~~ 29% orthopyroxene. A slowly cooled basalt will thus eventually contain augite with large exsolution lamellae of orthopyroxene as well as orthopyroxenes with large exsolution lamellae of augite (so-called inverted pigeonites).

When basalt cools quickly in lava flows, chemical equilibrium is not maintained because there are kinetic limitations on moving reactants around. The initial crystallisation of a quickly cooling basalt is much the same as that described above for a slowly cooling basalt up to the point of solidification at the eutectic. This is largely because diffusion rates are considerably faster in liquids than solids. However, once the system is solid, diffusion rates may be too slow to move Ca around within crystals limiting the amount of exsolution that can occur or preventing it entirely. Phase transitions will also not occur, preventing the reaction of pigeonite to orthopyroxene. Very quickly cooled basalts will contain augite and pigeonite with eutectic compositions of XDi~0.77 and XDi~0.17 in proportions of ~90% augite and ~10% pigeonite, respectively. Overall, quickly cooled basalts will contain homogenous crystals of augite and pigeonite, without exsolution lamellae.

b) Describe how the different partitioning behaviour of parent and daughter isotopes in the Rb-Sr isotope system leads to the creation of isochrons. Use information provided in Table 1 and/or your answer to a) to substantiate your answer. [15 marks]

The parent isotope, 87Rb, is more incompatible than the daughter and stable isotopes 87Sr and 86Sr. Glass compositions are therefore relatively enriched in 87Rb with respect to feldspar compositions because glass represent frozen liquid that was enriched in incompatible elements during crystallisation. Whole-rock compositions lie between glass and feldspar compositions because they are a mixture of the two. As time progresses, high glass 87Rb contents translate into high 87Sr contents. The greater the amount of radioactive parent isotope, the greater the amount of daughter isotope produced after any given time. Correspondingly, low feldspar 87Rb contents will result in low 87Sr contents. Glass and feldspar thus define two ends of an isochron, the former being rich in both parent 87Rb and daughter 87 Sr isotopes with respect to the stable 86Sr isotope, and the latter being poor in both.
