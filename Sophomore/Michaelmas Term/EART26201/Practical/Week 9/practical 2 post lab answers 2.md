3.4. Post laboratory questions

- Which is a better buffer solution tap water or deionised water?

The tap water was the better buffer solution than the deionised water, because the pH for tap water reduced less than the pH of the deionised water, when they both had 5 ml of 0.1 M of HCl added to them. So, tap water and deionised water absorbed the same amount of H+ and OH- ions, but tap water saw less of a pH change. When calculating the buffering capacities, tap water had a pH change of 0.7 per ml of HCl added whereas deionised water had a pH change of 1.4 per ml of HCl. So tap water had double the buffering capacity as deionised water.

This is because in the pure deionised water, the HCl will dissociate almost completely into H+ and Cl-, and because there is nothing else within the water to react with these ions. Although the composition of tap water is unknown,  it is likely to contain dissolved species (carbonate / bicarbonate) that react which the HCl that consume either the H+, reducing the pH change.

- What is the significance of the end-point in the titration of the 0.01 M NaOH solution with 0.1 M HCl? Explain how such a titration could be used to determine the concentration of an unknown NaOH solution.

The significance of the end-point of this titration was to show when the 0.01M NaOH solution stopped buffering the pH. This occurred when the pH dropped significantly from the alkaline pH 9.7 to the very acidic pH 3.7. The pH at which all OH- ions were consumed and there were no excess H+ ions is 7.

- Titration could be used by gradually adding 0.1M HCl into the unknown concentration of NaOH. The volume of HCL and NaOH would both be known, and the concentration of the HCl would be known, at the end of the titration. The number of moles of HCl could be calculated by multiplying its concentration by the volume added in the titration. In this experiment one molecule of HCl react exactly with one molecule of NaOH, so the number of moles would be the same. So, the number of moles of NaOH would be divided by the initial volume of the unknown solution of NaOH, to calculate the concentration in mol/L.

- The indicator bromo-thymol blue changes colour from yellow in acid solutions to blue in alkaline solutions over the pH range 6.0-7.6. Why might the transition pH range for bromo-thymol blue be more suitable for determining the NaOH titration with HCl end-point?

Bromo-thymol blue would be more suitable because it has a colour change that coincides with the pH at the end point of the titration. In this case  acid + base = salt + water, so endpoint pH 7.

- How good a pH buffer would a mixture of 100 ml of 0.1M NaOH and 100ml of 0.1M HCl solution be? Explain your answer.

It would be a poor pH buffer because there would be no buffering potential left:

Acid + base = salt + water

- Small samples of (i) a groundwater with a low buffer capacity; and (ii) a groundwater with a high buffer capacity; are taken and the pH values of these samples measured. Which sample's pH value is likely to be more representative of the pH of the groundwater from which it was taken? Explain your answer.

The ground water with a higher buffer capacity would more likely have a pH representative of the groundwater it was collected from, because it is more resilient. For example, if the lab user breathed CO2 into the groundwater samples, introducing some carbonic acid, the sample with the higher buffering capacity would be able to maintain the pH when the sample was originally collected. The sample with the low buffering capacity is more susceptible to a pH change because they are no ions to provide buffering.
