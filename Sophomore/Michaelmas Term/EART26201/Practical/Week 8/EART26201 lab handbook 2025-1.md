## INTRODUCTION

The handbook is designed for the laboratory element of module EART26201(Principles of Geochemistry) - it outlines the aims of this laboratory and provides necessary information on health & safety, use of equipment and report writing. PLEASE READ THIS HANDBOOK CAREFULLY AND IN GOOD TIME BEFORE COMMENCING WORK.

## AIMS OF LABORATORY COURSE

The aims of this laboratory course are to:

- enable you to gain experience of real chemical systems of relevance to important Earth systems

- enable you to reinforce learning of physical chemical principles with concrete practical examples

- introduce you to practical laboratory skills and working in a regulated practical environment

- develop your skill in making accurate and relevant observations

- develop your skill in recording your aims, methods, observations and interpretations

- enable you to gain an appreciation of natural variability occurring in experimental and analytical work.

## GENERAL INFORMATION

The practical component of the EART26201 (Principles of Geochemistry) course unit will comprise of five 2-hour sessions. The laboratory class supervisors will be Dr Greg Holland for lab writeup and Jordan Gaskell for the practical experiments assisted by demonstrators in the lab 1.18. Check timetable for details.

During each laboratory class we ask you to:

- comply with the Health and Safety rules and guidelines

- ask for advice from your demonstrator or laboratory supervisors when you are in difficulty or doubt about how to execute or interpret any work: their job is to answer your questions or indicate how an answer might be found.

- record all pertinent information in an A4 laboratory book.

After each laboratory class we ask you to:

- complete any required written assignment. At the end of the laboratory class we ask you to: ensure that all glassware/bottles that you have been issued with are clean

- return all items issued to you clean and undamaged

[You may not be permitted into the laboratory class without a 100% mark in the Health and Safety Test and submittal of the signed Health and Safety Declaration]

Laboratory Records if required, complete immediately after practical session

Post-laboratory Exercises if  required, complete in next classroom session

We hope that you enjoy this part of the course. Please read this entire handbook to maximise the benefit you gain and to familiarise yourselves with Health and Safety Rules and Guidelines designed to enhance your safety.

We strongly recommend that you prepare well for each practical and organise your laboratory time thoughtfully.

Most importantly, don't hesitate to ask if you need to know something - your laboratory class supervisors and demonstrators are there to help you.

### Emergency procedures:

Inhalation: move person into fresh air. If not breathing, give artificial respiration. Consult a physician.

Skin contact: Take off contaminated clothing and shoes immediately. Wash off with soap and plenty of water. Consult a physician.

Eye contact: Rinse thoroughly with plenty of water/eye wash for at least 15 minutes and consult a physician.

Swallowed: Do NOT induce vomiting. Never give anything by mouth to an unconscious person. Rinse mouth with water. Consult a physician.

Spillage on bench or floor: It is usually safe to sweep or mop up small spillages after neutralisation if necessary. Consult person in charge of the laboratory.

Notify COSHH safety advisor (Dr Abby Ragazzon-Smith) and report via the accident report form on the dept. H&S web site of any accidents, near misses and dangerous occurrences.

Further guidance specific to the experiments you will be undertaking in the WRC Laboratory

Chemical laboratories, including undergraduate teaching laboratories, unavoidably contain many potential hazards. To minimise accidents and injury in the chemistry laboratory, WE INSIST that you:

- Wear safety glasses or googles (meeting EN 1662001 ) at all times.

- Wear at all times a laboratory coat, which is fastened and sleeves are rolled down.

- Don’t have exposed limbs

- Nitrile protective gloves are worn at all times.

- Confine long hair and loose clothing.

- No personal electronic items. e.g. phones and laptops.

- Use fully enclosed shoes. No open front or back and no open weave type materials.

- Do not eat, chew gum, drink, smoke or apply cosmetics.

- Do not start work until you know the safety rules and procedures applicable to your work - ASK IF YOU DO NOT KNOW.

- Keep work areas dry, clean and tidy - you or others could be burned or exposed to hazardous substances if you do not clean up spillages immediately.

- Minimise exposure to all chemicals. This means avoid inhalation of gases and fine powder and avoid skin contact with any chemicals.

- Wash your hands well with water and detergent before leaving the laboratory.

- Think, act and encourage safe, considerate and responsible behaviour -	be prepared and don't rush any operations. USE YOUR COMMON SENSE.

- Report any accident immediately to a laboratory class supervisor.

- DO NOT work in the laboratory outside the stated teaching hours. Working in a laboratory either alone or without adequate supervision is considered to be hazardous and is strictly forbidden by the Department.

- Only carry out the experiments in this manual according to procedures laid out herein. YOU WILL NOT conduct any other experiments or procedures without authorisation from the laboratory class supervisors.

- Do not pipette by mouth; always use a bulb-type pipette filler. If you do not have one, ask to borrow one and be careful to return it at the end of the lab class

In addition to these general rules there are a number of further rules and guidelines applicable to particular chemicals and apparatus. These include:

- When mixing the contents of a volumetric flask by shaking make sure that you hold both the base of the flask and the stopper at the top.

Do not hold filled volumetric flasks by the neck as the neck may not be strong enough to support the weight of the flask and the included solution.

Improper handling of volumetric flasks may result in BROKEN FLASKS, SEVERE CUTS and UNCONTROLLED RELEASE OF POTENTIALLY HAZARDOUS LIQUIDS.

- When pouring liquids, always make sure that you:

- have the required reagent. CHECK THE LABEL!

- be sure you can securely control the reagent bottle(s) if they are heavy

- place the mouth of the flask/bottle as close as possible to that of the reagent bottle when pouring liquid

- do not pour from a wide-mouthed bottle into a narrow-mouthed bottle unless you use a suitable funnel

- NEVER add water to a concentrated reagent- this may cause local heating and density contrasts that may cause the solution to splash or spit back on to you. Always perform a dilution of a reagent by adding reagent slowly to water.

- NEVER remove any chemical substance from the laboratory

- Use an adequately functioning FUME HOOD when required.

Further advice will be given by laboratory supervisors concerning COSHH and disposal of chemicals but for the reagents that you will be using the main hazards are listed on the COSHH form – see over page. Please read them careful and take careful

note of each so you know how to minimise the risk of exposure. COSHH is the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002. Carrying out a COSHH risk assessment is required by law in order to minimise the risks of exposure or injury by working with hazardous substances.

ANY breach of these safety rules may endanger the safety of yourself and others and may result in you being excluded without compensation from the EART26201 laboratory course.

Hazards and their meanings Flammable

A substance or mixtures, which easily catches fire, pyrophoric and chemicals, which in contact with water emit flammable gases

Oxidizing

A substance that has the ability to oxidize other substances and cause them to catch fire.

Explosive

A reactive substance that contains a great amount of potential energy that can produce an explosion.

Toxic

A substance, which on contact with skin, swallowed or inhaled may cause lethality to the entire body, lethality to specific organs, major/minor damage, or cause cancer.

Harmful

A substance, which has the potential to cause harm to life or health. Such as irritation of the eys and skin. Skin sensitization and specific target organ toxicity following single exposure

Health hazard

A substance which can have long term harm to health and life such as, respiratory sensaization, reproductive toxicity, mutagenicity issues and carcinogenicity.

Environmental hazard

A substance which is ether harmful or toxic to the enviroment with long lasting effects.

Corrosive

A substance which cources serious eye damage and corrosion to skin and metals.

Compressed gases

- A substance which is stored as ether a compressed gases, liquefied gases, or

dissolved gases.

Danger!

The science labs can contain the following types of materials.

Teratogenic

A substance that may cause birth defects via a toxic effect on an embryo or foetus

Mutagenic

A Substance, which changes genetic material

Carcinogenic

A substance, which is directly involved in causing cancer

## PRE-LABORATORY PREPARATION

An organised professional will prepare in advance for work that he/she is going to undertake. This generally enables:

- potential problems to be identified

- the work to be carried out with less fuss and in a more relaxed and controlled manner with resultant greater efficiency of time and effort, better accuracy and improved safety.

This applies to your laboratory classes.

Indeed, you will find it difficult to complete all the tasks required in the space of each laboratory session unless you do some advance preparation.

- make a note of any step or steps in the procedure that you do not understand and, if appropriate, ask your demonstrator or supervisor for clarification prior to commencing the practical

- make a list of equipment and reagents that you will require so that you can check the availability of these at the beginning of the practical

## WRITING LABORATORY RECORDS

An important objective of the laboratory course is to develop your skill in recording experimental work.

The body of scientific knowledge is based on detailed experimental evidence obtained by numerous workers in different places at different times.

The development of science as a whole or indeed of any particular academic or industrial research or non-research project generally requires evidence to be considered, checked, criticised or assessed in relation to other items of evidence.

In order for such appraisal to be carried out, experimental evidence must be COMMUNICATED or be available for communication.

Experimental evidence is normally written up in two forms:

- an original record or laboratory record

- a report

We outline below essential points regarding the writing of laboratory records:

The laboratory record should be an accurate record of actual procedures carried out, actual observations made as well as the details of any calculations made. It should be written as an original record at the time of doing the experiment. As such, mistakes, failures and accidents should all be recorded. It should, at all times, be a record of what actually happened, not what you intended, predicted or wanted to happen.

You should write your laboratory records as follows:

- The laboratory record should be written in a duplicate note book with numbered pages. You should:

- write your name, course and year on the front cover

- reserve the first two pages for an index

- The laboratory record should consist of the following sections:

- Title of experiment and date carried out

- Procedure followed (or reference to it together with any modifications to the procedure, if appropriate) [for EART26201: there is no need to reproduce what is in the laboratory handbook]

- Results [for EART26201: a series of datasheets are given to you for each experiment. These are provided to offer a guide as to the critical information that you should be recording. Everyone is expected to record this information in their personal laboratory record, whilst we may ask for

a set of data sheets to be submitted by each group from time to time

- Calculations/preliminary interpretations

- Summary/conclusion

- You should write legibly and neatly. Remember to space the information out for ease of reading and to use tables and sketched graphs as appropriate.

- ALL experimental data should be recorded at the time they are obtained. Do not use scraps of paper or rely upon your memory.

- Do not scribble out, tippex over or rip out mistakes. If you make a recording error, draw a line through it and make the necessary revised entry.

- It is traditional to write the laboratory record in the 3rd person, past tense (since it is recording what has just happened). For example: "A black precipitate was observed".

## PRACTICAL LABORATORY TECHNIQUES

VOLUMETRIC GLASSWARE - GENERAL POINTS

Solutions of known compositions may be prepared accurately using volumetric apparatus - namely pipettes, burettes and volumetric flasks. These items are typical glassware, the volume of which has been accurately factory calibrated and marked. To use them accurately ALL these items:

- must be kept scrupulously clean

- must NEVER be heated (as the glass expands rapidly and is slow to regain its original dimensions)

- must be read and used correctly

On all three types of apparatus calibrated volumes are either etched or marked as a circle on the glass. For most aqueous solutions contained in glass pipettes, burettes and volumetric flasks, the top surface of the liquid is not flat but forms a curved surface, called a "meniscus". Typically this surface is highest adjacent to the glass surface or sides of the vessel and slightly lower in the middle of the vessel. When making a reading you must make sure that

- your eye is at the same level as the meniscus (in order to avoid problems due to parallax)

- the meniscus level is read at the curve's centre (i.e. the lowest point generally for a water meniscus in a glass vessel)

- In addition there are some points to be made specific to each type of apparatus:

PIPETTES

Pipettes are used for transferring accurately known volumes of liquid.

Pipettes are either calibrated for a particular volume or have calibrated graduations for several different volumes.

BURETTES

Burettes are designed to sequentially dispense accurate volumes of liquids, typically for titrations.

Burettes are similar in design to pipettes except that they have a stopcock at their base.

Simpler burettes such as you will generally be using are filled from the top using a funnel. It is a useful practice to clean out the burette with the solution you are about to dispense by allowing 5 ml or so of solution out to waste before starting a titration.

When dispensing liquid, the stopcock on the burette should be GENTLY and SLOWLY opened and closed by using ONE HAND ONLY placed behind the burette away from the operator. Burettes are fragile items so great care and finesse is required in their use.

The volume of solution dispensed by a burette is calculated by noting the calibration mark the solution meniscus reached before dispensing and subtracting this from the calibration mark the solution meniscus is level with after dispensing.

Thus, it is clearly impractical to fill a burette to higher than the "0.00 ml" calibration mark unless you wish to use the solution above this mark to initially rinse the burette out.

Volumetric flasks are used for the accurate preparation of solutions.

As for pipettes and burettes, it is good practice to rinse a volumetric flask with the solvent before use.

If a volumetric flask is to be used to make up solution from solids and solvent, then the solids should be completely dissolved BEFORE the volume is made up to mark.

## SOME USEFUL TIPS ON UNITS

Most physical quantities, such as temperature, volume, concentration and mass, have some unit of measurement, which ultimately enables the measurement made to be referred back to a well-defined and documented international standard.

For example, length is commonly measured in units of 1 metre, which was defined as the length of an international standard (a platinum bar) held in the National Bureau of Weights & Measures at Sevres, near Paris.

It is important that ALL measurements you report include the appropriate unit. A number on its own is generally ambiguous and mostly useless.

For example, if you measure the temperature of a solution, then recording "20" is not adequate because it is not clear whether you mean "20°C" or "20 K" or "20°F".

Reporting units of measurement becomes particularly important when dealing with concentration as there are a number of units in use. The more commonly used ones are defined below:

MOLALITY is the number of moles of a substance per kilogram of solvent (the solvent is commonly water) and is conventionally abbreviated as "m"

MOLARITY is the number of moles of a  substance per litre of solution and is conventionally abbreviated as "M"

For low concentration solutions the units can be represented as millimolar (mM) are used. 1M = 1000mM

For dilute aqueous solutions at low constant temperatures, molarity and molality are numerically almost the same. But for saline solutions (e.g. oil field brines, seawater) and at higher temperatures there can be large substantial differences between the two units.

In this handbook, we will generally express concentrations in units of MOLARITY because the practicals you are going to do generally involve preparing solutions by volume (in volumetric flasks or using burettes and pipettes) rather than by weight (using balances, which, if accurate, are tens to hundreds of times more expensive).

SOME USEFUL TIPS ON GRAPHS

Graphs provide an easy way of seeing the relationships between different variables.

Dependent and Independent Variables

Typically a graph will show the dependence of one variable on another variable. The DEPENDENT VARIABLE is conventionally plotted on the 'Y' axis whilst the INDEPENDENT VARIABLE is plotted on the 'X' axis.

For example, if we measured the temperature of a room at hourly intervals and wished to plot the temporal variation we would plot temperature vs time with temperature (the dependent variable) plotted on the 'Y' axis and time (the independent variable) plotted on the 'X' axis.

Choice of scales

Before plotting data, you should choose the most appropriate scales. In most cases it is desirable to choose the scale so that

- all the data may be plotted on the page

- the data are distributed over at least 2/3 of the page

- the minimum and maximum values and increments of the scale are rounded numbers (e.g.10, 20, 30, 40 rather than 9.7, 20.5, 31.3, 42.1).	The increments should generally all be the same on one axis.

We prefer that the data plotted and therefore the scale also should be pure numbers. That is they have no units. For example, it is preferable to plot "temperature in degrees Celsius" (which will be a pure number) rather than "temperature" (which will be a number of temperature units).

Labelling axes

Both the 'Y' and 'X' axes should be clearly labelled with the appropriate variable name and the units used. For example

"Temperature in degrees Celsius" "Volume of 0.1M HCl added in ml" "Conductivity in 10-6 S/cm"

Title

All graphs should have clear titles in a prominent position. The titles should explain the graph succinctly with the dependent and independent variables being clearly indicated. For example:

added"

"Conductivity as a function of salinity at 25°C"

Plotting Data Points

Individual (x,y) data points should be plotted as a point surrounded by a small circle or as a small cross. Wherever appropriate, some estimate of the errors in the measurement of both dependent and independent variable should be made and included as error bars centred on the data point. It should be clearly stated (under the title is a good place) how these error bars were estimated.

Curve fitting

Having constructed a graph with a series of data points it may become apparent that there is some non-random relationship between the 'X' and 'Y' variables. This relationship may be a linear relationship or some more complicated function. Whatever the relationship, the fit to the data points is rarely likely to be perfect: it is important to attempt to decide what variations are due to some underlying rational relationship and what variations are due to random noise.

Three types of lines might be drawn through a set of data points on a graph:

Model curve

In some experiments, a theory may exist that predicts the relationship between a particular dependent variable and a particular independent variable.

Plotting this model on this graph as your data enables you to determine how closely your results match the theory, with discrepancies being due to problems in the theory or its application or in your data!

best-fit empirical curve

The most common (but certainly not the only) example of this is a straight line whose equation is of the form:

y = mx + c

where y is the dependent variable, x is the independent variable, m is the gradient of the line and c is the 'y' axis intercept where x = 0

Microsoft Excel or IBM SPSS Statistics. Such curve-fitting is particularly useful where the gradient, m, or intercept, c, may be related to some theoretical parameter.

trend

## Titrations

Titration is a form of quantitative chemical analysis used to measure the unknown concentration, of a constituent of a sample, known as an analyte. It is achieved by adding to the measured sample an exactly known quantity of another substance with which the desired constituent reacts in a definite, known proportion. The process is usually carried out by gradually adding a standard solution (i.e. a solution of known concentration) of titrating reagent, or titrant, from a burette, essentially a long, graduated measuring tube with a stopcock and a delivery tip at its lower end. At the equivalence point of a titration, an exactly equivalent amount of titrant has been added to the sample. The experimental point at which the completion of the reaction is marked by some signal is called the end point. This signal can be the colour change of an indicator or a change in some chosen, e.g., electrical property, that is measured during the titration. The difference between the end point and the equivalence point is the titration error, which is kept as small as possible by the proper choice of an end- point signal and a method for detecting it.

For many titration reactions it is possible to find a suitable visual colour indicator that will signal the end point at, or very close to, the equivalence point. Such titrations, classified according to the nature of the chemical reaction occurring between the sample and titrant, include: acid-base titrations, precipitation titrations, complex- formation titrations, and oxidation-reduction (redox) titrations.

In an acid-base titration (i.e. the titration of an acid with a base, or vice versa), the indicator is a substance that can exist in two forms, an acid form and a basic form, which differ in colour. For example, litmus is blue in alkaline solution and red in acid solution. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid solution and pink in alkaline solution. A wide choice of acid-base indicators is available, varying not only in the colours of the two forms but also in the pH value at which the colour occurs.

The endpoint of precipitation titrations is marked by the appearance of a coloured precipitate. One way in which this can be done is by employing potassium chromate

- as the indicator. Another method involves the use of an adsorption indicator, the indicator action being based on the formation on the surface of the precipitate of an adsorbed layer of an indicator salt, which forms only when an excess of precipitant ions is present.

The most important titrations based upon complex-formation reactions are those involving the titration of metal ions with the reagent disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (a salt of edetic acid, or EDTA). The indicators are dyes that have the property of forming a coloured complex with the metal ion. As the titration proceeds, the reagent reacts first with uncomplexed metal ions, and, finally, at the end point it reacts with the metal-indicator complex. The colour change corresponds to the conversion of the metal-dye complex into the free dye.

In oxidation-reduction (redox) titrations the indicator action is analogous to the other types of visual colour titrations. Close to the end point, the indicator undergoes oxidation or reduction, depending upon whether the titrant is an oxidizing agent or a reducing agent. The oxidized and reduced forms of the indicator have distinctly different colours.

Alternatively, for many titrations the end point can be detected by electrical measurements. These titrations may be classified according to the electrical quantity that is measured. Potentiometric titrations involve the measurement of the potential difference between two electrodes of a suitable cell; conductometric titrations , the electrical conductance or resistance of the solution being titrated; and amperometric titrations , the electric current passing during the course of the titration. In the titrations just mentioned the end point is indicated by a marked change in the electrical quantity that is being measured.

### INTRODUCTION

#### BUFFERS AND MINERALS

If the compositions of groundwaters and hydrothermal fluids were completely randomly variable then we should have an impossible task to understand their chemistry. Fortunately, time and time again analyses of groundwaters and hydrothermal fluids show that their compositions often fall into quite narrow ranges.

For example, many groundwaters have silica concentrations close to that expected if they were in equilibrium with quartz. Typically, the host rocks for such groundwaters contain excess free quartz which controls the dissolved silica concentration: should the silica concentration decrease then more quartz will dissolve to tend to return the concentration to its equilibrium value:

SiO2 (quartz) + 2H2O (aq) ⇋ H2SiO4 (aq)

should the silica concentration increase then quartz may precipitate tending again to return the concentration to its equilibrium value:

H4SiO4 (aq) ⇋ SiO2 (quartz) + 2H2O (aq)

If these mechanisms are effective in maintaining a relatively constant level of dissolved silica then the quartz is said to BUFFER the solution (against change).

Other compositional parameters may also be buffered by minerals or mineral assemblages. For example, in many granite-hosted hydrothermal systems, the ratio (K+/H+) is buffered by the assemblage quartz + K-feldspar + muscovite according to the equilibrium:

3KAlSi3O8(K-feldspar) + 2H+ (aq) ⇋ KAl3Si3O10(OH)2 (muscovite) + 2K+(aq) + 6SiO2(quartz)

The quartz + K-feldspar + muscovite assemblage may act as a buffer only whilst all of the individual minerals in the assemblage coexist.

Composition parameters such as pH may also be buffered by the coexistence of substantial quantities of two appropriate dissolved species within the aqueous phase.
