EXPERIMENT 1:   SOLUBILITY AND METAL-LIGAND COMPLEXING

- OBJECTIVES

After completing this practical and the associated exercises you should be able to:

- conduct a precipitation titration

- manipulate logarithms algebraically

- determine the formula of a metal-ligand complex from suitable titrimetric data

- understand the principles of metal-ligand equilibria in solution and how such equilibria affect mineral solubility

- INTRODUCTION

- Solubility and metal-ligand complexing

- The solubility of many minerals in water may be enhanced considerably by the formation of metal-ligand complexes. For example, the solubility of galena (PbS) is greater in salty waters than fresh waters because of lead-chloride complex formation:

PbS (galena) + H+ (aq) 	⇋ Pb2+ (aq) + HS- (aq)

Pb2+ (aq) + 4C1- (aq) 	⇋ 	PbC142-(aq)

Our ability to model mineral solubility behaviour during water-rock reactions involving saline waters therefore depends on our knowledge of the nature and stability of metal-ligand complexes formed.

In this experiment, we aim to determine the formula of the predominant silver-ammine complex in ammonia-rich solutions.

- Silver-ammine complexing

If a silver nitrate solution is titrated with potassium bromide, the former providing silver ions and the latter bromide ions, then at the precipitation end-point i.e. when silver bromide just starts to precipitate, the following equilibrium applies:

AgBr (s) ⇋ Ag+ (aq)	+Br- (aq)

With					Ksp = [Ag+(aq)]  [Br	-(aq)]

Where Ksp is the solubility product of silver bromide, and [ ] refers to the concentration of the enclosed species.

If the silver nitrate solution also contains large amounts of ammonia, then the following equilibrium may be established:

Ag+ (aq) + nNH3 (aq)⇋Ag(NH3)n+ (aq)

With

Kn = [Ag(NH3)n+ (aq)] / ([NH3(aq) ]n[Ag+ (aq)])

where Kn is the formation constant of the given silver ammine complex, and [ ] refers to the moles of the enclosed species.

moles of KBr = (moles of NH3)n  Kn  Ksp/ moles of AgNO3

Taking logarithms of both sides gives:

log(moles of KBr)= n log(moles of NH3) + log	(Kn  Ksp/ moles of AgNO3)

Thus a plot of log (moles-KBr) against log(moles-NH3) for a series of solutions in which moles of AgNO3 was constant would be expected to result in a series of points forming a straight line with slope equal to n, the number of ammine molecules present in each molecule of the predominant silver-ammine complex.

Thus if we titrate silver nitrate-ammonia solutions of various different ammonia amounts against a potassium bromide solution, then we may be able to determine the formula of the  predominant silver ammine complex, Ag(NH3)n+(aq),using the  equation above.

If we ensure that the total volume of the solution is the same at the end-point of each titration, then it follows that the volume of ammonia solution added and the volume of potassium bromide solution added will be proportional to the concentrations NH3 and KBr respectively and that concentrations of AgNO3 will remain constant.

Thus, n may be determined by plotting log (moles of KBr solution) against log (moles of NH3 solution).

- PROCEDURE

- You will have on your bench a clearly labelled plastic bottle containing approximately 60 ml of 0.01M silver nitrate solution. Pour this into the small beaker and use the syringe to take a 20ml aliquot and place into the 250ml beaker.

- Take a 40ml aliquot of 2M ammonia and add to the 20ml of silver solution in the 250ml beaker. The ammonia can be obtained from a large beaker in the fume cupboard.

- Dilute the silver nitrate/ammonia mix with de-ionised water to a total volume of 100 ml.

- Titrate the mixture in the beaker with the 0.02M KBr solution from the	50 ml burette until the first permanent turbidity appears. You may find it useful to view the beaker against a dark background. Record the volume of KBr solution used as well as the total volume of the mixture. Rinse out the beaker with deionised water. You may find it useful to conduct a rough trial titration to determine the approximate volume of titrant required and how the solution changes colour over the end-point.

- Repeat steps	 4.1 to 4.4 however this time using only 10 ml of the 2M NH3 solution. Be sure to add a sufficient volume of deionised water to bring the TOTAL volume of the solution near to that recorded initially in step 4.4.

- Dispose of the beaker contents into one of the collection beakers in the fume cupboard. DO NOT dispose of the SILVER BROMIDE down the sink.
