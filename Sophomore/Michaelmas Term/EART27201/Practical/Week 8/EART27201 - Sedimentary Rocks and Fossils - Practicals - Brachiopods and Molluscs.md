# Brachiopods

We learned in the lecture that there are three major groupings of brachiopods in the latest taxonomy of the group. Let’s meet these a little more detail now (well, those we have available as fossils).

Rhynchonelliformea: This group represents the greatest diversity of brachiopods today and in the fossil record,  and maps to the original idea of the Articulata.  It comprises brachiopods with a pair of calcite valves and a posterior hinge  (i.e.  they pivot open and closed at the back of the animal).  The calcite in their shell is distinct from the other groups of brachiopods in having a layer of fibrous calcite.  The shells open anteriorly along the commissure,  which zig-zags in some species increasing the area of the gape but not its width.  This,  and associated ridges,  also strengthen the shell.  Some members also have a depression in the middle  (the median fold and sulcus)  to separate incoming from outgoing water.  The hinge (or articulation)  is achieved using teeth on the ventral valve corresponding to sockets on the dorsal one.  The shells open and close through muscle action  -  the diductor muscles open the shell, and adductors close it.  These leave scars in the interior of the shell. Internally,  some species have a structure called the brachidium supporting the lophophore,  and the group lacks an anus  (they just shoot waste out of their mouth).  Most  (but not all)  members of this group attached to substrate using a pedicle that emerged through an opening  (the foramen)  in the ventral valve.  The morphology of the group is summarised in the images below  (courtesy of Benton & Harper 2020), and you can learn more on the link to the right.

Linguliformea: These little guys don’t have calcite shells.  Rather their shells are organophosphatic  (comprising calcium phosphate,  proteins and a natural polymer called chitin)  and are typically fairly symmetrical.  They open and close these using a complex system of muscles,  without an associated hinge mechanism.  In brief,  opening is achieved through the withdrawal of the soft parts posteriorly forcing the valves apart.  Relaxing then allows the valves to close again.  The pedicle in some members of the group emerges from between the valves,  although others do have a foramen as in the above group.  These animals have a recognisable U ‐ shaped alimentary tract ending in an anterior anus. Modern forms are burrowers specialising in inter- to subtidal habitats.

Craniiformea: This is a diverse group.  They are inarticulate  (i.e.  lack a hinge,  like the last group),  but have calcite shells like the first group.  This is sometimes described as organocarbonate  -  the shells are constructed from an outer organic layer,  and then two layers of calcite.  These are punctate: i.e.  they have small canals of tissue through the shell.  The valves open and close through hydraulic mechanisms,  rather than employing muscles.  In general,  the group attaches to a hard object with all or part of the ventral valve, and as such, individuals do not have a pedicle.

# Brachiopods vs bivalves

One of the harder bits of invertebrate palaeontology can be telling brachiopods and bivalves apart,  especially when you have fragmented shells.  So let’s give this a go.  Bear in mind that these are completely different animals from relatively distantly related animal groups  (if you don’t believe me have a look at the phylogeny I use in lectures).  This is also reflected in major differences in their organisation.
