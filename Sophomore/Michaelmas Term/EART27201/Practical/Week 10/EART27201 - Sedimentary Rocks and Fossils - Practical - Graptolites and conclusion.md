# Graptolites

The origins of the graptolites lies in the Cambrian period; the group probably evolved from a solitary,  worm ‐ like animal which had arms and tentacles.  These early representatives of the group might have been pseudocolonial filter ‐ feeders,  found in clumps on the seafloor.  As the Cambrian progressed clumps became more erect structures,  and by the end of the Cambrian we see a range of dendroid forms.  These are multibranched colonies resembling small shrubs,  attached to the substrate by holdfasts,  some of which are shown left  (scale bars 10mm).

It was during the late Cambrian and Early Ordovician that some of these dendroid graptolites moved from the benthic communities of the time to a planktonic mode of life  –  and from dendroid graptolites, graptoloid graptolites evolved! There is then an evolutionary trend towards fewer branches  (from eight down to two,  then one),  and the group evolved through a large number of forms,  graptoloids being dominant from the Ordovician.

Once we hit the middle Ordovician,  and into the Silurian,  we find fossils of scandent graptoloids  -  this is where the thecae are found on the outside of the colony.  After this,  forms appear where a stipe has been lost  –  these are known as uniserial scandent graptoloids,  such as we see in the genus Monograptus .  Monograptids such as this dominated graptolite faunas in the Silurian,  and developed a huge variety of forms that can be identified via the shape and arrangement of the thecae. The last graptoloids are found in Early Devonian rocks.  We don’t,  however,  know for sure what drove this evolutionary sequence.  Was the simpler stipe configuration more hydrodynamically stable thus better adapted to turbulence? Did it assist graptolites when moving through the water column feeding? Did it prevent the interference between adjacent stipes and their thecae, thus improving efficiency? All have been suggested.

## Brackish Lagoonal Environments

|Context|Commonly associated with terrestrial,barrier bar,estuarine,marsh or shallow marine shoreface<br>facies.|
|---|---|
|Lithology|Typically shales though siltstone,sandstones and less commonly coarser grained sediments may<br>be introduced by tidal or storm processes in food-tidal deltas and washover fans.|
|Taphonomy|Dispersed in situ or disturbed neighbourhood assemblages,typically as thin shell<br>concentrations.Tidal and storm processes may introduce allochthonous assemblages from<br>shoreface and inner shelf environments.|
|Taxonomy/Diversity|In situ faunas typically of low diversity; usually ostracodes,bivalves and gastropods.|

## Shoreface Environments (BA1) 0-5m

|Context|Nearshore and associated beach facies,distinguished from inner shelf deposits by generally<br>having only rare mudstones interbedded with sandstones.Shoreface facies commonly cap<br>upward-coarsening shallow marine cycles.|
|---|---|
|Lithology|Typically sandstones with locally developed mudstones.Sandstones exhibit planar-or<br>cross-stratifcation<br> formed<br> by<br> wave,<br> tidal<br> or<br> wind-forced<br> currents,<br> or<br> hummocky<br>cross-stratifcation formed by oscillatory or combined fow.|
|Taphonomy|Allochthonous shell concentrations that may occur as coquinas,on bedding planes or<br>cross-stratifed foresets.|
|Taxonomy/Diversity|Palaeozoic assemblages characterized mainly by brachiopods with more rarely bivalves and<br>gastropods.Mesozoic/Cenozoic assemblages characterized by varied bivalves(including deep<br>burrowers and attached forms)and gastropods.Taxa afected by substrate mobility(e.g.Corals)<br>are rare.In situ faunas rare.Diversity,though still generally low,can be elevated by introduction<br>of allochthonous faunas by storm,tidal and wave activity.|
|Abundance|Generally low in Palaeozoic rocks,but Cenozoic strata typically possess more abundant remains.<br>In more subdued shoreface environments shells may be relatively common in coquinas.|
|Ichnology|Typically Skolithos,more rarely Psilonichnus and Cruziana ichnofacies depending on substrate<br>mobility and subtle gradients in hydrologic sedimentologic and ecologic parameters.Diversity<br>typically low.|

## Inner (shallow) Shelf Environments (BA2) 5-15m

|Context|Facies subject to frequent storm waves and tidal processes,commonly occurring below<br>shoreface sandstones and above mid-shelf sequences in coarsening-upward cycles.|
|---|---|
|Lithology|Sandstones dominate,but may occur in equal abundance with interbedded shales.<br>Storm-infuenced facies exhibit hummocky cross-stratifcation; tidal facies may exhibit<br>herringbone cross-stratifcation,sigmoidal cross-stratifcation or mud drapes.|
|Taphonomy|Allochthonous or disturbed neighbourhood assemblages at the base of storm sandstones,<br>typically as coquinas.Tidally infuenced sandstones commonly exhibit transported assemblages<br>or shell lags distributed along foresets.Shales possess disarticulated and dispersed shells and<br>bedding plane assemblages.In situ faunas rare or absent.|
|Taxonomy/Diversity|Palaeozoic strata dominated by moderately but variably diverse assemblages of brachiopods,<br>particularly orthoids and strophomenoids: Mesozoic/Cenozoic strata typically possess variable<br>infaunal and epifaunal bivalves.Representatives of other benthic macroinvertebrates may occur,<br>sometimes in abundance.|
|Abundance|Generally moderate,but high in coquinas.Faunas can,however,be generally sparse where a<br>freshwater infuence is established,as,for example,in inner shelf environments in front of<br>deltas.|
|Ichnology|Typically moderate to high diversity assemblages of the Cruziana and/or Skolithos ichnofacies.<br>Mudstones commonly intensely bioturbated and possess trace fossils typical of the Cruziana<br>ichnofacies: associated sandstones contain representatives of the Skolithos ichnofacies<br>providing substrate mobility does not result in their destruction.|
