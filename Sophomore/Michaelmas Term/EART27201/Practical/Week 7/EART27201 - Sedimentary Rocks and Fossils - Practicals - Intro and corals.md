# Ecology

We also met some terms describing the ecology of particular animals  -  i.e.  where they live in a marine environment. These were:

Pelagic: Describing organisms that swim or drift in a sea or a lake,  as distinct from those that live on the bottom  (see benthos). Pelagic organisms are divided into plankton and nekton.

Benthic: Occurring on the bottom of a sea or lake.  Benthic organisms may crawl,  burrow,  or remain attached to a substrate.

Plankton  (Planktonic): The collection of pelagic organisms that drift or float passively with the current in a sea or lake.  Plankton forms an important food source for many other members of the aquatic community and is divided into zooplankton and phytoplankton.

Nekton (Nektonic): Pelagic organisms that actively swim through the water.

Epifaunal: Applied to benthic organisms that live on the surface of the sea bed,  either attached to objects on the bottom or free-moving. They are characteristic of the intertidal zone.

3

Infaunal: Applied to benthic organisms that dig into the sea bed or construct tubes or burrows.  They are most common in the subtidal and deeper zones (i.e. the area seaward of the low-water mark).

Sessile: Describing animals that live permanently attached to a surface,  i.e.  sedentary animals.  Many marine animals, e.g. sea anemones and limpets, are sessile.

# Preservation

We talked briefly about the process of fossil preservation in the lecture.  There are a number of different forms of preservation. Here’s a quick overview.

Unaltered remains: Sometimes the hard parts of an organism are found in rocks unaltered.  We can thus find the original materials  (often biomineralised,  but sometimes tissues)  the organism created in life  -  shells are often a good example of this.

Permineralization: When the pore spaces in a porous material get filled with minerals this is called permineralization.  This is common in  -  for example  -  bone and wood.  Common minerals include calcite or silica,  and are often sourced from groundwater.  The original non-pore space may remain unaltered,  or itself be replaced with minerals.  A famous example of this form of preservation is found in the form of petrified wood,  where the pore space is filled with minerals, and the wood is later replaced - thus no original wood is left.

Carbonised fossils: This kind of fossil occurs when organisms were buried quickly,  and most often when there was little oxygen around.  The remains of the organism are two-dimensional carbon films found on bedding planes.  They often look black,  because they are so carbon rich.  The famous Burgess Shale,  which you learned about last year, comprises carbonised fossils, but also this is relatively common in plant fossils, especially from the Carboniferous!

Replacement: Replacement happens when the original materials in a fossil are replaced with some form of secondary material  -  often shortly after burial.  For example,  in ammonites the mineral pyrite  (FeS2 )  can replace calcium carbonate to create a pyritized fossil.

External moulds: Sometimes a fossil can dissolve.  The impression it leaves is a mould.  Similarly,  when you split a rock, on one part you will have the fossil,  but you will find a mould on the other bit of rock you split off.  If this mould represents the form of the outside,  it is an external mould.  These are relatively common within concretions  -  growths of a mineral around a fossil which harden,  prior to its complete decay, leaving a void and a mould.

Internal moulds: Just sometimes,  we get internal moulds.  This occurs when,  for example,  an organism dies,  and rots,  but its shell was filled with sediment,  or a mineral.  If,  then,  the shell eventually dissolves  -  but what filled it does not  -  we are left with an internal mould! Sometimes,  if you're lucky,  you can find an external mould to go with the internal one.

Casts: These form when an external mould  (or gap between an internal and external one)  is filled with a substance that then lithifies.  This reproduces the original form of the structure.  So a mould is formed off an original structure,  and a cast when a mould is filled.

## Orders

In our lecture we briefly met three different kinds of corals. Let’s do so now in more detail.

Rugosa  (tetracorals) : A Palaeozoic order of solitary and colonial corals which appears in the Ordovician and becomes extinct in the Permian.  The corallum contains radial plates  (septa)  and horizontal plates  (tabulae),  and sometimes oblique plates  (dissepiments).  The septa developed in insertion cycles of four and many species retain a degree of bilateral symmetry.  Solitary rugosans seem to have preferred soft substrates since they had no means of cementation and colonial forms seem to have relied for stability on the weight of the skeleton.

Scleractinia: Order of solitary or,  more commonly,  colonial corals,  which always possess an external calcareous skeleton consisting essentially of radial partitions.  Septa develop following the pattern of the radial infoldings in the body wall (mesenteries) in cycles of 6, 12, 24, 48, etc. The order first appeared in the Middle Triassic.

8

Tabulata: A Palaeozoic coral order comprising individuals that are always colonial,  never solitary,  and that possess small corallites.  Tabulae are the major skeletal elements and septa are usually absent or poorly developed.  The corallum is built of individual corallites,  not always connected to each other,  and may be encrusting,  may consist of thin sheets,  or may be massive.  Other forms may be joined side by side to form fence-like structures.  Smaller tabulates appear to have lived in deeper waters,  whereas the larger tabulates appear in coral–stromatoporoid associations (stromatoporoidea are a group of sponges around from the Ordovician to Devonian).

## Day/lunar month length

Presumably we’re all happy with the concept that time passes.  On Earth we tend to measure that relative to cycles. We have:

A (solar) year: The time it takes for Earth to revolve once around the sun, 365 days today.

A month: We often think of time in calendar months  -  each 1/12 of a year,  as part of a calendar famously standardised by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582,  building off that of Julius Caesar  (46 BC).  But there is also a lunar  -  or synodic  -  month. These reflect the natural orbital period of the Moon: there are 12.36 lunar months per year  -  this is why the full moon falls on different days of the month.  It doesn’t impact on our practical,  but do bear in mind that there are also lunar calendars,  where the seasons cycle through the months in a 30+ year cycle,  and it’s entirely arbitrary  (and an artefact of history) that many of us are used to solar, rather than lunar calendars.

A day: The time it takes for the Earth to rotate once around its axis, today split into 24 hours.

There are a number of ways we can address this question,  but fossils  -  including corals  -  are one! How,  I hear you ask? Well, let’s find out by figuring out the length of a day in the Devonian period. Cool idea, huh?

In order for this to work, you need some key info:

- Today’s corals grow a small amount every day.  Each day’s growth leaves a really small but visible growth line on the epitheca.

- Modern corals also have bands,  comprising a number of growth lines.  These seem to correspond with lunar cycles  (our lunar  -  or synodic  -  month).  These may be linked to tidal effects,  nutrient variability,  or a lunar breeding periodicity.

- Finally,  some modern corals produced thickened growth bands  (annulations),  which are yearly.  These might reflect changes in water temperature across seasons.
