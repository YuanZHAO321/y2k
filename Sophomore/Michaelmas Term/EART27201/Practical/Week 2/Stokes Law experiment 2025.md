## BACKGROUND AND RELEVANCE

Particle settling is extremely important in sediment transport and deposition.  As soon as grains are entrained into a flow, they begin to fall back to the bed.  The distance that they travel down current before they again come to rest on the bed depends on the drag and lift forces exerted by the current (including turbulence) and the _settling velocity_ of the particles.

Sediment particles also settle through static or very slowly moving fluids, for example silt from river mouth plumes entering the ocean, dead pelagic shells sinking through the ocean, or ash and pumice from a volcanic eruption.

As we discussed last week, a knowledge of particle settling provides the basis for a number of hydraulic methods for the measurement of particle size.

However, the concepts you will think about in this class have a broad application that extends beyond sedimentology.  For example, settling rates are often used in the food industry for separating dirt and debris from incoming raw materials or in the environmental sector for studying the dispersal of airborne pollutants such as smoke particles.

## ANALYSIS

The unrealistic result is the consequence of the inherent assumptions in Stokes’ Law about what contributes to the drag or fluid resistance force (FR).  This exercise illustrates the clear need to understand a formula _before_ you use it to make calculations or else your results will be nonsense!

Buoyancy is one contributing factor to the fluid resistance force on a particle (the upward directed force which slows the particle descent), as we saw in an extreme case when you tried to get the green balls to sink!  However, a particle moving down through a fluid also generates resistance due to the shape and size of the particle, and the fluid’s viscosity and density. We saw in Part A that to take care of this there is a term within the derivation of the FR called the drag coefficient, _CD_ .

In Stokes’ Law the _CD_ is based on the dynamics of the flow around the particle i.e. whether the flow around the grain is turbulent or laminar.  This doesn’t mean the whole flow has to be laminar- the grain is only aware of the flow directly around it.  Even in cases where water is turbulent (which is usually the case) if a grain is small enough in diameter relative to the characteristic diameter of the turbulence, then the grain will act as if it is locally in laminar flow (or if you prefer, the grain thinks it is in laminar flow).

_So how can we determine whether the flow around the grain is laminar or turbulent?_ In the lecture we saw that we can determine if a flow is laminar or turbulent using the Reynold’s number.  In this application of the Reynolds number the length scale, D, is the grain diameter rather than water depth.  Consequently this is called the grain Reynolds number (Regr) and is a measure of the degree of turbulence at the grain-fluid boundary (i.e. if the boundary layer around the particle is turbulent or laminar).

In equation form we can say the same thing as:

Grain Reynolds Number is equal to the settling velocity times the grain diameter divided by the kinematic viscosity of the fluid (i.e. molecular viscosity divided by the density), or

> A. For Re _gr_ <1 there is a laminar boundary layer in the vicinity of the grain and no turbulent eddies.

Therefore, the flow around the grain can be viewed as having laminar behaviour.

> B. For Re _gr_ >1<1000 the laminar boundary around the grain separates and there is a turbulent wake.

C. For Re _gr_ >1000 there is a turbulent boundary layer in the vicinity of the grain and a turbulent wake.

## **Part C: Influence of settling velocities on sediment sorting and textures**

Many textural attributes of sedimentary deposits, such as grain size grading, are probably a function of settling velocity.

## PROCEDURE AND OBSERVATIONS

## _Blue-caped Cylinder 1_

With the lid of the cylinder on tightly, shake the cylinder vigorously (i.e. create turbulence so that all the grains are entrained into the flow).  Let the cylinder sit.  Wait a few minutes until the grains have settled.  The fluid in this cylinder is water

This distribution is known as **normal grading** .  If a flow suddenly stops moving, all the grains will fall out and normal grading will occur.  Because the mass of a particle (which drives the downward motion of the grain) increases with the cube of the grain diameter, but the resisting forces only increase with the square of the grain diameter (or its surface area), larger particles settle more quickly than small particles.  Very small particles can be kept in suspension indefinitely by static charges and Brownian motion.
