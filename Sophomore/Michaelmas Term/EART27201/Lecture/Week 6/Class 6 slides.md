# Carbonate Rocks

The carbonate rocks make up 10 to 20% of sedimentary rocks.

Mostly marine in origin (some lacustrine carbonates) They largely consist of two types of rocks:

• Dolostones which are composed mostly of dolomite [CaMg(CO3)2] • Limestones which are composed mostly of calcite (CaCO3) or high Mg calcite [(Ca,Mg)CO3]

#### Carbonate Precipitation

- Modern sediments are made of two minerals, both CaCO3: Calcite and Aragonite Limestones often described as “chemically precipitated” but… Calcium carbonate removed from seawater in _two_ ways: • Chemically (inorganically)

- • Biochemically

Chemically (inorganically), by direct precipitation from supersaturated water – fine crystals in the water column, which then settle to the seafloor, or as successive spherical shells deposited around a nucleus particle – although calcite more stable in pure water, observed arganonite is precipitated from sea water

#### Carbonate Precipitation

###### Most however,

Biochemically, by organisms to construct skeletons

- calcareous algae: _Halimeda_ and other green algae, aragonite; _Lithothamnium_ and other reef-making red algae, calcite

- modern corals (hexacorals): aragonite

- Paleozoic corals (rugose corals, tabulate corals): probably calcite, by good preservation of structures

- brachiopods, bryozoans, foraminifera: calcite then and now

- echinoderms: calcite then and now; large single-crystal skeletal components, very durable

- trilobites: probably calcite, by good preservation of structures

- mollusks: mostly aragonite, sometimes partly or entirely calcite

- Aragonite is metastable and is not preserved in lithified limestones _Originally_ aragonite grains preserved as moulds or as calcite casts _Originally_ calcite grains preserved with original structures intact

###### Important effects

temperature _:_ as the water temperature increases, the equilibrium solubility of CO<sup>2</sup> decreases, so as sea water is warmed there is a tendency for CO<sup>2</sup> to be released back into the atmosphere and for CaCO<sup>3</sup> to be precipitated.

photosynthesis _:_ in photosynthesis, plants take up CO<sup>2</sup> from the environment and fix it in organic compounds in their tissues, thereby releasing oxygen.

where organisms live: carbonate-secreting marine invertebrates live in greatest numbers in the warm shallow parts of the oceans. Can secrete CaCO3 even from water that is not saturated in CaCO3, but they do it best and most abundantly where the water is saturated

# Limestones

As with terrigenous clastic sediments, limestones can be considered as comprising grains, matrix and cement Grains = allochemical particles or allochems Four common types: Bioclasts (plants and animals) Ooids Peloids Non-biogenic constituents Intraclasts See practical handout

Many limestones are composed of bioclasts - whole or fragmented shells and other organism hard parts

Bioclasts: Skeletal fragments

Whole or broken skeletons of organisms (fossils) e.g., gastropods, crinoids, bivalves, corals and plants (algae) Precipitated by organisms that formed elsewhere and became included in the carbonate sediment

Range in size from gravel to fine sand Relatively short distance of transport (not resistant) Degree of rounding and sorting of the grains may not be a reflection of the energy of the transporting medium, but may be biologically determined – inherited rounded shape – well sorted if many of the same size organisms die at the same place

###### Coccolithophores

- first appeared after Carnian Pluvial Episode (late Triassic) in epicontinental seas

- consist of an algal cell surrounded by calcite plates called coccoliths

Over half carbonate removed from ocean today is extracted by coccolithophorids Abundant floating in near surface water of the oceans (planktonic inhabiting the pelagic zone)

#### Formation of coccolith ooze and chalk

- When the coccolithophore dies, the sphere breaks apart and coccoliths accumulate on the ocean floor

- Coccolith ooze on the ocean floor lithifies to chalk

- Coccolithophores are a major food source for herbivorous plankton whose faecal pellets are deposited in pelagic sediments

- Influence the global carbon cycle because CO2 is effectively removed from the atmosphere and buried on the sea floor

#### Ancient Pelagic Sediments: Cretaceous chalk

Ancient pelagic sediments sometimes accumulated in shallower water than those of today

- Classic case is Upper Cretaceous Chalk, a cemented coccolith ooze with a diverse benthonic fauna including molluscs and echinoids

- Accumulated in water at most a couple of hundred metres deep

- Cretaceous sea-levels very high, reduced terrigenous input, pelagic facies spread into shallower water

#### Ancient Pelagic Sediments: Differences

Older pelagic sediments differ from modern sediments because the nature of plankton changes:

- Planktonic forams and coccolithophorids only become important in Cretaceous

- In Triassic and early Jurassic there were some pelagic bivalves and there were larger organisms such as ammonites and belemnites

- In Palaeozoic calcareous plankton almost absent

- Radiolaria have been around throughout Phanerozoic

- Pelagic sediments of Palaeozoic and later times below CCD dominated by Radiolarian Cherts

- Radiolaria made of opaline silica SiO2.nH2O

- This eventually recrystallizes to quartz siliceous ooze

# Porosity in Sedimentary Rocks

Porosity is the amount of void space, expressed as volume %.

- may be filled with air, water or, occasionally, hydrocarbon

• some porosity is primary – formed at the time of deposition

• other porosity is secondary – it has appeared in the rock as a result of some post-depositional process

###### The CCD controls the distribution of siliceous and calcareous oozes on the ocean floor

- CaCO3 solubility increases with depth (due to temperature decrease and pressure increase)

- At CCD rate of calcium carbonate material forming and sinking is equal to the rate the material is dissolving

- Most opaline silica dissolves in surface waters (warmer, Al/Si of shells, high surface area) only about 3% of global silica flux is buried in sediment

#### The depth of the CCD has changed through time

Distribution of pelagic and neritic sediments today

- Cretaceous - early Eocene the CCD was much shallower due to intense volcanic activity = higher atmospheric CO2 concentrations (higher pCO2 in oceans) → increased dissolved CO 2 in surface waters

- • Palaeozoic and early Mesozoic, prior to evolution of coccolithophores, siliceous oozes may have deposited at much shallower depths than when there was calcareous plankton available for sedimentation
