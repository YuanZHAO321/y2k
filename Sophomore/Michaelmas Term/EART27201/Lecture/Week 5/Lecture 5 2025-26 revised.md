### Why do we care?

Observing and correctly identifying individual sedimentary structures and bedforms allow us to interpret the palaeohydraulic conditions under which they formed

Studying vertical (and lateral) sequences, allow us to interpret changes in space and time in:

 direction of flow (palaeotransport direction)  strength of flow and flow depth  duration and variability of flow And therefore to interpret:  depositional environments  changing climate and sea levels  provenance and tectonic processes

### Classification of Sedimentary Structures

Definition: Features, on the scale of hand specimens to large outcrops, produced within a depositional environment, during or (usually) not long after deposition

Classified on basis of:

 Kind of mechanism that produces them (i.e., physical, chemical, or biological)  Time of development relative to time of deposition (i.e., primary or secondary)

### Stratification

 Stratification: layering brought about by deposition

 Caused by changes in depositional conditions with time

 Layering, aka stratification, aka bedding, is not exclusive to sedimentary rocks.  Also occurs in lavas, plutonic and metamorphic rocks

 Two key questions to consider:

 What it was about depositional conditions that changed with time to give rise to stratification?  What it is about the rock itself that makes the stratification manifest?

 To identify stratification look for vertical changes from bed to bed in:

 grain size (texture)  composition (maybe indicated by colour, differential weathering)  structure

 or, more rarely, grain shape, packing, or orientation

### Terminology

### Beds and bedding surfaces

Beds are bounded above and below by bedding surfaces or planes  Can be readily recognised where defined by changes in lithology Bedding surfaces are produced during periods of non-deposition, or abrupt change in depositional conditions.  Non-deposition commonly associated with erosion

Surfaces are essentially time synchronous (form in hours, days, years = instant in geologic time); therefore, a bed is a time-stratigraphic unit of limited areal extent and relatively short time span

### How bed forms make cross stratification

Cross stratification is formed by the erosion and deposition associated with a train of bed forms

As the trough of the upstream ripple moves downstream, material is eroded from the stoss and deposited on the lee face of the downstream ripple

If no new material is added to the bed while the ripples move, then the average elevation of the bed does not change with time

 the foresets deposited by a downstream ripple are entirely eroded away again as the next trough upstream passes by

If bedform stopped moving abruptly, youd see most recent position of the lee faces preserved

### Origin of angle of climb

If new sediment is added to build the bed the will have a everywhere upward, ripples component of upward movement

The angle of climb (θ) of the ripples depends on the ratio of rate of bed aggradation to speed of ripple movement

As the ripples climb in space, their troughs climb with them.

The erosion surface associated with the downstream movement of a given trough passes above the erosion surface that was formed when the preceding trough passed by.

The lowest parts of the foresets deposited by the ripple that was located between those two troughs are then preserved rather than eroded entirely .

This remnant set is bounded both above and below by erosion surfaces.

### Climbing cross stratification

At high angles of climb, the entire ripple profile is preserved, and there are no erosion surfaces in the deposit

At low angles of climb, only the lower parts of foreset deposits are preserved • the individual sets are bounded by erosion surfaces

Climbing ripple cross stratification

The height of the sets is always less than the height of the bed forms that were responsible for the cross stratification

### Graded bedding

Bed = one flow event

Graded bedding in ~10cm-thick beds Grading is from coarse sand at base to fine sand at top

### Sole Marks

-  Sole marks: a group of sedimentary structures normally seen on the underside of beds in positive relief

-  A common feature of successions of alternating sandstones and mudstones with the structures seen on the bases of the sandstones

-  Result from a short-lived current acting on a cohesive or semi-cohesive bed, usually fine grained sediment (mud)

-  Two broad classes:

-  Scour marks: caused by erosion (turbulent scour) of the bed by a strong current (include obstacle scours, longitudinal scours and gutters and flute casts (aka flute marks)

-  Tool marks: caused by mechanical disruption of the bed by objects carried by a strong current
