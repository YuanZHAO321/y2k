VEI 4 eruptions should occur less frequently than every 1 year, but more frequently than once every 10 years. This means that we should have observed the next VEI 4 eruption at Lascar some time before 2003.

Since the 1993 eruption, Lascar has produced two VEI 3 eruptions (in 2005 and 2006), seven VEI 2 and three VEI 1 eruptions. There has not been a VEI 4 eruption since 1993. In light of this information, comment on whether the VEI is a reliable indicator of eruption frequency.

The pattern of historic activity at Lascar does indicate that there is an inverse correlation between eruption frequency and eruption magnitude – smaller eruptions happen more frequently. However, the style and frequency of activity is actually somewhat unpredictable. Eruptions of the same magnitude are not necessarily evenly spaced in time, and the repose period between eruptions of the same VEI is not necessarily the same as predicted by the VEI. This is particularly true at the higher end of the VEI scale.

Critical analysis: The VEI is very useful for quantitative classification of recent eruptions where deposit thicknesses can rapidly be measured in the field, and eruption column height can be measured using groundor satellite-based observations. What problems might arise when trying to reconstruct the VEI of an ancient volcanic eruption, and why?

For an ancient volcanic eruption, the only information available to reconstruct VEI is the deposits preserved in the geological record. We need to know the distribution and thickness of the deposits – i.e., we need a good isopach map. So the limiting factor in reconstructing the VEI is whether or not the deposits are preserved in the geological record so that a reliable isopach map can be constructed. This presents numerous challenges, for example:

- If most of the deposits landed in a marine, lacustrine or glacial environment, they may be inaccessible.

- All pyroclastic deposits will be susceptible to erosion. Thin, distal deposits will be more susceptible to complete removal than thick, proximal deposits.

- Volcanic deposits could be disturbed by later faulting and/or tectonic activity.

- Disturbance or removal of deposits will lead to the volume of erupted material being underestimated. Depending on the scale of removal, this could lead to the VEI being underestimated.

The uncertainties and limitations of isopach maps are well understood by field volcanologists, and in some instances they can be corrected for. Despite the known limitations, VEI is still a useful tool for estimating the magnitude and explosivity of ancient eruptions, and this in turn is useful for predicting the style and magnitude of future explosive activity that could occur at the source volcano (if it is still active).

# **<u>Ignimbrites, Rambla de las Negras, Cabo de Gata, Spain</u>**

Notes: A debris avalanche deposit is another term for a block-and-ash deposit. Fumarolic pipes are gas escape pipes; they form when there are trapped gases within the ignimbrite flow. The trapped gases could come from still-degassing pumices, or from vapourised groundwater.

# Key observations:

The most obvious feature of this outcrop is the spectacular reverse grading. The sorting is poor. The sharp basal contact and erosive top contact are both visible: this flow unit was deposited on top of a pale white ignimbrite deposit, and is overlain by a debris avalanche deposit. The predominant clast type appears to be light grey pumices. There is a huge range of clast types, and the range of clast types appears to with height. There are some fumarolic gas pipes to the right of the outcrop which appear to be well consolidated and have a dark yellow-brown colour.

The debris avalanche deposit is very coarse and poorly sorted. The basal contact is irregular and erosive. There is some evidence for reverse grading, with finer material being found immediately above the base of the bed. There is possibly some weak indication of clast imbrication.

# Interpretation:

This is an ignimbrite deposited by a clast-rich pyroclastic flow, most probably caused by a collapsing eruption column. The style of eruption was therefore probably vulcanian or Plinian – it can’t have been a Hawaiian or Strombolian eruption because these styles do not produce eruption columns. The fumarolic pipes could indicate that the pumices were still in the process of degassing and vesiculating when they were deposited – in which case, they must still have been quite hot when they were deposited – although not hot enough to weld. The clast size and unit thickness suggest that this outcrop represents a high-energy density current. This could mean that the outcrop is located relatively proximal to the vent and/or that the deposit represents quite a large eruption (VEI 3+).

The debris avalanche deposit was deposited by a block-and-ash flow. This could have been generated by subsequent collapse of a volcanic dome (a ‘hot’ debris avalanche) or else by the collapse of a crater wall or unstable slope on the volcano (a ‘cold’ debris avalanche). Clast imbrication suggests that the flow direction could have been right to left on the outcrop, although the observational evidence for this is not strong.

There are several different pyroclastic units in this outcrop. The lowest unit is a poorly sorted unwelded ignimbrite deposit. The clasts are angular and irregular; they mainly comprise grey-white pumices in a fine grey matrix; there are also dark grey to black clasts that are probably lithic fragments. # Interpretation:

The three units probably represent three different eruptive events. The lowest unit was deposited by a clastrich pyroclastic flow, probably generated by a collapsing eruption column.

The second unit is a pyroclastic surge deposit; the evidence for this is the fine lamination and the sedimentary structures like truncated cross-bedding. The surge deposit represents a dilute pyroclastic density current – these are possibly produced when conditions in the eruption column are very close to the boundary condition that separates stable convection from column collapse.

The uppermost unit is labelled as an ash fall deposit, presumably because the thin individual beds are normally graded. If it is a fall deposit then it must represent pyroclastic fallout from a stable convecting eruption column. However, the deposit does not appear to be laterally continuous, nor to mantle the topography of the underlying pyroclastic surge deposit – so perhaps it represents a second pyroclastic surge. An alternative possibility is that the unit has been disturbed by post-depositional faulting that destroyed the lateral continuity of the fall deposit.
