# **Don’t try to interpret the order of crystallization in mantle rocks!**

The Earth’s mantle is solid. Mantle rocks undergo partial melting to produce basaltic melt. Depleted mantle rocks are the residue (leftover) material after partial melts have been extracted. Fertile mantle rocks have not yet undergone significant melting. The last time the whole mantle was molten was ~4.4 Ga, and this early Hadean magma ocean had fully cooled and solidified by ~4.2 Ga. Over the past ~4 Ga the original crystallization textures have been completely overprinted by solid-state processes including plastic deformation, diffusion, recrystallization, and Ostwald ripening. This means that it is not useful or meaningful to attempt to determine an order of crystallization for a mantle rock. Instead, look for polygonal textures with ~120° triple junctions between grains. This texture forms because it minimises the surface free energy at grain boundaries, but it requires long timescales to form by solid-state diffusion and recrystallization.

# **A. Samples from ophiolites**

Ophiolites are slices of oceanic lithosphere that, instead of being recycled into the Earth’s mantle at a subduction zone, are preserved by being thrust onto continental crust. At the base of a typical ophiolite sequence is mantle peridotite. This peridotite is the source of the basaltic magma that goes on to crystallize and form the oceanic crust. The peridotite at the base of ophiolite sections has therefore undergone partial melting.

# <u>NC1</u> _New Caledonia_

The two main primary minerals in this rock are olivine and orthopyroxene. The olivine is extensively altered to pale green serpentine. The orthopyroxene contains exsolution lamellae of clinopyroxene. There is also minor spinel, which has a deep red-brown colour in plane-polarised light. Classify the rock based on the relative proportions of the main silicate minerals. Is your classification consistent with the tectonic setting of this sample?

Harzburgite. Yes, this is consistent with the rock being part of an ophiolite sequence. The mantle peridotite at the base of ophiolites has undergone partial melting, and harzburgite represents the residue left behind after partial melt has been extracted from lherzolite.

The texture of this rock indicates that it has been deformed (tectonised). What evidence for deformation is there? Suggest a cause of the deformation. Where might the water have come from which caused the serpentinisation?

Evidence for deformation: (1) Bent opx, shaped like **augens** (this is actually a metamorphic term), which are wrapped around by serpentine. The augen texture is also visible in the hand specimen. (2) Olivine appears to have been stretched and broken into subgrains (this is not always easy to see because of the extensive serpentinization). The deformation probably occurred when the oceanic lithosphere was obducted onto continental crust.

The water that caused the serpentinization of olivine is most probably seawater, in which case the serpentinization occurred either before or during ophiolite emplacement onto the continental crust. Further alteration could have occurred after emplacement due to circulation of meteoric water, although this alone could not explain the full extent of serpentinization in this sample.

# **B. Mantle xenoliths in kimberlites**

<u>BT2</u> **or** <u>15565</u> _Kimberley, South Africa_ (hand specimens for BT2)

There are four main silicate minerals in these samples. The hand specimens of BT2 show the different colours of these minerals very clearly. The clinopyroxene is chrome diopside, and it has a distinctive bottle green colour due to its high chromium content. Look at the slide by eye and try to pick out the four minerals, including one pink mineral and one green mineral. Both samples contain minor phlogopite, a light brown mica that looks very similar to biotite. Identify the four main silicate minerals, estimate their modal proportions, and classify the rock.

Olivine, clinopyroxene, orthopyroxene, garnet. This is a garnet lherzolite.

Could this be a sample of fertile mantle?

Yes. “Fertile” means that the rock has previously undergone no (or little) partial melting; therefore it could undergo partial melting to produce a basaltic liquid. The residue left behind after melt extraction is referred to as depleted mantle, and contains fewer minerals (usually just olivine and opx). Rocks BT2 and 15565 contain cpx, garnet, and some minor phlogopite; all of these minerals would be consumed if the rock were to melt.

The bottle-green mineral is chrome diopside. Pure diopside is a clinopyroxene with composition CaMgSi2O6. Chrome diopside has the formula Ca(Mg,Cr)Si2O6 – in other words, some of the Mg has been substituted by Cr. Chrome diopside is common in mantle xenoliths.

What does the mineralogy of this rock tell you about its depth of origin relative to that of sample 15565/BT2?

The aluminous phase in S.115 is spinel. The aluminous phase in BT2/15565 is garnet. Garnet is stable at higher pressure than spinel, so S.115 must come from a lower pressure than BT2/15565. We can say that S.115 came from the spinel stability field, which is ~0.8-2.0 GPa. We would need additional geochemical information (e.g. to use in the Al-in-opx barometer) to arrive at a more precise pressure estimate.

4. What are the advantages and weaknesses of using this simple ternary phase diagram to understand mantle melting reactions?

Strengths: Ternary phase diagrams are simple to use, and very powerful. This diagram does a very good job of explaining the observed mineralogy of depleted mantle rocks.

Weaknesses: This particular ternary diagram simplifies the true mineralogy of real mantle samples. It does not show the aluminous (garnet, spinel) or hydrous (phlogopite) phases, all of which would be involved in the melting reactions.

# <u>BF2</u> _Bultfontein, South Africa_

Most kimberlites are fragmental rocks. This is not a typical kimberlite, either mineralogically or texturally. It comes from a small sill associated with a true kimberlite.

There are three main minerals in this sample. Two of these are non-silicates. The first is perovskite (CaTiO3), a brown, isotropic, square-shaped mineral. What is the second non-silicate mineral? What does it tell you about the chemical composition of the magma?

Calcite: it has high relief, and very high birefringence (pale pink/green; may look like a sandy brown). Calcite is a carbonate mineral, CaCO3. The most likely source of the carbonate is CO2 dissolved in the kimberlite magma. This tells us that kimberlite magmas are extremely volatile-rich. (Kimberlites also have very high H2O contents, but this is not preserved in the mineral record.)

Describe the texture of this rock, and the shape of the olivine crystals. Many of these olivine crystals are **xenocrysts** : they are derived directly from the mantle, and were incorporated into the kimberlite magma as it drilled through the mantle and lithosphere on its way to the surface. The olivine crystals have reaction rims of serpentine. Where might the water have come from that caused the serpentinisation?

The overall texture is porphyritic. Some of the olivines are euhedral, some are subhedral, and some are rounded. The rounded shapes suggest that these crystals were not in chemical equilibrium with the kimberlite magma, and have been partially resorbed (re-dissolved). The serpentinization surrounding the olivines is caused by a chemical reaction between olivine and hydrous fluids. The fluids come from the kimberlite magma itself.
