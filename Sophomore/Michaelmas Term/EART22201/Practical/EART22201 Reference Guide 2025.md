###### **3. Presence or absence of phenocrysts**

The texture is porphyritic if phenocrysts are present: use “XX-phyric” for a rock with phenocrysts of mineral XX. If the rock has no phenocrysts, use even-grained if the rock is crystalline, or aphyric if the rock is a lava or is glassy.

The above three descriptions can usually be written down in a single sentence. Example: “This melanocratic rock is hypocrystalline, very fine-grained, and is olivine-phyric.”

#### **Thin Sections**

**1. Describe the features visible to the naked eye:** grain size, presence or absence of phenocrysts, any layering or banding, any contacts or inclusions.

**2. Examine the thin section under the microscope.** Before writing or drawing anything, look around the whole section to make sure you have seen all the different minerals and an overview of the rock texture.

###### **3. Drawing a good thin section diagram**

A thin section diagram is often referred to as a “sketch”, but in reality it is a technical drawing that captures a representative view of the rock, its mineralogy and its key textural features.

- Make the sketch large. Give yourself at least half a page to sketch and add labels. A good size for the sketch would be 10 cm.

- Draw at an appropriate scale for the grain size of the rock. You may need to do several drawings at different scales. If the rock is very coarse grained, you will need to draw a zoomed-out view to convey the texture.

- Include a scale bar. You can estimate the field of view by putting a transparent ruler under the microscope. Don’t use a “x10” or “x20” label – this only gives information about the objective lens you used to make the observations; it doesn’t translate to the scale of your diagram.

- Use line weight, shading and colour to add detail.

- The sketch should show all the different minerals in the rock, and all the textural features that you mention in your description.

Formal reports such as dissertations often present photographs of thin sections instead of handdrawn sketches. The author needs to choose whether plane-polarized light or crossed polars (or both) will best show the important features of the rock. Photographs must have a scale bar and an appropriate caption, and can include unobtrusive labels.

###### **4. Writing a good thin section description**

The thin section description contains both observations and interpretation. It concisely summarizes the observational information contained in the sketch, adding details where needed. It then uses the observational evidence to interpret the sequence of events that formed the rock.

- **a) Describe the individual minerals present.** Describe the minerals in the following order:

   - Primary minerals – large crystals (e.g. phenocrysts, cumulus crystals)

   - Primary minerals – small crystals (e.g. groundmass, interstitial minerals)

   - Accessory minerals, e.g. spinel, opaque oxides, apatite, sphene, zircon

   - Secondary minerals, e.g. serpentine, chlorite

4

The mineral description should include some or all of the following information:

- Size, habit and shape of the grains

- Colour and pleochroism

- Composition (only if it is possible to obtain this information from the thin section)

- Any twinning, zoning or exsolution

- Any alteration (note which mineral has been altered, e.g. olivine to serpentine)

You may find it useful to arrange the information in a list or table.

###### **b) Describe the texture**

The texture is the spatial and geometric relationship between the crystals. Describe features such as:

- A preferred mineral alignment or banding

- One mineral included within another

- The range of crystal sizes. Is one mineral noticeably larger or smaller than the others? Are any minerals present across a range of crystal sizes?

- Is there any evidence of deformation or compaction, e.g. bent twin planes, undulose extinction?

The key textural features should be captured and labelled in the sketch.

Use the texture **determine the order of crystallization** as far as possible. In most igneous systems, multiple minerals crystallize together simultaneously, so Bowen’s Reaction Series is not usually good evidence for the order of crystallization. Instead, look at the rock’s texture to try to distinguish ‘earlier’ from ‘later’ phases. Textural evidence to support the proposed order of crystallization might include:

- When one mineral has grown around another, the surrounded mineral is older and the enclosing mineral is younger.

- Early-formed crystals are generally euhedral. Later-forming minerals must grow around any pre-existing crystals so tend to have less well-formed crystal shapes.

- If the rock contains both large and small crystals of the same mineral, the large ones grew first.

###### **5. Classify the rock**

The mineralogical and textural information you have gathered should allow you to classify the rock by selecting an appropriate classification scheme. Usually, igneous rocks are named on the basis of the modal proportions of the major minerals (i.e. _essential_ and _type_ minerals).

###### **6. Interpret the sequence of events that formed the rock**

The interpretation should be supported by your observations and might address questions such as: Is the rock intrusive or extrusive? Which crystals grew slowly, and which grew rapidly? Which crystals grew first? What igneous environment could produce crystals with these shapes, sizes or textures? What tectonic environment might this rock have formed in (e.g., mid-ocean ridge, subduction zone volcano)? You may wish to include diagrams or cartoons of the relevant geological processes.

### **Classification and nomenclature of igneous rocks**

Igneous rocks can be classified according to their chemical composition, or by their mineralogy. The choice of classification scheme will depend on the types of information available. If only thin sections or hand specimens are available, a petrological scheme must be used. Compositional classification schemes such as TAS require a geochemical analysis of the rock.

The scheme below uses essential and type minerals to classify igneous rocks. It can be used in the lab or in the field.

###### **How to use this scheme:**

1. Identify the minerals in the rock.

2. Assign the root name based on the essential minerals and the grain size. (NB the root names in

- the table apply only to fine-grained rocks.)

3. Add qualifiers based on the type minerals present.

6

Essential minerals are implied by the root name. Example: the rock name “granite” tells you that the rock contains quartz, plagioclase and alkali feldspar.

Type minerals are not implied by the root name. Example: the rock name “granite” doesn’t tell you whether there are any minerals _in addition to_ quartz, plagioclase and alkali feldspar. The rock name is therefore used to describe the type minerals. If there is more than one type mineral, they are listed with the least abundant first. Example: biotite muscovite granite.

Essential and type minerals together constitute the major minerals. Minerals that are not major minerals are accessory or secondary.

- Accessory minerals constitute only a small proportion of the rock (usually less than 5%). They are not important for classifying the rock type, composition and origin. They are not included in the rock name. Common examples: opaque oxides; apatite.

- Secondary minerals form after the magma had completely crystallised. They may fill void spaces or replace original minerals. They are not included in the rock name. Common examples: serpentite (alteration of olivine); chorite (alteration of biotite); zeolites (filling amygdales).

Some additional pointers:

- For andesites and diorites, the mafic mineral is often included in the rock name. Example: hornblende andesite.

- Diorite can usually be distinguished from gabbro by its abundant hornblende.

- If a mineral occurs as phenocrysts in a fine-grained rock, it can be included in the rock name by adding “XX-phyric” to the root name. This nomenclature can be used even if the phenocryst plase is one of the essential minerals. Example: plagioclase-phyric basalt.

##### **Granites and granitoids**

Quartz-Alkali feldspar-Plagioclase-Feldsathoid (QAPF) classification. Used for silica-saturated rocks containing the essential minerals Quartz-Alkali feldspar-Plagioclase (QAP), and for silicaundersaturated rocks containing the essential minerals Alkali feldspar-Plagioclase-Feldspathoid (APF). A rock _cannot_ contain both quartz and feldspathoid. In EART 22201 we will not study rocks that contain feldspathoid minerals.

To use the QAPF scheme:

1. Determine the modal mineralogy of the sample. Estimate the % of the essential minerals quartz (Q), alkali feldspar (A), plagioclase (P) and feldspathoid (F) in the sample.

2. If there is no feldspathoid, scale the proportions of Q, A and P so that they add up to 100%, and plot the result on the QAP triangle.

3. If there is no quartz, scale the proportions of A, P and F so that they add up to 100%, and plot the result on the APF triangle. The feldspathoid (“foid” for short) in the diagram below is nepheline, but if the rock contains a different feldspathoid (e.g. leucite) then this is substituted for nepheline in the diagram.

4. The QAP(F) classification provides the root name of the rock. Further classification can be achieved by examining the type minerals of the rock.

### **Common igneous terms used in rock descriptions**

##### **1. Degree of crystallinity**

holocrystalline The rock is entirely composed of crystals. hypocrystalline The rock consists partly of crystals and partly of a glassy base. holohyaline The rock consists entirely of glass. The terms glassy and vitric are also used.

##### **2. Sizes of individual crystals**

aphanitic None of the crystals (except phenocrysts) can be seen by the naked eye. Rock cooled quickly so apply volcanic terminology.

phaneritic All the principal minerals are visible to the naked eye. Rock cooled slowly so apply plutonic terminology.

groundmass A fine-grained crystalline matrix in which larger crystals may be embedded. microcrystalline Groundmass crystals can be distinguished under the microscope. cryptocrystalline Groundmass crystals cannot be distinguished under the microscope.

equigranular Crystals are all approximately equal in size. inequigranular Crystals have a range of different sizes.

Textural terms should normally be accompanied by estimates or measurements of the crystal size.

##### **3. Crystal shape and habit**

12

- euhedral Shows perfect or near-perfect crystal form. Typical of early-crystallizing minerals.

- subhedral Some crystal faces are moderately well developed. anhedral Crystal faces are absent; has an irregular or rounded shape. skeletal Shows a branched, tree-like form with internal cavities, often with an overall euhedral outline. Forms under very rapid growth conditions.

- embayed Crystal faces are dimpled, caused by partial resorption or dissolution of the mineral.

- sieve Abundant small and interconnected glass inclusions found inside the crystal, giving it a spongy or porous appearance. Usually seen in feldspars in volcanic rocks, e.g. sieve-textured plagioclase.

#### **4. Textural relationships of crystals**

###### **Equigranular textures**

- granular Most of the grains are approximately equant. granitic Medium- to coarse-grained rocks in which all or most of the minerals are equal in size and display interlocking borders. Most of the grains are anhedral or subhedral. Generally used for rocks of granitic composition.

- microgranitic Most of the grains are anhedral or subhedral, and <5 mm diameter.

#### **How to distinguish between olivine and augite**

Olivine and augite (clinopyroxene) are often found together in basalts. They have similar relief and interference colours and are sometimes difficult to tell apart. Here are some key features to look for in distinguishing between them. Those in italics are most diagnostic.

#### **How to distinguish between orthopyroxene and clinopyroxene**

- <u>Extinction angle is the most widely used means of distinguishing between orthorhombic and</u> monoclinic pyroxenes. To use this method, take care to select a crystal that shows a single cleavage trace (not two cleavages), and that shows the highest available interference colour.

- Colour and pleochroism: Clinopyroxenes may have a pink to brown colour, and some may show pleochroism. Orthopyroxene is usually colourless and non-pleochroic. The only exception is enstatite (Mg2Si2O6 orthopyroxene) which sometimes exhibits a weak but very distinctive pale pink to pale green pleochroism.

- Interference colours. Orthopyroxenes have low first-order interference colours. Most clinopyroxenes have higher interference colours – typically second-order.

- Twinning. Orthopyroxenes never exhibit twinning, whereas clinopyroxenes may do so.
