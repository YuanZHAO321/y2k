- 4) These rocks are related to one another by fractional crystallization. What do the sudden changes in trend mean? Briefly inspect Table 1 and comment on how other major element oxide contents change as SiO2 increases.

The change in trend must indicate a change in the crystallizing assemblage. In other words, each trend is associated with the crystallization of a different mineral (or group of minerals). Or, alternatively, the same minerals are crystallizing but in different proportions.

From the table: Fractional crystallization causes the Na2O and K2O contents to increase – this means that Na and K are not being taken up in the crystallizing minerals. The CaO content decreases during crystallization, so it must be being taken up in the crystallizing minerals (most probably into cpx and plagioclase). The TiO2 content increases to ~2 wt.%, but then decreases – so a mineral that takes up Ti must start crystallizing when the SiO2 content reaches ~55 wt.%. The Al2O3 content remains fairly constant during crystallization.

The next task is to determine what crystallising mineral(s) can produce this liquid line of descent. We will work on the graph of FeO vs. MgO that has been provided for you.

- 8) We will now examine the liquid line of descent that links the magma compositions between ~10 and ~5 wt.% MgO. Add a linear line of best fit through these compositions, and use this best-fit line to predict the possible combinations of crystallizing minerals that could explain the chemical trend.

The back-projection of the trend that links these rock compositions intersects the plag-cpx and plag-ol tie lines, and crosses through the triangle. It doesn’t intersect the cpx-ol tie line. So, the crystallizing assemblage could be any one of the following three options:

- olivine + plagioclase

- plagioclase + clinopyroxene

- olivine + plagioclase + clinopyroxene

There is not enough information to determine which of these three options is correct, but the most likely answer is the three-mineral assemblage.

- 9) Now look at the trend that links magma compositions between ~5 and 0 wt.% MgO. Can this trend be explained by crystallizing any combination of the three minerals on your plot?

No, it can’t – because the trend through these magma compositions doesn’t pass through the triangle or any of the tie lines linking olivine, plagioclase and cpx. This means that the crystallizing assemblage must change at ~5 wt.% MgO. This is consistent with the sudden change in trend.

- 10) What can you predict about the composition of the mineral that must join the crystallizing assemblage at ~5 wt.% MgO?

The trend through the low-MgO magma compositions shows a sharp decrease in FeO, so the new mineral must be Fe-rich. The most likely option is an oxide like magnetite (Fe3O4) or ilmenite (FeTiO3). Table 1 shows that the TiO2 content of the magmas starts to decrease at the same point the FeO contents start to decrease – so this suggests that ilmenite is the new mineral, because ilmenite crystallization will remove both Fe and Ti from the melt.

- 12) The most MgO-rich basaltic melt composition that has ever been measured in Iceland contains ~11 wt.% MgO. The picrites all contain >11 wt.% MgO. Fractional crystallization (removal of crystals) always causes MgO to decrease, so the picrite compositions cannot have been produced by fractional crystallization. What alternative process could produce the high-MgO picrites?

If it’s not crystallization (removal of crystals) then it must be addition of crystals. Crystal accumulation and entrainment can happen if an erupting magma picks up crystals from the floor of the magma chamber before it erupts, or if the magma picks up crystals from old magma chambers as it makes its way to the surface. To explain the picrite compositions, the added crystals must be of MgO-rich minerals.

- 13) In light of your answer to (9), predict the abundance and types of phenocrysts you might expect to find in the picrite rocks.

The trend line through the picrites passes through the ol-cpx and plag-ol tie lines of the triangle, and through the centre of the triangle. So the magma must “pick up” a crystal assemblage that contains either:

- olivine + plagioclase

- olivine + clinopyroxene

- olivine + plagioclase + clinopyroxene

The common feature is that all the possible accumulated crystal assemblages contain olivine – and this is consistent with the requirement that the magma must be accumulating Mg-rich minerals. Picrites are therefore basaltic rocks that contain a high proportion of olivine phenocrysts, plus or minus some cpx and/or plagioclase.

# **Part 2 (15 minutes): Observations of mineral textures**

# <u>93-2 or R342A basalt</u>

Examine the hand specimen. You should see two different phenocryst minerals in a glassy groundmass. What are the phenocryst minerals?

The phenocrysts are olivine and plagioclase. The texture of the hand specimen is glassy.

Examine the thin section. Before you put it under the microscope, hold the thin section up to the light and notice that it contains light and dark regions. You should also be able to see the phenocrysts.

Look at the light part of the thin section. In plane-polarised light the groundmass is light brown, but under crossed polars it is isotropic. It is therefore glass (which is an amorphous solid, and has isotropic optical properties). What does the presence of glass tell you about how quickly the magma cooled?

Basalt glass only forms when the cooling rate is extremely fast.

The glass contains phenocrysts of two different silicate minerals. What are they? Sketch the characteristic morphology (shape) of the phenocrysts. What does their size and shape tell you about the environment in which they grew? Comment on the likely ratio between the nucleation rate N and the growth rate G when the phenocrysts formed.

Olivines are typically euhedral. Plagioclases are typically tabular laths. The near-perfect crystal morphologies suggest that the minerals grew freely and unimpeded in the melt (liquid). The phenocrysts most probably grew in a magma chamber.

When the phenocrysts formed, G > N. This happens at small degrees of undercooling – that is, the magma was only a few degrees cooler than its liquidus temperature. The nucleation rate is low, so few crystals form. But the growth rate is moderate to high, so any crystals that do nucleate can grow to large sizes.

Some of the minerals in the light part of the thin section have a skeletal morphology, which is caused when the crystal edges grow faster than the faces. Sketch one of the skeletal olivine crystals.

Moving towards the centre of the thin section, notice the brown rims which appear around the phenocrysts. These rims are composed of radiating feathery crystals which nucleated on the phenocrysts during the rapid cooling of the basaltic melt. Comment on the likely ratio between the nucleation rate N and the growth rate G when the feathery crystals formed.

The feathery crystals form when G << N, at very high degrees of undercooling – that is, the magma temperature was dropped suddenly to several tens of degrees below the liquidus. The nucleation rate is very high, so lots of crystals can form. The growth rate is very low, so the crystals remain small.

# Suggest a possible environment for the formation of this basalt.

The thickness of glass observed in these thin sections and hand specimens can only be produced during rapid cooling. There are no vesicles, which tells us that gas was not escaping from the magma. This means that it must have erupted under a confining pressure – most probably under water. (Not under air – otherwise there would be vesicles.) The most probable tectonic environment is a mid-ocean ridge. A subglacial eruption would also produce similar textures.
