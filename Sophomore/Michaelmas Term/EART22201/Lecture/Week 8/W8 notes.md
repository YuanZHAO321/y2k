### The water required for flux melting comes from the downgoing slab

(I) Depth <20 km: Release of sedimentary pore fluids. Low temperature so no melting.

(II) Depth ~20-100 km: Further dehydration of sediments and oceanic crust. Temperature still too low for melting.

(III) Depth >100 km: Hydrous minerals in the subducting oceanic lithosphere break down and release their structurally bound water.

Rüpke et al. (2004) Earth Planet Sci Lett 223:17-34 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.epsl.2004.04.018

margaret.hartley@manchester.ac.uk

### The water required for flux melting comes from the downgoing slab

(a) Diagram showing expected water contents within a subduction zone. Colours represent rock H2O content. In the slab, H2O decreases with depth due to dehydration, then breakdown of hydrous minerals. The high-H2O region in the mantle wedge, beneath the volcanic arc, shows where flux melting of mantle peridotite begins.

(b) Diagram showing the schematic path of fluids derived by breakdown of hydrous minerals in the slab. The fluid migrates upwards into the mantle wedge (solid orange arrows), where it reacts with peridotite and forms new hydrous minerals. The hydrated mantle flows downwards (dashed arrows) and then dehydrates, re-releasing the fluid. After multiple hydration-dehydration steps, the fluid reaches a region of the mantle where the temperature is high enough for flux melting to occur.

Manning (2004) Earth Planet Sci Lett 223:1-16 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.epsl.2004.04.030

### Flux melting of peridotite occurs in the mantle wedge

Isotherms inverted Isotherms parallel to mantle flow lines

The vast majority of the magma volume at subduction zones is produced by partial melting of asthenospheric sub-arc mantle peridotite in the mantle wedge. Melting can occur in the garnet and spinel peridotite fields.

The slab is too cold to undergo partial melting.

margaret.hartley@manchester.ac.uk

EART 22201

### Does the slab itself ever melt?

Isotherms will be higher (and the system will be hotter) if:

• The convergence rate is slower

• The slab is young and near the ridge, therefore warm

Isotherms inverted Isotherms parallel to mantle flow lines

• The arc is young (<50-100 Ma)

Most of the downgoing slab **remains too cold** to undergo partial melting. However, some subduction zone magmas have chemical signatures that are consistent with partial melting of sediments or oceanic crust. This indicates that, under the right thermal conditions, it is possible for the uppermost parts of the slab to undergo partial melting.

**Calc-alkaline** fractionation trend shows no Fe enrichment

- Enhanced crystallization of Fe-Ti oxides in calc-alkaline magmas, due to oxidising conditions

### Recap: Types of incompatible trace element

**High field strength elements (HFSE)** Too highly charged to fit into crystal lattices Largely immobile in aqueous fluids

**Large ion lithophile elements (LILE)** Too large to fit into crystal lattices Highly mobile in aqueous fluids

**Rare earth elements (REE)** Show related behaviour during key geological processes like melting and crystallisation

### Trace element compositions of subduction zone magmas

###### **Nb**

Continental and island arc basalts are:

- Generally enriched in incompatible trace elements

### Chemical disequilibrium can cause magmatic corrosion

Changed magmatic conditions can cause a previously stable crystal to become chemically unstable with respect to the melt. The mineral begins resorbing (dissolving) into the melt.

Embayments

Forms by resorption without new growth. Common in quartz

#### Sieve texture

Forms by resorption followed by growth of new zone(s) once equilibrium is restored. Common in plagioclase
