Weathering Processes

In week 4 we introduced  the idea that the particles that make up sedimentary rocks come from weathering older sedimentary, metamorphic and igneous rocks.

But what are the processes responsible for weathering?

Weathering results from both chemical and mechanical processes. These slides summarises the main processes of each.

## A word of warning about clay minerals

- A confusing name!

- Clay is used for a grain size (<0.004 mm) and a group of

   - sheet silicate minerals

- Clay minerals are usually, but not always, clay-sized

• Clay-sized grains are not all clay minerals. e.g. the quartz produced by weathering reactions is often claysized too.

## Mechanical / Physical Weathering

Process by which rocks are broken into smaller fragments, but without significant change in chemical or mineralogical composition

Major mechanisms:

- _Freeze / thaw_

- _Salt weathering_

- _Diurnal heating and cooling_

- Biological activity (e.g. _Plant roots expanding)_

- _Stress-Release_

- _River incision_

- _Glacial activity_

- _Wave activity_

## Controls on weathering and erosion rates

Climate is main control on weathering rates

- Chemical reactions more rapid at higher temperatures

- Humid Tropics: chemical weathering is most rapid: intense leaching of both silicates and carbonate

- Temperate climates: chemical weathering less rapid but freeze/thaw becomes important particularly in upland areas, so mechanical weathering dominates

- Arid climates (hot or cold): weathering rates are low
