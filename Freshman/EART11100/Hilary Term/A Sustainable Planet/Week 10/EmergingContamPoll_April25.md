## Slide 7

### Contaminants vs Pollutants

- a contaminant is any physical, chemical, biological, or radiological substance present in the environment at levels that are not naturally occurring, or that are above natural background levels, but that does not necessarily cause harm

- UNEP (2021), Making Peace with Nature Report

- a pollutant is a contaminant that causes, or is likely to cause, adverse biological, chemical, or physical effects to organisms or ecosystems

- The transition from contaminant to pollutant is determined by evidence of harm

### Why is plastic an environmental issue?

- Plastics are persistent, lasting for decades to centuries without fully breaking down:

- 🧪 Strong Chemical Bonds: Long chains of carbon-carbon (C–C) or carbon-hydrogen (C–H) bonds that are very stable and hard to break under natural conditions

- 🦠 Resistance to Biological Attack: Most microbes and enzymes cannot recognize or break down synthetic plastic polymers; they evolved to degrade natural materials, not human-made ones

- 💧 Hydrophobicity: Plastics repel water, reducing chemical weathering processes like hydrolysis that would otherwise help degrade them

- 🧱 Physical Durability: Tough and flexibleeaning they resist mechanical damage, abrasion, and temperature changes that could otherwise help fragment and degrade materials

- Not all plastics are equally recyclable

- https://environment.govt.nz

- PET

- easily melted and reformed into new products without significant loss of quality

- consistent composition, high demand for recycled PET (rPET), and mature sorting infrastructure

- HDPE

- simple chemical structure and

- widespread use in bottles and containers; easy to sort, clean, and reprocess into high-quality recycled materials

- PVC

- contains several chemical additives that can release toxic substances during processing (e.g. phthalates)

- requires complex separation and cleaning steps; costly and environmentally risky compared to other plastics.

- PS

- lightweight but bulky = inefficient transportation

- tends to break into small fragments (microplastics)

- often contaminated with food

- very low market value when recycled

## Slide 19

### The reality of recycling

- Recyclable ≠ Recycled

- 💸 Cost: Virgin plastic is often cheaper than recycled plastic, reducing demand for recycled plastics

- 🛑 Infrastructure: Recycling systems vary widely

- 🚢 Global waste trade: Wealthy nations (e.g. the UK!) export plastic waste overseas, where it may be poorly managed, burned, or dumped rather than recycled

- 📜 Weak policy and regulation: Without strong legislation or incentives, recycling remains voluntary or economically unattractive for many industries

- 🔥 Contamination: Mixed or dirty plastics are costly to clean and recycle, meaning much collected material is incinerated or landfilled instead

## Slide 40

- Towards effective plastic remediation

- ♻️ A multi-pronged approach is essential:

- Waste reduction: Reducing plastic production and single-use plastic consumption

- Improved recycling: Mechanical and chemical recycling to recover valuable materials

- Active collection efforts: River booms, beach cleanups, ocean plastic interceptors.

- 🧪 Emerging innovations complement but do not replace core strategies:

- Biodegradable plastics offer potential but face scale and stability challenges.

- Microbial and enzymatic degradation (e.g., PETase) is promising but not yet sufficient for large-scale environmental remediation.

- Combination of reduction, recycling, and recovery is currently the most realistic and effective solution to the plastic crisis.

### PFAS (aka ‘Forever Chemicals’)

## Slide 42

- What are they?

- Synthetic compounds found in firefighting foams, stain-resistant fabrics, non-stick cookware, food packaging

- Characterized by extremely strong carbon–fluorine (C–F) bonds

## Slide 43

- Transport and persistence

- Highly mobile: leaches into groundwater and surface waters

- Resistant to chemical, biological, and thermal breakdown

- Found globally — from tap water to Arctic wildlife

## Slide 44

- Transport and persistence

## Slide 45

### Environmental behaviour

- Longer-chain PFAS (more carbon atoms) bioaccumulate more strongly

- Marine environments generally show higher bioaccumulation than freshwater or brackish systems

- Both perfluorocarboxylates (PFCA) and perfluorosulfonates (PFSA) accumulate, but patterns differ slightly by chemical type and ecosystem

## Slide 46

### Evidence of Harm

- Bioaccumulates in human and animal bloodstreams

- Linked to cancers (kidney, testicular), immune suppression, thyroid disease (EPA, 2022)

- Detected in drinking water, wildlife (even in remote Arctic ecosystems)

- Health advisories now recommend near-zero exposure levels

- Urgent regulatory and public health responses underway

- PFAS are clearly pollutants

## Slide 47

- Addressing the PFAS issue

- Capture technologies: Activated carbon, ion exchange resins

- Destruction methods: High-temperature incineration, plasma treatment (expensive)

- Nature-Based Solutions (early-stage): Wetland attenuation, sorption into organic-rich soils

- Prevention focus: Reducing use and emissions of PFAS at source

- No single "magic bullet" — integrated approaches needed

## Slide 50

### Key Takeaways

- 🌍 We are living in the Anthropocene — human activities are reshaping planetary systems

- 🔍 Emerging contaminants are substances of growing concern; they become pollutants when evidence of harm is demonstrated.

- 🧪 Plastics (macro and micro):

- Widespread, persistent, and fragmenting into microplastics

- Recycling is imperfect; solutions require waste reduction, improved recycling, and collection efforts

- Nature-based innovations like PETase and mussel filtration are promising but complementary

- 🧪 PFAS (‘Forever Chemicals’):

- Synthetic, highly mobile, and resistant to degradation

- Strong evidence of human and environmental harm; bioaccumulate across ecosystems

- Urgent need for capture technologies, destruction methods, and nature-based attenuation

- 🌱 Nature-Based Solutions (NBS) are essential but must complement prevention and innovation strategies

- ♻️ A multi-pronged approach — reduction, recycling, recovery, and innovation — is needed to tackle pollution and stay within planetary boundaries

### Paper of the Week

## Slide 53

- ABSTRACT

- Environmental exposure to active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) can have negative effects on the health of ecosystems and humans. While numerous studies have monitored APIs in rivers, these employ different analytical methods, measure different APIs, and have ignored many of the countries of the world. This makes it difficult to quantify the scale of the problem from a global perspective. Furthermore, comparison of the existing data, generated for different studies/regions/continents, is challenging due to the vast differences between the analytical methodologies employed. Here, we present a global-scale study of API pollution in 258 of the world’s rivers, representing the environmental influence of 471.4 million people across 137 geographic regions. Samples were obtained from 1,052 locations in 104 countries (representing all continents and 36 countries not previously studied for API contamination) and analyzed for 61 APIs. Highest cumulative API concentrations were observed in sub-Saharan Africa, south Asia, and South America. The most contaminated sites were in low- to middle-income countries and were associated with areas with poor wastewater and waste management infrastructure and pharmaceutical manufacturing. The most frequently detected APIs were carbamazepine, metformin, and caffeine (a compound also arising from lifestyle use), which were detected at over half of the sites monitored. Concentrations of at least one API at 25.7% of the sampling sites were greater than concentrations considered safe for aquatic organisms, or which are of concern in terms of selection for antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, pharmaceutical pollution poses a global threat to environmental and human health, as well as to delivery of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals.

## Slide 58

- Massive Range Shifts: Climate change and land use changes will cause about 3,139 mammal species to move their geographic ranges by 2070, especially into high elevations and biodiversity hotspots

- Increased Viral Sharing: These shifts will result in at least 4,000 new opportunities for cross-species viral transmission

- Human Risk: New hotspots of viral sharing are expected to overlap heavily with human population centers, especially in Asia and Africa, raising the risk of zoonotic (animal-to-human) disease spillover

- Role of Bats: Bats are projected to drive the majority of novel viral transmissions due to their high mobility and evolutionary tendencies

- No Easy Fix: Even limiting global warming to under 2°C will not significantly reduce the risk of viral sharing, because species' range expansions will still occur under moderate warming

- Call to Action: The authors stress the need for viral surveillance programs to be paired with biodiversity monitoring, particularly in tropical regions where most emerging diseases originate

- Climate change is already setting in motion ecological changes that will heighten the risk of pandemics, and urgent, global cooperation in monitoring wildlife and viruses is necessary to prevent future outbreaks
