## Slide 21

### Physical weathering

- mechanical breakdown of rocks by frost, water and wind

- Differential expansion and contraction during rapid heating and cooling

## Slide 22

### Chemical weathering

- Breakdown of primary minerals in the rock and formation of new secondary minerals

- Water = essential agent

- Reacts with minerals

- Transports dissolved species e.g. CO2 that can react

- Many different reactions including:

- Hydrolysis

- Hydration

- Carbonation

- Oxidation

- Ion exchange

- Chelation

## Slide 24

- Hydration

- Addition of water molecules to a mineral structure. Common in clay minerals, causes minerals to swell and fall apart

## Slide 25

- Carbonation

- Reaction with carbonic acid, which forms when CO2 dissolves in rainwater. Particularly reacts with carbonate minerals such as calcite and dolomite (limestones)

## Slide 26

- Oxidation

- Bonding of oxygen to metallic elements from the primary rocks.

- Firstly release of the mineral components e.g. by hydrolysis

- Fe2+ in solution oxidises from O2 in the water and atmosphere to form a solid mineral-Goethite (FeOOH)

## Slide 30

### Soil Classification

- Colour

- e.g. black = rich in organic matter,     red = rich in Fe oxides

- Texture (particle size)

- Sand: 2.0-0.05 mm

- Silt: 0.05-0.002 mm

## Slide 31

- Soil horizons

- O = horizons rich in organic matter

- A, B etc. = horizons of different mineral compositions

- R = bedrock

## Slide 32

### Clay minerals

- Clay minerals particularly affect soil chemistry

- Atoms are arranged in layers

- Water molecules and ions can become weakly bonded in the layers

- Can store nutrients in soils such as N, P and K via ion exchange

- Higher capacity for ion exchange can lead to a more fertile soil

- Muscovite

## Slide 77

### Study Method

- Assessed global urban water scarcity in 2016 and 2050 and the feasibility of potential solutions for water-scarce cities.

- Quantified spatial patterns of the global urban population for 2016 at a grid-cell resolution of 1 km2 by integrating spatial urban land-use and population data. We then identified water-scarce areas at the catchment scale by combining global water resource availability and demand data, and calculated the global urban population in water-scarce areas in 2016.

- Quantified the global urban population in water scarce areas for 2050 under four socioeconomic and climate change scenarios by combining modelled projections of global urban area, population, and water availability and demand.

- Evaluated the feasibility of seven major solutions for easing water scarcity for each affected city. We discuss the implications of the results for mitigating global urban water scarcity and improving the sustainability and livability of the world’s cities.
