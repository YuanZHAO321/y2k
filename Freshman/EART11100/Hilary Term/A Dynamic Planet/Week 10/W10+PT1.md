## Slide 4

- What’s the difference?

- Diagenesis is caused by simple burial by the addition of overburden in sedimentary basins.

- Metamorphism is caused by the addition of heat or mechanical energy not possibly by simple addition of overburden by burial in basins.

- Diagenesis can involve the addition of new materials (esp. cements), and a substantial change in the bulk composition of the rock.

- Metamorphism is largely a closed system, with no change in the bulk composition of rock.

- Sedimentary textures are still recognisable during and after diagenesis, but are not following metamorphism.

## Slide 7

- Compaction

- Mechanical compaction by overburden causes grain reorganisation/rotation in order to reduce volume

- rock volume is not reduced, but pore-space is reduced

- grains are better “packed”

- causes “dewatering” – expulsion of water that occupied the pore space.

- Grains typically reorganise such that the long-axis moves to become perpendicular to compaction/shortening direction.

- Expulsion of water.

## Slide 12

- Compaction

- Pressure solution is another manner in which compaction by overburden can reduce rock thickness.

- Material at grain contacts perpendicular to compaction / shortening dissolve

- The material re-precipitates as cement at grain contacts parallel to compaction / shortening.

- Net result is also porosity loss.

- Very common in carbonates for the ease at which those minerals will go into solution

- Requires greater depths of burial for clastic sedimentary rocks.

## Slide 15

- Dissolution

- Dissolution is caused by changes in T and P, and or the composition of pore-water, which results in the mineral being more stable in solution

- For example, pumping fresh (“meteoric”) water through many carbonates results in dissolution of calcite and aragonite: this can happen close to the surface

- But quartz will go into solution with greater difficulty (needs greater burial depth).

- Dissolution without reprecipitation occurs in systems which are subjected to prolonged pumping of exotic pore-water, with an abundant fresh supply

- Inhibits the saturation of the pore waters and re-precipitation of minerals

- Common in carbonates, less common in clastic sedimentary rocks

## Slide 17

- Cementation

- Cementation is the process by which chemical precipitates (new crystals) form in the pores of a sediment or rock and bind the grains together.

- Common cements are quartz, and various carbonates (calcite, aragonite, dolomite), and clays.

- Cementation reduces primary porosity by filling in the pore spaces between the grains.

## Slide 21

- Mineral replacement

- Mineral replacement occurs when a newly formed mineral replaces a preexisting one in situ.the new grain may have the same composition, but is a polymorph of it (e.g. replacement of biogenic Aragonite with Calcite)

- complete replacement on a mineral phase with another (e.g. Calcite to Dolomite)

- Changes in polymorph or ion exchange leading to the formation of new minerals, can lead to changes in volume, which can destroy or increase porosity

- E.g. transformation of calcite to dolomite leads to an 11% decrease in mineral volume (and equal increase in pore space)
