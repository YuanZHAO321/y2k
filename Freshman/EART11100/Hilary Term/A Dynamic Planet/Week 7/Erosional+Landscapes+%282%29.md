## Slide 5

- The difference between an erosional and depositional landscape

- Because erosional landscapes are dominated by UPLIFT, and WEATHERING AND EROSION, they

-  tend to have topographic relief, and

-  are dominated surfaces covered in weathering products (“regolith”), or

-  or dominated by bare eroding bedrock.

- By contrast, depositional landscapes are dominated by SUBSIDENCE and DEPOSITION of sediment. They are

-  low areas, either flooded by the lakes or sea, if they haven’t been filled with sediment, or

-  very flat (little relief) if they have been filled up with sediment.

-  characterised by bedrock buried under 10 to 100s m of sediment.

## Slide 11

- Controls on weathering rates

- Rates of weathering are climate controlled.

- Generally,

- Areas of high rainfall provide the water that is necessary for chemical weathering.

## Slide 14

- Controls on weathering rates

- Rates of weathering are slope controlled.

- Regolith growth is self-limiting.

- reduction in grainsize and formation of clays reduced permeability, and limits access of water to for chemical weathering.

- so weathering rate decreases downward in the regolith profile.

- However, if the regolith is readily removed, then water can access the reaction front.

- So rapid (or continuous) removal of regolith increases weathering rates.

## Slide 16

- Controls on weathering rates

- The concept of weathering-limited and transport-limited slopes.

- On weathering-limited slopes, transport processes are so efficient that debris is removed more quickly than it can be generated by further weathering.

- Transport-limited slopes occur where weathering processes are efficient at producing debris but where transport processes are inefficient at removing it from the slope

## Slide 17

- Controls on weathering rates

- And we can readily recognise weathering-limited and transport-limited slopes in our environment.

- Transport-limited slopes: thick regolith

- Weathering-limited slopes:

- Thin regolith

- Transport-limited slopes occur where weathering processes are efficient at producing debris but where transport processes are inefficient at removing it from the slope

- On weathering-limited slopes, transport processes are very efficient so debris is removed more quickly than it can be generated by further weathering.

## Slide 22

- Hillsope processes

- Erosion is the process of removal of the regolith that was generated through weathering, and its transport to a new location.

- There are two main mechanisms for erosion

- 1. Gravity moves material from uplifted areas (mountains, hinterlands) to low areas (sedimentary basins)

- The movement of material under its own gravitational potential in areas of steep slope.

- 2. Fluid flow exerts shear stress which moves material from one place to the next

- The flow of water in streams and rivers (fluvial processes), which entrains sediment (gravity driven)

- The flow of ice (glacial processes), which entrains sediment (gravity driven)

- The flow of air (wind, or aeolian processes) from areas of high to low  atmospheric pressure, which entrains sediment.

## Slide 24

- Hillsope processes

- Fall – detachment occurs with little or no shear, movement occurs through free-fall, bouncing or rolling.

- Common in glacial, canyon and sea-cliff geomorphic terranes with steep valley walls.

- The clasts and the piles thereof are called talus, and are a good indicator of the process of falling.

## Slide 25

- Slide – detachment and movement occurs along one or several  surfaces of shear (a scar and fault), with limited brittle internal deformation (fracturing or faulting).

## Slide 26

- Debris flows

- Debris flows form where water saturated soil, sediment or regolith, fails – usually via catastrophic (rather than creeping) slumping

- The kinetic energy imparted by the mass movement, causes grains to collide and bounce apart, allowing to flow freely between the grains, so that the material (even though it is composed of >50% grains) behaves as a fluid.

## Slide 37

- Fluvial erosion and geomorphology

- Through erosion, rivers incise the uplifted land to form valleys.

- Glacial erosion and geomorphology

- Typically, valley glaciers form in, and re-excavate major fluvial trunk river valleys.

- The Aletsch Glacier, Switzerland, is the longest glacier in the Alps.
