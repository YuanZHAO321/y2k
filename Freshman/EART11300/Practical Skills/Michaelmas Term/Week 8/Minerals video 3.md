## What is ‘cleavage’?

- Many minerals tend to break smoothly along particular planes. This is related to the internal atomic structure: it is a manifestation of weaker bond strength across these planes.

- Cleavage planes are always parallel to possible (or actual) crystal faces. Like crystal faces, they are related to the crystal’s internal symmetry.

## Cleavage

- Cleavage is more useful as a diagnostic property than crystal shape, since it is not affected by the external conditions which determine crystal shape (the only limitation here being the size of the grains).

- It is an extremely valuable diagnostic property in thin sections of minerals viewed in the optical microscope.

- Cleavage may be developed in 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 directions.

- Cleavage may be classified qualitatively as perfect,

- good, moderate or poor, and also in terms of the angular relationship between cleavage planes.

- Some minerals do not develop any cleavage.

- Notice how the cleavage lines on the drawing are shown to indicate that the cleavage occurs as cracks inside the crystal. You can also see these inside the crystal in the photo. Seeing sets of parallel cracks inside the crystal is very useful for telling you that you are looking at cleavage.

### Examples: 5. Quartz, SiO2

- **<u>Quartz</u>** has no cleavage. It has a “framework” structure and there are no planes across which the bonding is weak. The bonding is mainly covalent, which is inherently strong.

- Quartz fractures conchoidally, like glass. (“conchoidally” = with

- curved surfaces and concentric lines like growth rings on a shell)

- The absence of cleavage means you will never see fine parallel cracks in quartz crystals, such as you would expect to see in, e.g., barite and calcite.
