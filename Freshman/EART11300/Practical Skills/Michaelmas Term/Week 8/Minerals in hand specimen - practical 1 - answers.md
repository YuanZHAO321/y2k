# **Crystal habit**

The combination and relative development of crystal faces is called the crystal’s ‘habit’. It depends partly on crystal structure, but also on the way the crystal has grown, allowing some faces to develop preferentially. Terms used: equant, tabular, platy, prismatic (moderately elongate), acicular (needle-shaped), fibrous. Massive = compact mass of interlocking crystals with no clear division between individual grains.

The mineral samples that you have been given are all single crystals (occasionally there may be 2 or 3 interlocking crystals), but they are not **whole** crystals, as they have been obtained by smashing up larger crystals with a hammer, so unfortunately you are unlikely to see any crystal faces. But if the mineral contains **cleavage** , it is likely to break along cleavage planes when it is hit by the hammer (as these are planes of weakness in the crystal structure). So you will find that some or all of the surfaces of these mineral samples are cleavage planes. You can identify them as cleavage planes as they are flat surfaces that reflect the light well. Four of your samples have cleavage, though one isn’t as clear as the others. The other mineral has no cleavage and therefore breaks into irregular fragments like pieces of glass when hit with a hammer. In the following exercise, only look at the 4 minerals whose faces are cleavage planes, and treat them as if they are crystal faces. You will also look at some mineral specimens from the departmental collection which do have nice crystal faces.

# **Cleavage**

Many minerals tend to break smoothly along particular planes. This is related to the internal atomic structure: it is a manifestation of weaker bond strength across these planes. Cleavage planes are always parallel to possible (or actual) crystal faces. Like crystal faces, they are related to the crystal’s internal symmetry.

Cleavage is more useful as a diagnostic property than crystal shape, since it is not affected by the external conditions which determine crystal shape (the only limitation here being the size of the grains). It is an extremely valuable diagnostic property in thin sections of minerals viewed in the optical microscope. Cleavage may be developed in 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 directions. Cleavage may be classified qualitatively as perfect, good, moderate or poor, and also in terms of the angular relationship between cleavage planes. In the absence of cleavage, crystal breakage results in the development of fracture, which may be irregular, or in some cases, conchoidal (producing characteristically curved surfaces, as with broken glass).

Use the table and figure below to determine the type of cleavage that each of the minerals has. This is not easy for some of the display minerals, since most of the surfaces you can see are crystal faces, so you will need to look for planar cracks inside the crystals. Note also that the quality of cleavage (how ‘good’ it is) varies from mineral to mineral.  If cleavage is ‘perfect’, the mineral will easily split along the cleavage planes, but if it is ‘poor’, then it won’t split easily. In between ‘perfect’ and ‘poor’ are ‘good’ and ‘moderate’. These terms are only qualitative, so don't worry if you are not sure whether to say ‘good’ or ‘perfect’.

# **Colour**

The impression of colour in minerals results from the preferential absorption of certain wavelengths from white light, so that the complementary colour is seen. Although colour is the mineral property which makes the most immediate impact, its causes are complex, and it must be used as a diagnostic property with some care.

Some minerals are intrinsically coloured because of their chemical composition (this applies particularly to minerals containing essential Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Co and Cu) and they produce a characteristic colour (streak) when powdered. In practice this is generally achieved by drawing the mineral across an unglazed porcelain plate (only works if the mineral is softer than the plate, H < 6 ½).

Most other minerals are essentially colourless in powdered form (i.e. produce a white streak), but may occur as distinctly coloured crystals in the natural state due to impurities. Such minerals may be very variable in body colour depending on the amount and type of impurity in particular occurrences (e.g. fluorite, quartz, etc.). Colour may also be produced by defects in the crystal lattice. Some minerals develop an iridescent film or tarnish as the result of surface oxidation (e.g. chalcopyrite).

Others produce a ‘play of colours’ or schiller effect due to internal reflection from minute platy inclusions or other discontinuities such as twinning. This is seen in some varieties of feldspar, and also in opal (which consists of minute spheres of amorphous SiO2), when it is termed opalescence.

## You can now finish identifying your minerals:

You will have found that 2 of your minerals have the same habit and cleavage, but have very different colours. One is black and the other is silvery. If you peeled away a thin layer of the black one it would either still look black, or be brown and transparent, depending on how thin a layer you can peel (try it…), but if you do the same with the silvery one it will be colourless (and therefore transparent). The black one is black because it contains **iron (Fe**<sup>**2+**</sup> **)** ; the colourless one does not contain iron. These are both minerals in the **mica** group. **Biotite** has the composition **K (Mg,Fe)3 [Al Si3] O10 (OH)2** and **muscovite** has the composition **KAl2 [Al Si3] O10 (OH)2** . The amount of Fe in biotite is variable; the more Fe, the darker the colour. Muscovite can contain impurities which give it a hint of colour.

The other minerals are all transparent or translucent (but small pieces would be transparent) in shades of white, pale grey and orangey-pink (powdered samples would be colourless). The mineral without cleavage is **quartz** , which has the composition **SiO2** . Pure quartz is completely colourless and transparent, like glass. Small amounts of impurities are possible in quartz, but not common. “Milky” quartz is common, the white colour caused by tiny fluid inclusions.

**Calcite CaCO3** is also colourless when pure, and white when it contains fluid inclusions. Any pink colouration in your crystal is from iron: Fe<sup>2+</sup> can substitute for Ca<sup>2+</sup> in the crystal structure, and may later oxidise to Fe<sup>3+</sup> to give a rusty colour.

**Feldspar** (variable composition; the end-member closest to your sample is **orthoclase KAlSi3O8** ) has a pinkish colour, due to iron impurities. You’ll see that the colour is not uniform, and looks more pink adjacent to little fractures running through the crystal. This indicates variable amounts of oxidation of the Fe<sup>2+</sup> impurity to Fe<sup>3+</sup> .

Observe the beautiful play of colours (known as schiller) shown by the large polished crystal of labradorite feldspar on display. # **Hardness**

Defined as resistance to scratching. Hardness depends on bond density, and can vary with direction in a mineral because there are different numbers and strengths of bonds in different directions. It is usually measured by reference to a set of standard minerals – Mohs’ Scale of Hardness (1812). This is a qualitative test: each mineral is capable of scratching the minerals with lower hardness.
