# **A. Attributes of the three categories of rock in hand specimen or thin section:**

# **Igneous Rocks**

## **These rocks have crystallised from partially molten material – magma – either within the Earth or on Earth’s surface.**

When you are looking at a hand specimen or thin section:

- Igneous rocks tend to have interlocking crystals

- Some crystals have their own shape (i.e. are bounded by flat crystal faces) indicating unhindered growth from a liquid.

- Contain relatively few minerals (normally 5 different mineral  species or less).

- Crystals of each mineral tend to be quite uniform.

- May contain fossil gas bubbles (= vesicles).  Not otherwise porous.

- Crystals don’t usually have a preferred orientation (but there are exceptions, e.g. cumulates and rocks with flow-aligned crystals)

- Do not contain organic matter or fossils.

# **Sedimentary rocks**

**These rocks have formed by the accumulation, as layered deposits on the Earth’s surface, of material derived by weathering of pre-existing rocks or material of biological origin.**

When you are looking at a hand specimen or thin section:

- Commonly consist of particles (clasts) of pre-existing rocks, which may include polycrystalline rock fragments.

- Clasts may be rounded, indicating abrasion during sediment transport.

- May contain fossils.

- May contain organic matter.

- May be quite porous.

- Commonly layered (bedded), often with dramatic compositional contrasts. (May only see this when looking at a large scale, e.g. on maps or in the field.)

# **Metamorphic rocks**

## **These rocks have formed by alteration of pre-existing igneous or sedimentary rocks, in the** **<u>solid state, through the action of heat and/or pressure.</u>**

When you are looking at a hand specimen or thin section:

- Compositional layering (fossil bedding) may be present, but may be deformed (folded, etc).

- Contain relatively few minerals (normally 6 or less); crystals of each mineral tend to be quite uniform.

- Crystals often have a preferred orientation (tectonic fabric) produced by deformation. If the fabric is planar (foliation), it may be discordant to any layering present.

- Some crystals have their own shape; others have shapes indicative of competitive growth in the solid state (analogy:  bubbles in foam).

- No significant porosity.

- No fossils usually survive.

# **C.  Further classification of the different rock types:**

## **Igneous Rocks.**

A simple classification of igneous rocks is based upon the grain size, which tells us how quickly the rock has cooled:

- Medium- to coarse-grained = slow cooling = **plutonic**

- Fine-grained = fast cooling = **volcanic**

Note that if the rock contains larger crystals in a finer-grained “groundmass” (= the rock matrix), then this indicates a 2-stage cooling process, with the larger crystals growing slowly in a magma chamger, and the groundmass crystallising in the eruption.

## **Sedimentary Rocks.**

If the rock is made of particles, then it can be classified according to grain size, which indicates the distance travelled from source to deposition:

- **Proximal** = close to source = coarser grain size.

- **Distal** = far away from source = finer grain size.

The grain size reflects the amount of energy available for transportation. This classification does not work so well for rocks containing fossils whose size is determined by the kind of fossil rather than distance travelled.

## **Metamorphic Rocks.**

Can be classified according to the extent that the rock has changed during metamorphism, which usually depends on the temperature reached, and is known as **metamorphic grade.**

- **Low grade** display the least amount of change. If the rock started out as fine-grained (e.g. a mudstone), then low-grade metamorphism will result in a rock that is still fine-grained.

- **Medium grade** show an intermediate level of change. It will be coarser grained.

- **High grade** show the most change. It will have the largest crystals.
