## Slide 3

### AIR POLLUTION: AN overview

- London’s Great Smog of 1952:

- Coal burning (domestic/industrial heating) anticyclonic conditions

- Thousands of deaths

- Anti-pollution legislation - “Clean Air Act” (1956)

**Notes:**

The London Smog, also known as the Great Smog of 1952, was a severe air pollution event that affected London mostly from December 5 to December 9, 1952. Due to a combination of cold weather, windless conditions, and the burning of large amounts of coal, a dense layer of smoke and fog formed hanging over the city.
Cause: The smog was primarily a result of burning low-quality coal in homes and power stations under windless conditions. The pollutants from the coal burning, including particulate matter, soot and sulphur dioxide mixed with water vapor in the atmosphere, creating a thick, toxic fog.
Impacts: The smog severely reduced visibility and penetrated homes, affecting respiratory health. It is estimated to have caused between 4,000 and 12,000 deaths due to respiratory illnesses like bronchitis and pneumonia.
The smog led to major disruptions in transportation, halting railways, air traffic, and even ambulances. It also harmed livestock and wildlife.
The London Smog highlighted the dangers of air pollution, leading to increased public awareness and eventually prompting the Clean Air Act of 1956 in the UK. This act regulated the burning of coal and led to a reduction in air pollution through the introduction of "smoke control areas" and restrictions on the use of coal in urban areas.

The event is often cited as a pivotal moment in environmental and public health policy, showing the deadly effects of air pollution.

## Slide 7

### A Costly Error: NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter(1999)

- In 1999, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because of a unit conversion error. One team used the metric system (newton-seconds), while another used the imperial system (pound-seconds) to calculate the spacecraft's trajectory.

- This led to the spacecraft entering Mars' atmosphere too low, causing it to burn up. The mission, which cost $125 million, was lost.

- Even minor errors, if not caught, can have major impacts, underscoring the importance of attention to detail in all forms of data collection.

**Notes:**

## Slide 8

- Systematic errors

- Reproducible errors which affect all your measurements in the same way

- Typical causes:

- Observational error

- Imperfect instrument calibration

- Environmental interference

**Notes:**

Systematic errors are consistent, repeatable inaccuracies caused by flaws in measurement devices or methods.
Examples:
Instrument Calibration: Incorrectly calibrated sensors can give consistently biased readings.
Sensor Drift: Over time, air quality sensors may drift and need regular recalibration.

Long-term biases in data leading to incorrect conclusions about air quality trends.

## Slide 11

- RANDOM errors

- Cause one measurement to differ slightly from the next

- Caused by unpredictable changes during an experiment:

- Instrumental noise

- Real variations in what is being measured

**Notes:**

Random errors are unpredictable variations in measurement, often due to environmental factors or device limitations.
Examples:
Environmental Noise: Sudden temperature changes or humidity spikes can affect sensor readings.
Power Supply Fluctuations: Voltage fluctuations affecting the sensor’s performance.

Random errors increase uncertainty but can often be minimized through multiple measurements or statistical methods.

## Slide 13

### Gaussian nature of RanDOM ERRORS

- Random errors are just as likely to move the measured value in either direction (positive or negative with respect to the mean)

- They have what we call a normal Gaussian distribution - characterised by a mean and a standard deviation:

- Mean = “central” or average value	    Standard deviation = spread of data points around the mean

- Normal distributions have an equal number of measurements above and below the mean value

**Notes:**

Law of large numbers: Increased number of samples will reduce the uncertainty in estimating mean (average) or the most likely out come of the population.

## Slide 15

- How to deal with

- RANDOM ERRORS

- Random error ALWAYS occur

- They can’t be predicted (but they can be measured)

- To measure the random error, we can take many data points (typically >100) and average them.

- This will quantify random error and will help you to estimate the true mean value.
