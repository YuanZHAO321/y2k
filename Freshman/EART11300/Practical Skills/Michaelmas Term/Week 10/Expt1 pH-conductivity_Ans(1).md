# **The effect of the concentration of a basic or acidic solution on its conductivity and pH**

## **Intended Learning Outcomes**

## **Introduction**

In this experiment hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solutions will be diluted. The aim of this experiment is to determine what effect diluting these solutions has on their conductivities and their pH's. The first thing we have to do is to make a current detector.

## **A simple LED circuit**

The current detector consists of a simple circuit used to drive an LED (light emitting diode). The power supply for the circuit will be a 9V battery and the components will include wires connected to a snap-on battery cap, an LED and a resistor.

Why do we need a resistor? The 9V battery typically produces a current of up to a few hundred milliamps (mA), whereas our LED bulb has a maximum rating of 20 mA. If we connected it directly to the battery it may work for a few seconds/minutes but it would become hot and eventually may burn-out (don’t try this).

We use a resistor to control the current delivered to the LED. Although the LED has a maximum rating of 20 mA anything between a few to 10 mA provides a perfectly adequate light.

We need to know what resistor to use in our circuit. We can use Ohm’s Law to calculate the lowest gain resistor to limit the current to 20 mA.

Voltage of the battery, Vb = 9V

Voltage drop across the LED (obtained from the technical specification sheet) Vf = 4V I = maximum current of the LED (20mA or 0.02 A).

Use this form of Ohm’s Law to find the resistance.

Resistance (ohms) = (Vb – Vf)/I = …250….. ohms

We can use a higher gain resistor than the value calculated and it will still provide enough current for the LED (remember it only needs a few mA).

Resistors are colour coded, usually with 4-6 colour bands. The trick is to know which direction to read them, usually there is a notable wider gap between the final two bands, although it is not always easy to spot on a 6-band resistor.

## **Experimental Procedure**

## **Apparatus**

2 x 10 ml beakers 1 x 10 ml syringe

1 x current indicator with connections

## **Chemicals**

Sodium hydroxide NaOH(aq) (0.10 M) Hydrochloric acid HCl(aq) (0.10 M) Universal indicator test paper (for pH) De-ionised water

## **Procedure**

1. Draw up a few millilitres (ml) of **water** into your syringe. Then using a beaker to collect the drops, carefully depress the syringe counting the number of drops equivalent to a volume of 0.5 ml. Repeat 3 times to check for repeatability. Calculate the volume per drop as follows:

   - 0.5/No.drops  = …0.04……..ml/drop (based on my average of 12.5 drops = 0.5 ml, this may vary a little)

2. Use the syringe to add 0.1 ml of sodium hydroxide (0.10 M) into Beaker 1.

3. Rinse the syringe with water to clean it.

4. Add 1.9 ml of water into Beaker 1.

5. Swirl the solution in Beaker 1 to mix the contents.

6. Use the syringe to take-up 0.1 ml of the solution in Beaker 1 with the syringe and place this into Beaker 2.

7. Rinse the syringe with water to clean it. Dispense 1.9 ml of water into Beaker 2.

8. Swirl the solution in Beaker 2 to mix the contents.

9. Insert the electrodes of the current meter into the solution in Beaker 1. Observe what happens to the light emitting diode (LED). Record your result in the Table below.

10. Wipe the electrodes clean and then test the conductivity of the solution in beaker 2 in the same way. Record your result in the Table below.

11. Test the pH in beakers 1 and 2 using the universal indicator paper strips. Record your result in the Table below.

12. Rinse and dry the beakers and syringe and repeat steps 1 to 10 as before, using hydrochloric acid (0.10 M) instead of sodium hydroxide.

3. What causes the current indicator to glow, write the dissolution reactions? HCl (aq) = H<sup>+</sup> (aq) + Cl<sup>-</sup> (aq)

NaOH (aq) = Na<sup>+</sup> (aq) + OH<sup>-</sup> (aq) In wires, it is electrons that carry the current, but in solutions the current is carried by ions. NaOH and HCl strongly dissociate into ions in solution.

4. What is the effect of the concentration of a basic or acidic solution on its conductivity and pH? The electrical conductance depends upon the total concentration of the ions (and hence pH). The most mobile ions are H<sup>+</sup> and OH<sup>-</sup> . So the more acidic or basic the solution the greater the electrical conductivity will be.
