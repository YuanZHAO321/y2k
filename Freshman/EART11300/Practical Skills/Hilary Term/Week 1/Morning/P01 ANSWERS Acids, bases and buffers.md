### Background: recap from EART11200 Chemistry of the Earth Topic 9

An acid is a substance that donates hydrogen ions (H+) when dissolved in water (aqueous solution).

The pH of a solution is related to the concentration of hydrogen ions:

pH = -log10[H+(aq)]		([X] means “concentration of substance X”)

Strong acids, such as HCl, H2SO4 and HNO3, are almost completely dissociated in solution. The dissociation equations can therefore be shown with a reaction arrow to indicate that the process of dissociation essentially goes to completion:

HCl(aq)  H+ (aq) + Cl- (aq)

H2SO4 (aq)  2H+ (aq) + SO42- (aq)

Weak acids are only partially dissociated in solution, so the dissociation process is indicated using a reversible reaction arrow. For a general acid which has the formula HA the process is:

HA ⇋ H+ (aq) + A- (aq)

Example 1: ethanoic / acetic acid (approximately 0.4 % of acid molecules dissociate)

Formula:     CH3COOH (aq) ⇋ H+ (aq) + CH3COO- (aq)

Example 2: carbonic acid (a diprotic acid)

H2CO3 (aq) ⇋ H+ (aq) + HCO3- (aq)

HCO3-  (aq) ⇋ H+ (aq) + CO32- (aq)

#### Part 2: Plotting a titration curve

Note: results tables are provided at the back of these worksheets

- Carefully transfer the burette stand and burette to the floor, place a funnel in the top of the burette.

- Place an empty beaker under the burette and with the burette tap open rinse the burette first with deionised water and then with 0.100 M NaOH solution. Discard the rinsings into the sink.

- Close the burette tap, carefully fill the burette past the top reading mark with 0.100 M NaOH and drain again to ensure no air bubbles remain in the tap. Refill/drain burette so the bottom of the meniscus is on 0.00 cm3.

- Remove the funnel and carefully place the burette stand back on the lab bench.

- Use a 25 cm3 pipette (and filler!) to transfer two portions of 25.0 cm3 of 0.050 M acetic acid to a clean 250 cm3 beaker (so that you have 50.0 cm3 acetic acid in total).

- Place the beaker of acetic acid on the magnetic stirring apparatus and beneath the burette. Add the magnetic stirring bar to the beaker. Start the stirring apparatus on the lowest setting for gentle mixing of solutions and to minimise risk of damage to the pH probe.

- Add 2-3 drops phenolphthalein indicator to the acetic acid in the beaker (colour change at end point: colourless  pink so the indicator will be initially colourless).

- Place the probe into the solution of acetic acid ensuring that the bulb is fully submerged and avoiding the stirring bar. Record the pH once the measurement is stable in the results table.

- Slowly open the tap to add 2.0 cm3 of NaOH from the burette to the beaker then close the tap, record the pH.

- Continue adding 2.0 cm3 portions of NaOH and record the pH next to the total volume of NaOH added until the NaOH is in excess (identified by stable high pH value probably around pH=12). Note carefully which addition of NaOH coincides with the end point where the pH changes rapidly and the phenolphthalein undergoes the colour change from colourless to pink.

- Repeat the titration but just before the onset of the rapid pH change at the end point (which could be anticipated by checking the results table of the previous titration), add 0.20 cm3 portions of NaOH until the rapid pH change is complete then continue to add 2.0 cm3 portions until the NaOH is in excess (stable high pH achieved).

### Further discussion points

Around the half neutralisation point the pH remains roughly constant (increases very slightly) over a range of added volumes. This is the ‘buffering’ zone where the mixture of the weak acid (HA) and it’s conjugate base anion (A-) are present in almost equal amounts and so the system behaves as a buffer. This can be explained by considering the equilibrium reaction for the weak acid dissociation when both the concentration of HA and A- are relatively high:

A general weak acid dissociation: HA ⇋ H+ (aq) + A- (aq)

Acetic acid dissociation: CH3COOH (aq) ⇋ H+ (aq) + CH3COO- (aq)

When a small volume of acid (H+) is added to this system at pH close to the half-equivalence point, what happens to the position of equilibrium?

Adding H+ to the system should decrease pH

The system CH3COOH (aq) ⇋ H+ (aq) + CH3COO- (aq) behaves as a buffer because:

As H+ is added the concentration of H+ increases and so the position of equilibrium shifts to the left as H+ ions reacts with conjugate base anions CH3COO-

H+ (aq) + CH3COO- (aq)  CH3COOH (aq) i.e. the reverse of dissociation

This process removes H+ so the decrease in pH is resisted.

When a small volume of base (in this case OH-) is added, what happens to the position of equilibrium?

Adding OH- to the system should increase pH

The system CH3COOH (aq) ⇋ H+ (aq) + CH3COO- (aq) behaves as a buffer because:

The OH- added reacts with H+ on the right-hand side of the equation (OH- + H+  H2O). As H+ is removed by the reaction with hydroxide, the position of equilibrium shifts to the right and the weak acid molecules CH3COOH dissociate, releasing H+ and resisting the increase in pH.
