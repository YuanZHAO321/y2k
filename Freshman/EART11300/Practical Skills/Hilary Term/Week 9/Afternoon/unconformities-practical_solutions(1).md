# **Week 8 – Unconformities**

# **<u>Introduction</u>**

In this class, we are going to focus on unconformities. As Bennison (2003) writes, “ _<u>an unconformity represents a period of time during which strata are not laid down</u>_ ”. In other words, an unconformity is a surface, or contact, between some overlying sediments and underlying older sequences of rocks, where there is evidence for a gap in time, which we call a ‘hiatus’.

In simple cases, such a gap in time may be marked by an absence of deposition. More often, underlying rocks are uplifted, tilted, folded or eroded to varying degrees before deposition resumes. If the hiatus is particularly long, underlying beds may experience intrusion by igneous bodies such as dikes, or even undergo metamorphism. The ultimate result of any hiatus, usually best observed in the field, is that overlying strata rest on the eroded surface of an older set of beds (Bennison, 2003).

Unconformities are essential to understanding an area’s full geological history, because the absence of rocks can be just as informative as the rocks which are present. The overall aims of today’s class are to be able to:

- Define unconformities and categorize them according to their geometry, and relationships to units above and below;

- To identify unconformities in stratigraphic columns on published maps;

- To identify unconformities in map view, and draw simple cross sections and stratigraphic columns displaying these unconformities;

- [time permitting] To make sketch summaries of large areas of geological maps, highlighting such features as major faults, folds and unconformities

# **<u>Categorizing Unconformities</u>**

Unconformities are categorized on the basis of:

(a) the shape of the unconformity surface and the relative orientation of the units on either side of it, or

(b) the relative orientation between the units above or below the unconformity surface and the unconformity surface itself.

Today we will focus mostly on (a). Those of you who have chosen to pursue Earth and Planetary sciences pathways next year may spend more time thinking about (b).

Before proceeding further, we need to define some more terms. An **<u>angular unconformity</u>** is one where the younger sediments lie upon the eroded surface of tilted or folded older rocks (Figure 1). The implication is that the tilting or folding of the older rocks occurred in the time-break represented by the unconformity. It is important to recognise that the dip and strike of the beds above the unconformity surface would be different from the dip and strike of the underlying beds.

In contrast, a **<u>disconformity</u>** is one where the overlying younger beds and underlying older beds have the same dip and strike (or dip direction). Broadly speaking, this means that there may have been

erosion of the older beds during the hiatus but that there was not significant tilting or folding. A special type of disconformity is a **<u>paraconformity</u>** <u>, where the gap in</u> strata is not very apparent, e.g. it may only be determined by dating of the rocks with fossils or isotopic methods. Disconformities and paraconformities could be together termed ‘parallel unconformities’, in contrast to more common angular unconformities.

Finally, a **<u>nonconformity</u>** describes where sediments are deposited onto an eroded surface of older igneous or metamorphic basement.

# **<u>Summarising Geological Maps</u>**

The amount of data that are presented on geological maps can be very confusing at first glance. Sometimes we need to simplify what is presented to just show the main features. The drawing of cross sections is a very important part of interpreting the geology but another way is to draw a simplified sketch map.

The sketch map should show what you think are the important features but it does not contain the detail and complexity of the real map. You could use a sketch map to highlight particular features that were of interest to you e.g. where the unconformities or the positions of faults and folds are.

The last page of the handout lists some characteristics of both good and bad sketch maps. You should refer to these but note that today’s sketch map is your first attempt, and you should simply try to do your best at summarising the geology, and not getting the map perfect.

# **A good sketch map will show:**

The main grid lines, a scale and a key along with a descriptive title explaining what the sketch map has been drawn to show. It will always be neat and easy to read.

It should use the same colours and letter keys as the original map

The outlines drawn accurately of the outcrop shapes of the important formations on the map. It may be useful to group formations together into larger units to keep the simplicity.

Some of the topographic features e.g. major river valleys and spot heights.

The main structural features such as the trend of major faults and the axial traces of folds

The strike and dip of formations if feasible

Annotations to explain features and complexities e.g. region of many parallel faults.

# **A bad sketch map:**

Will have forgotten to number the grid lines, it will have no scale and no title to tell the viewer what the sketch map refers to.

It will not have a key and the colours and letter keys will show no relationship to the original map.

It will just be a detailed copy of the original map instead of a simplification or synthesis of the original.

It will just have copied the original formations and not grouped them into larger units.

A bad sketch map will not highlight the important features

It will give no indication of topography or strike and dip

It will have no annotations

It will be messy and hard to read.
