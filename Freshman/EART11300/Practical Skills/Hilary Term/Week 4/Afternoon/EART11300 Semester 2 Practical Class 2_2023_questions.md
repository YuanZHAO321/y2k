## **Sandstone (R25 or SK)**

This is a sedimentary rock. It contains 2 minerals, one with low interference colour, one with high interference colour. Look at the low interference colour mineral. Does it contain any cleavage? This mineral is **quartz** . Notice how the crystals are all very clear – there is no alteration. This is a useful property of quartz. Other properties of quartz are: low relief, no twinning or zoning (which you will see in some low-interference colour minerals in other thin sections).

The other mineral, with high interference colour, is **calcite** . The sandy interference colour, with green and pink tinges, is a typical high interference colour. Calcite (and its fellow carbonate mineral, dolomite) is the only common mineral you will see with high interference colour. Notice how the calcite is filling in the gaps around the quartz crystals, forming a cement. Sketch a few quartz grains and annotate your sketch to show the calcite cement.

<mark>Now look at the evidence that this is a sedimentary rock, using the possible attributes of sedimentary rocks:</mark>

- Does it consist of particles (clasts) of pre-existing rocks? Yes, many of the quartz grains have a very irregular shape, indicating that they are not fresh crystals but have been abraded from a pre-existing rock.

- Are the clasts rounded? Some of them.

- Does it contain fossils? No.

- Does it contain organic matter? No.

- Does it contain any porosity? No (the calcite cement holding the quartz grains together has filled any earlier porosity).

- Is it layered? Not that you can see on the thin section scale.

**<mark>TWINNING.</mark>** <mark>Some minerals tend to develop crystals composed of two or more distinct parts, chemically identical, but of different orientation. Some twinned crystals consist of two parts</mark> **<mark>(simple twinning),</mark>** <mark>while others comprise many parallel 'twin' units within a single crystal</mark> **<mark>(multiple twinning).</mark>** <mark>In thin section, twinning is most effectively seen under crossed polars, because the individual units tend to display different interference colours and/or extinction positions, and yet can be clearly seen to belong to a single crystal in terms of the other optical properties. The boundaries between the units are typically plane surfaces, and appear as straight lines in thin section, provided that they are approximately perpendicular to the surface of the section.</mark>

# **Attributes of the three categories of rock in hand specimen or thin section:**

# **Igneous Rocks**

**These rocks have crystallised from partially molten material – magma – either within the Earth or on Earth’s surface.**

When you are looking at a hand specimen or thin section:

- 1.Igneous rocks tend to have interlocking crystals

- 2.Some crystals have their own shape (i.e. are bounded by flat crystal faces) indicating unhindered growth from a liquid.

- 3.Contain relatively few minerals (normally 5 different mineral species or less).

- 4.Crystals of each mineral tend to be quite uniform.

- 5.May contain fossil gas bubbles (= vesicles).  Not otherwise porous.

- 6.Crystals don’t usually have a preferred orientation (but there are exceptions, e.g. cumulates and rocks with flow-aligned crystals)

- 7.Do not contain organic matter or fossils.

# **Sedimentary rocks**

**These rocks have formed by the accumulation, as layered deposits on the Earth’s surface, of material derived by weathering of pre-existing rocks or material of biological origin.**

When you are looking at a hand specimen or thin section:

- 1.Commonly consist of particles (clasts) of pre-existing rocks, which may include polycrystalline rock fragments.

- 2.Clasts may be rounded, indicating abrasion during sediment transport.

- 3.May contain fossils.

- 4.May contain organic matter.

- 5.May be quite porous.

- 6.Commonly layered (bedded), often with dramatic compositional contrasts. (May only see this when looking at a large scale, e.g. on maps or in the field.)

# **Metamorphic rocks**

## **These rocks have formed by alteration of pre-existing igneous or sedimentary rocks, in the** **<u>solid state, through the action of heat and/or pressure.</u>**

When you are looking at a hand specimen or thin section:

- 1.Compositional layering (fossil bedding) may be present, but may be deformed (folded, etc).

- 2.Contain relatively few minerals (normally 6 or less); crystals of each mineral tend to be quite uniform.

- 3.Crystals often have a preferred orientation (tectonic fabric) produced by deformation. If the fabric is planar (foliation), it may be discordant to any layering present.

- 4.Some crystals have their own shape; others have shapes indicative of competitive growth in the solid state (analogy:  bubbles in foam).

- 5.No significant porosity.

- 6.No fossils usually survive.
