# **Geological Maps – 1**

## **Orientation of a plane: dip and strike**

Beds (strata) originate as approximately horizonal layers that may subsequently become folded during tectonic events leading to the formation of mountain belts. Beds are geometrically considered as planes, and geologists record planar orientations by recording two pieces of information.

- _(1) Strike: is the direction of a horizontal line on a plane._

- _(2) Dip: is the maximum angle of slope of the plane and is always perpendicular to strike._

_Figure 1 Explanation of the strike and dip measurements of geological surfaces._

Fig. 1 illustrates the strike and dip on an inclined plane and shows the strike/dip symbol that is often used on geological maps to indicate the orientation of strata.

Strike and dip readings are made in the field and are recorded using different formats, the one we will use is: **strike** / **dip** and **dip direction** . In the example shown in Fig 1 the information would be recorded as 180/60 E.

## **Layered rocks and Topography**

Most Earth scientists us geological maps where the geological information is superimposed on the topographic relief. It is useful to know the elevation of the land surface because strata will outcrop at different elevations depending on the dip of their contacts. Figs 2 and 3 illustrates the principles, showing how topographic relief is important in controlling the shape of outcrop pattern on the map. The block diagrams in Fig. 2, show a stream in a valley (note the topographic contours V upstream) and a geological layer (stratum) outcropping on the cross-sections of the block.

- (a) Horizontal stratum - the outcrop perfectly follows the topographic contours.

- (b) Vertical stratum – shows no sympathy with topography and cuts across the hillside

- (c) Dipping stratum into the hill shows an outcrop that V’s upstream and cuts across the topographic contours.

## **A simple construction method for mapping geological contacts of strata**

How does a geologist, using information from a few scattered exposures, determine the direction of bedding contacts across hillsides and valleys? Often a reliable geological map needs to be made of an area where perhaps only a few % of the land surface is exposed bedrock.

If we assume in the simplest case of beds being represented by plane-dipping layers then we can use a method sometimes known as a **three-point problem** to extend bedding contacts to complete a map. This relies on having three locations in an area where the same bedding contact is exposed and involves the construction of **stratum contours** .

Stratum contours are the points of intersection of a geological plane and topographic contours. For a uniformly dipping plane, stratum contours are straight, parallel lines, equally space corresponding to higher and lower elevations of a plane (Fig. 5).

_Figure 5 A plane-dipping layer extending below the land surface at an angle given by the dip. Stratum contours are marked on the plane for two different depths below the surface._
