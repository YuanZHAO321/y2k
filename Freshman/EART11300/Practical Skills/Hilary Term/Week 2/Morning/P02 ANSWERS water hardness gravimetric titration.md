### Background

Accurate quantitative analysis of the amounts (i.e. concentrations/masses) of species in solution can be carried out using a volumetric technique (titration). With a growing emphasis on sustainability, methods have been devised to carry out these procedures in microscale to conserve resources although these techniques may result in a larger error in our measurements. The

hardness of drinking water supply can be measured by titrating a water sample with 0.01M **e** thylene **d** iamine **t** etraacetic **a** cid ( **EDTA** ) solution at pH 10. Results can be assessed against national frameworks to identify potential impacts on local populations.

## **Aim** : To measure and assess the total water hardness (Ca<sup>2+</sup> and Mg<sup>2+</sup> ) in samples of water from different localities

#### **Theory (the chemistry!):**

#### What is water hardness?

Water hardness is caused by the presence Ca<sup>2+</sup> and Mg<sup>2+</sup> ions in water supply. As water from precipitation percolates through deposits that contain a high proportion of calcium or magnesiumrich minerals, divalent metal cations (Ca<sup>2+</sup> and Mg<sup>2+</sup> ) may dissolve in the water. Local geology therefore affects water hardness. Criteria for assessing water hardness are not universal but frameworks often refer to the concentration of CaCO3 or Ca<sup>2+</sup> measured in milligrams per litre of water (ppm). United Utilities<sup>**1**</sup> and The Drinking Water Inspectorate<sup>**2**</sup> for the UK define degrees of water hardness as follows:

|**mg CaCO3/**l|**mg Ca**<sup>**2+**</sup>**/**l|**Classification**|
|---|---|---|
|0-50|0-20|Soft|
|51-100|21-40|Moderately soft|
|101-150|41-60|Slightly hard|
|151-200|61-80|Moderately hard|
|201-300|81-120|Hard|
|>300|>120|Very Hard|

When hard water is heated, thermal decomposition of bicarbonates can lead to deposition of solid calcium and magnesium compounds (scale) which may block pipes or affect the performance of water heating appliances. Conversely, soft water corrodes metal pipework at a faster rate than hard water<sup>**1**</sup> . Consuming hard water may have benefits for human health<sup>**3**</sup> but currently no guideline values are proposed for drinking water by the World Health Organization (WHO)<sup>**4**</sup> . Levels found in drinking water are not considered a health concern but water with [Ca<sup>2+</sup> ] > 100 mg/l can have a strong taste which might affect acceptability<sup>**4**</sup> .

#### The gravimetric method

In this procedure water samples are tested using a dilute solution (0.01M) of EDTA. We can therefore assume that the solution and samples have the same density as pure water (1 gcm<sup>-3</sup> ). Using a fine-tip Pasteur pipette to deliver small volumes of our standard solution (EDTA), we can measure the total volume transferred during the titration by recording mass changes i.e. the mass of EDTA solution required in grams is numerically equivalent to the volume transferred in cm<sup>3</sup> since the density of a dilute solution is assumed to be the same as pure water.

#### The titration complexation reaction

The structure of the EDTA molecule is shown below. At pH = 10 the EDTA molecule is in its fully deprotonated form (i.e. it has donated all its acidic hydrogen ions to the aqueous solution).

Fully deprotonated EDTA can bind to metal cations to form water-soluble complexes.

EDTA is a **hex** adentate ligand which means it has **six** lone pairs of electrons which can be <u>donated to metal cations. The metal cation (</u> **M** = Ca<sup>2+</sup> or Mg<sup>2+</sup> in this case) is enclosed within EDTA-complex and the process of complex formation releases smaller monodentate ligands (i.e. the 6 x water molecules) into the solution. This reaction is driven by the large increase in entropy as the number of particles in the system is increased when proceeding from the reactant side (2 particles) to the product side (7 particles). This phenomenon is known as the **chelate effect** (from Greek chelos = ‘crab’ because of the way in which the polydentate ligand figuratively wraps claws around the metal cation).

**Note the reaction ratio between EDTA and the dissolved metal ion M (** Ca<sup>2+</sup> or Mg<sup>2+</sup> **) is 1:1** .

#### The Indicator Reaction

Indicators are added to show when the end point of the reaction has been reached. Eriochrome Black T appears **red-purple** in the presence of Mg<sup>2+</sup> but will turn **blue** once the EDTA in the standard solution has formed complex ions with all the calcium and magnesium ions in the water sample.

### Procedure

#### **Record all masses to 2 decimal places in the results table below** .

1. Place a 10.5 ml glass sample vial onto the balance and record the mass of the empty vial in column 1 of the results table.

2. Select one of the waters from the stock samples to investigate and decant a small portion (between 2 – 5 ml) into a small beaker. With the sample vial still on the balance, use a dropping pipette to add ~2.5 g of the water to be tested into the vial and record the mass in column 2 then work out the mass of water added in column 6.

3. Use a dropping pipette to add ~0.2 g pH 10 standard buffer solution to the water sample.

4. Add two drops of Eriochrome Black T indicator from the dropping bottle provided to the water sample and swirl the vial carefully to mix the solutions.

5. Record the total mass of your water sample mixture in column 3 of the results table.

6. Remove the vial from the balance and place it over a piece of white paper – this enables the colour change at the end point to be identified easily.

-

- 7. Draw 0.01M EDTA solution into a fine <u>tip Pasteur pipette.</u>

8. Add one drop of EDTA solution to the vial and swirl carefully to mix. Observe the colour of the indicator.

9. Continue adding drops of EDTA and swirling the mixture until the end point of the experiment identified when the indicator changes from **red-purple to blue** .

10. At the end point, record the new total mass of your mixture in column 4 and use this measurement to calculate the mass of EDTA added in column 5.

11. Repeat the experiment (recording results in a new row of the results table) to check for concordant amounts of EDTA added. Ideally, three concordant results should be obtained and an average calculated. If time is an issue, you could share results with your peers to confirm repeatability.

12. Repeat this process for additional water samples and record your results in the table.

### Uncertainties in the measurement using the microscale technique

1. The mass balance measures mass to 2 decimal places, so the absolute uncertainty of the instrument is ±0.005g. A difference of two masses is used to find the mass of EDTA solution transferred so this uncertainty must be considered twice (i.e. the uncertainty is 2 x ±0.005g = ±0.01g).

The fine-tip Pasteur pipette can deliver a drop of solution with a volume of 0.02 ml (i.e. 0.02cm<sup>3</sup> ). Assuming the solution is very dilute, this corresponds to a mass of 0.02g of solution. It may be that one drop contains more moles of EDTA than required to complex with the remaining metal ions and so the mass of EDTA added is more than required. This could be up to almost the entire mass of the drop so we can say that the uncertainty introduced by the volume of the drop is up to a maximum of +0.02g.

The uncertainty in our measurement is asymmetric: -0.01g and +0.03g

Using one result for each of the water samples tested, calculate the percentage errors in each of the measurements for the upper and lower bounds.

e.g. first result for sample 1 from results table above EDTA = 0.86g

lower bound % error = (-0.01g/0.86) x 100 = 1.2 %

Assuming the end point was identified with the addition of one drop of EDTA (+0.02g) and difference of two masses introduces uncertainty of ±0.01g

3. In calculating the total water hardness, the atomic mass (Ar) of Ca was used instead of some weighted average of Ca and Mg. What is the impact of this assumption on the accuracy of your results – will your results be greater or less than the ‘true’ value? Explain your answer.

We used the molar relationship in equation 2 (mass = Ar x moles) to convert from molar to mass concentration

This means the mass concentration will be greater than the true value since factoring in the atomic mass for magnesium (which is considerably less than that of calcium - Ar(Mg) = 24.305) should reduce the atomic mass number used to convert between the molar and mass concentration.

4. Tap water may be delivered though old plumbing systems which incorporate lead piping. Some divalent lead ions (Pb<sup>2+</sup> ) may dissolve in the water supply. Pb<sup>2+</sup> ions will combine with the Eriochrome Black T indicator molecule. If lead ions were present in a sample of tap water tested by this method, what effect would these have on your results for total water hardness? Explain your answer.

The calculated total water hardness (mass concentration of Ca<sup>2+</sup> + Mg<sup>2+</sup> ) would be greater than the true value because a larger volume of EDTA would be required to form complexes with the Ca2+, Mg2+ and Pb2+ ions than if no contamination from Pb2+ was present. This means that the moles of Ca2+ + Mg 2+ is greater than the true value and this would propagate through the calculation of mass concentration.
