# **Week 6 – Folds and Faults on Geological Maps**

# **<u>Introduction</u>**

Most of your work to date on geological maps has considered relatively flat-lying deposits or those where the rock layers (strata) are arranged conformably, in sequences from oldest at the base to youngest at the top. Today, our focus is on folded and faulted rocks – which are the result of tectonic forces or stresses acting on the solid crust. The overall aims of the class are to be able to:

- Describe the geometry of folds and faults within geological strata;

- To understand the tectonic processes, or geological properties of the rocks themselves, that cause folds and faults to develop;

- To be able to identify folds and faults on geological maps, and describe their geometry in simple terms.

# **<u>Describing the Orientation of Rock Strata using Dip and Strike</u>**

How do we describe the orientation of geological strata? Note that “strata” are just layers of rock, the word for an individual layer is “stratum”. One way to describe strata orientation is using **<u>strike</u>** and **<u>dip</u>** <u>.</u>

The **<u>strike</u>** of some planar surface, such as a bedding plane, is _<u>the compass bearing of the imaginary line formed by the intersection of the planar surface with a horizontal surface</u>_ . A strike line is always horizontal. The compass bearing is measured from north (000°) and reported in three digits (e.g. 145°).

The **<u>dip</u>** of a planar surface, e.g. the same bed, is always measured at 90° to strike and is _<u>the angle measured down from horizontal</u>_ <u>, so will always a number between 0° (horizontal) and 90° (vertical). To</u> avoid ambiguity, dip direction should be indicated with a compass point, e.g. 145 / 50 SW.

**<u>Dip and dip direction</u>** is another method of showing the orientation of planar surfaces on maps. Dip is the same as in the strike and dip method. Dip direction is the compass bearing of the direction of dip projected to the horizontal. For example: a geological bed dips at an angle of 30 degrees towards the south. This would be written as 30/180 (because the compass bearing for south is 180°).

**Figure 1** . The left panel shows modern British Geological Survey map icons, where strike is the longer line and dip is the short line with a numerical label. Right panel shows older (though still common) notation, where the arrow represents dip direction. The number in both examples represents the dip angle.

# **<u>Folds and Folded Rocks</u>**

Folded rocks are the consequence of compressive forces within the Earth’s crust, and represent a ductile mode of crustal shortening. Remember that ductile deformation is folding and brittle deformation is faulting (which we’ll come to later).

First, let’s learn some essential terminology.  A folded surface which closes downwards is referred to as a **<u>synform,</u>** one which closes upwards is an **<u>antiform</u>** . A fold in which the youngest rocks are in the core (the inside part) is called a **<u>syncline</u>** <u>; a fold with the oldest rocks in the core is an</u> **<u>anticline</u>** <u>. Synforms and antiforms are therefore purely</u> geometric descriptive terms, while anticlines and synclines imply we have some geological knowledge about the relative ages of the beds.

The Y-shaped icon shown in Figure 3 is called a younging symbol. The tail of the ‘Y’ points towards the youngest strata. It is always drawn at 90° to the strike of the beds. Ask a demonstrator if you are unsure about this!

**Figure 3** . Some simple folds.

How can we describe folds more fully? Look at Figure 4:

- a fold has two **<u>limbs</u>** formed from the bedding planes that are dipping in different directions;

- the two limbs meet at a **<u>fold hinge</u>** where the bedding changes from one orientation to another;

- the imaginary line along the fold, parallel to the hinge, is called the fold axis;

- the imaginary plane that can be drawn through the hinge line (and fold axis) that bisects the profile of the fold is called the axial plane;

- the angle between the limbs is, very pleasingly, called

**Figure 4.** Antiform, illustrating key fold terminology.

the interlimb angle.

# **Faults and Faulted Rocks**

Faults are formed, instead of folds, when rocks undergo brittle (instead of ductile) deformation. This can be due to the material properties of the rocks involved, or because the rocks in question are at lower depths and temperatures (higher pressure and temperature favours ductile deformation), or also because of high strain rates (rapid deformation) which favours brittle deformation.

A fault can be defined as a planar <u>surface within the crust along which rocks have been displaced. Different</u> kinds of faults are found in different tectonic settings and are characterised by different kinds of displacement. In the lecture, you have seen something like Figure 9, a classification scheme for faults according to the orientation and relative magnitude of principal stresses (forces) acting on the rocks.

To fully characterise a fault we need to know (1) the orientation of the fault plane surface, (2) the orientation of the slip vector (which describes the movement direction) and (3) the amount of displacement.

**Figure 9.** Illustrations of the main types of fault, defined by orientations of maximum (σ1), minimum (σ3) and intermediate (σ2) principal stress (after Anderson, 1905).

Today we’re mostly concerned with distinguishing three main types of fault, based on their geometry and the sense of motion:

- normal faults have steeply dipping fault planes (~60°) and the hanging wall block (“H” in Figure 10a) has moved down (is “downthrown”) along the fault plane. This has the effect of placing young rocks (white colour in Figure 10a) against, or on top of, older rocks (greys in Figure 10a).

- reverse faults may also have steeply dipping fault planes, similar to normal faults, but they can also be much shallower (~30°), in which case they are called thrust faults. The hanging wall in a reverse or thrust fault moves up vertically and this results in older rocks (grey in Figure 10b) being placed on top of younger rocks (white in Figure 10b).

- both normal and reverse faults are called ‘dip-slip’ faults because they have a strong vertical component to their displacement

- strike-slip faults involve mostly lateral/horizontal displacements, though can involve movement to the left or the right (imagine standing on one side of the fault plane and looking across, to see in which direction the rocks have moved).

The presence of types of fault in a particular place can help to build up tectonic history of an area. Across the UK for example, large ~SW-NE thrust faults reveal how the country has been assembled from a number of ancient crustal fragments. Some of you will return to this topic in more detail later.

# **<u>Exercise 6 – Faults on the Keswick Map</u>**

Now we will explore how faults appear on geological maps. At this stage, we will take advantage of the fact that different symbols are used to represent different types of fault. <mark>Figure 14</mark> shows the different lines used to represent faults on British Geological Survey maps.

What is most important to note is that these symbols give an indication of displacement direction across or along faults, and help us identify which side of the fault is the hanging wall or footwall. If we also use the colour of rock types and their relatives ages, obtained from the strasigraphcic section on the maps, we can understand why faults are marked as normal, thrust, or strike-slip.

What type of fault is the Causey Pike Fault? Along the western parts of its outcrop the Causey Pike Fault is a thrust fault and in the eastern part of the map it has changed to a strike slip fault. The diamond symbols (“teeth”) on the fault contact are on the hanging wall side of the fault. Use the teeth to figure out the hanging wall and the stratigraphic column to determine the age juxtaposition. What type of fault is the Derwent Water Fault? This is a strike slip fault – the arrows show the sense of movement. Encourage the students to try to determine whether it is left or right lateral. What type of fault is the Coniston Fault? This is a normal fault. The small “tick” symbols are on the downthrown hanging wall side of the fault plane. Try to find examples of younger rocks displaced downwards in the hanging wall placing them in contact with older rocks in the footwall. This is also relatively complex as the downthrown side changes from north to south along the length of the fault!
