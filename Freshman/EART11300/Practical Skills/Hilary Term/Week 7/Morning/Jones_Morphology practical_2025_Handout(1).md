Comparative Morphology Practical – March 2025

Key Terms

Morphology – The study of biological form, shape, or structure.

Comparative morphology – Examining variation in morphology across individuals, life stages, or species

Biomechanics – Study of the structure and function of biological systems

Artiodactyls – Even-toed ungulates including Cervidae, Bovidae, and Giraffidae.

Metapodial – Long bones of the hands (metacarpals) and feet (metatarsals) which connect the toes/fingers to the leg/arm bones

Epiphysis – The end part of a long bone which articulates with other bones to form a joint.

Introduction

Comparative morphology can provide zoologists and biologists insights into the ecology, development, and evolutionary history of living and extinct organisms. There is a particular interest in the comparative morphology of bones because these elements are most likely to be preserved in the fossil record, offering an opportunity to study deep-time evolution. In this lab, we will learn how an understanding of comparative morphology and biomechanics can provide evolutionary insights.

Many artiodactyls share the evolved characteristic of fused third and fourth metapodials in both the forelimb and the hindlimb (Fig 1.). Fusion of hand and foot bones is associated with elongation of limbs in running mammals. Here will investigate morphological variation and biomechanics of metacarpals in artiodactyls.

Metacarpal biomechanics - The breadth of the shaft of the metacarpal relative to its length is related to the mode of locomotion and to the extent of elongation of the limbs in the different animal groups. A relatively longer metacarpal indicates a more elongate limb, producing a long out-lever for rapid limb movements during running (Fig. 2). The surface area of the joint of the metacarpal is designed to carry a large proportion of the animal’s body weight, so heavier animals are expected to have larger epiphyses to distribute the load.

Task 2: Joint Scaling

In most quadrupedal animals approximately two-thirds of the weight of the body is carried on the forelimbs.  Therefore, we expect the surface area of the forelimb joints to be proportional to body mass in order to limit the stress on the animal’s joint cartilage.  The area-mass relationship can be tested by estimating the areas of the joint surfaces of the distal epiphyses of the metacarpals and then plotting these areas against body mass values (the latter are taken from the literature).

A: Data collection

(a)  Measure the medio-lateral width (W) and the anterior-posterior diameter (D) of the distal epiphysis and add the data to the google spreadsheet.

(b)  The joint surface of the distal metacarpal epiphysis approximates to half of a cylinder, so you can calculate the surface area of this joint surface using the formula below. Use the google spreadsheet to calculate the joint surface area.
