Acclimation to Sun and Shade in Digitalis purpurea.

Background: Plants need light to grow. Light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll, which absorbs blue and red wavelengths of light (giving leaves a green colour). Absorbed light energy is used to drive an electron transport chain (see How to Grow a Planet, Week 1), with the energy driving the production of sugars that support growth. Plants contain two forms of chlorophyll – chlorophyll a and b, which absorb slightly different wavelengths.

In nature, plants compete for light along with other resources. To optimise their light capture, they change both their morphology (arrangement of leave, height, leaf thickness etc.) and their biochemistry, including changing their chlorophyll content.

In this experiment you will examine the response of a plant species to growth in sun vs shade

Study species: the plant you will examine is Digitalis purpurea, the foxglove. This species is native to woodland margins and clearing and hedgerows (Johnson et al. 1993). As such it is adapted to grow in a range of different light conditions. When it grows in different conditions, it will acclimate to those conditions (Gjindali and Johnson 2023).

Adaptation – a genetic difference between individuals or species that makes them better able to survive in particular conditions

Acclimation- a change in the phenotype of an organism in response to the environment in which it develops

Aims: In this experiment, you will examine the morphology and the chlorophyll content of D. purpurea grown in either sun or shade.

- You will now extract the chlorophyll from the leaf pieces.

- Take one leaf piece and place it in the mortar.

- Using a Pasteur pipette, add 3-4 drops of 96% ethanol.

- Using a pestle, grind the leaf as completely as possible. This should result in a green smear on the bottom of the pestle.

- Add a few more drops of ethanol. Stir with the pestle to produce a uniform green solution, without any leaf pieces in it.

- Using a new Pasteur pipette transfer the green liquid to a measuring cylinder. (IMPORTANT: make sure you do not mix up the pipettes, you must avoid getting chlorophyll into your clean ethanol stock!)

- Wash the mortar with a little more ethanol, and transfer this into the measuring cylinder

- Add more ethanol to make the volume in the measuring cylinder up to 10ml.

- Cover the measuring cylinder with parafilm and mix carefully.

- Take 3 cuvettes. Carefully fill one with clean ethanol. Place your two chlorophyll extracts into the other two. Be careful to remember which is which and be careful not to touch the sides of the cuvette with your fingers.

- Set the wavelength of the spectrophotometer to 665 nm

For any light-absorbing molecule, in a given solvent, we can measure the extinction coefficient for each wavelength. In our experiment, we are measuring absorbance of two forms of chlorophyll, each having overlapping spectra. They both absorb light at 665 nm and at 649 nm, but they have different extinction coefficients at each wavelength. We can therefore use simultaneous to calculate the concentration of each chlorophyll in our mixed solution. Neither molecule absorbs at 750 nm, so why do we measure at this wavelength?
