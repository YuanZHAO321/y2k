### **Condensation of water vapour:**

Condensation of water vapour in a cloud is an equilibrium process.

Condensation is the reverse of evaporation.

Water vapour in the atmosphere is not a huge reservoir like the ocean, so its isotopic composition changes as water condenses.

The first raindrops have higher d<sup>18</sup> O than the vapour: this makes the vapour lighter, i.e. enriched in d<sup>16</sup> O  and dD.

**Meteoric water – water derived from precipitation (rain, snow, etc)** In addition to distance from the ocean, other effects change the isotopic composition of rain:

- latitude  - hot at equator, cold at poles

- • elevation  - warm at sea level, cold at tops of mountains

At high latitude and high elevation, meteoric water is more depleted in<sup>18</sup> O and D.

d<sup>18</sup> O = -50 ‰ and dD = -450 ‰ have been measured at the South Pole.

**Bases** are substances that react with acids and neutralise them. They are usually metal oxides, metal hydroxides, metal carbonates or metal hydrogen carbonates. Many bases are insoluble - they do not dissolve in water. If a base does dissolve in water, we call it an **alkali** .

When **alkalis** dissolve in water they produce hydroxide ions, OH<sup>-</sup> . For example, sodium hydroxide:   NaOH(aq) → Na<sup>+</sup> (aq)<sup>+ OH-</sup> (aq)

### **The pH scale**

Acidity is a measure of the concentration of H<sup>+</sup> ions (protons) in solution. pH means “potential” Hydrogen.

pH = -log10 [H<sup>+</sup> ], where [H<sup>+</sup> ] is the concentration of H<sup>+</sup> ions in mol kg<sup>-1</sup> (= mol L<sup>-1</sup> ).

**pH = -log [H**<sup>**+**</sup> **]  =  log (1/[H**<sup>**+**</sup> **])**

Lower pH indicates an acidic solution, higher pH an alkaline solution. At 25 °C, pure water has a pH of 7.

Example: a solution contains 5×10<sup>−6</sup> mol kg<sup>-1</sup> hydrogen ions.

pH  =  log (1 / (5 x 10<sup>-6</sup> ) )

pH  =  5.30                                            (mildly acidic)

**Acidity:** The acidity of a solution is its capacity to donate protons.

A strong acid is dissociated almost completely when dissolved in water. Examples: hydrochloric acid HCl, nitric acid HNO3, sulphuric acid H2SO4 .

A weak acid dissociates to a small extent, i.e. only a small percentage of its H<sup>+</sup> ions is released into solution. Examples: acetic acid CH3COOH, carbonic acid H2CO3, phosphoric acid H3PO4, silicic acid H SiO 4 4 .

**Alkalinity:** The alkalinity of a solution is its capacity to accept protons.

Strong bases: hydroxides of alkali and alkaline earth elements Examples: caustic soda NaOH, slaked lime Ca(OH)2 Weak bases: NH4OH, Mg(OH)2, Fe(OH)3 (NH4+ = ammonium ion)

In an acid-base reaction, the acid is the _proton donor_ and the base is the . _proton acceptor_

### **Solubility of Salts**

Back to salts:  A **SALT** is an ionic compound that contains a cation other than H<sup>+</sup> and an ion other than OH<sup>-</sup> or O<sup>2-</sup> .

The degree to which a salt dissolves in a solution is called its **SOLUBILITY** .

The **solubility** of a substance in water is the concentration of the substance in the water that is in equililbrium with the substance at the temperature and pressure of interest. Such a solution is called a **saturated solution** because it cannot hold any more of the salt.

### **CHEMICAL WEATHERING**

Chemical weathering is the process by which minerals at Earth’s surface interact with water (particularly acidic water) and the gases in the atmosphere (CO2 and O2) to form mineral assemblages that are thermodynamically more stable under the ambient pressure and temperature (P-T) conditions.

Acids are produced from biogenic activity (plant activity, bacterial metabolism) as well as acidic rainwater.

Chemical weathering can involve :

- **dissolution** reactions e.g. dissolution of halite, carbonates

- • **hydration** reactions e.g. formation of gypsum

Common _products_ of chemical weathering reactions are

- clay minerals e.g. kaolinite, smectite formed in **hydrolysis** reactions

- • oxides, hydroxides e.g. hematite, goethite, gibbsite formed in **oxidation** reactions
