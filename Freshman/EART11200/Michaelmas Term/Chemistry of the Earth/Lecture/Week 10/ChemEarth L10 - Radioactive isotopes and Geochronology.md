### **What do we mean when we talk about the “age” of a rock?**

#### **Igneous rocks**

The “age” of an igneous rock refers to the time when it solidified from a molten state. This is the time when the minerals in the rock formed.

#### **Metamorphic rocks**

The “age” of a metamorphic rock refers to the time when the _minerals that are being dated_ formed. Metamorphic rocks can retain a record of continuous change in pressure, temperature and fluid composition. Different minerals in the rock might have formed at different times.

#### **Sedimentary rocks**

The meaning of an “age” for a sedimentary rock needs to be specified. Sedimentary rocks consist of grains from a range of different source rocks that each has its own age. The rock also has a cement that obviously was the last material to form.

#### **Factors that make a good chronometer**

#### **-** **<u>Half life</u>**

- Geochronometers are useful over a timescale up to about 10 half-lives

- If the half-life is very long we need very high precision to measure small

- changes – this is challenging

- If the half-life is short, the parent decays completely on geological time-scales

#### **<u>Chemistry</u>**

- Parent and daughter have different chemistry.

- Parent and daughter are fractionated among different minerals in the rock,

- i.e. they have different partition coefficients in different minerals.

- Daughter isotope is in low abundance naturally so that the amount of

- radiogenic isotope is measurable.

#### **-** **<u>Ar Ar system</u>**

The chronometer is often measured as<sup>**39**</sup> **Ar –**<sup>**40**</sup> **Ar** . This is because it is easier to measure two isotopes of Ar at the same time than to measure K and Ar separately.  The sample is irradiated in a nuclear reactor which turns<sup>39</sup> K into 39Ar, then 39Ar is measured as a proxy for 39K.
