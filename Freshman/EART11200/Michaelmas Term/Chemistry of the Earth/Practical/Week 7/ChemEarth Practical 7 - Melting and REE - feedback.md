a) For the composition considered above (70% anorthite), what is the temperature when the first melt appears (the eutectic temperature)?

The eutectic temperature is 1274 °C. For any mixture of anorthite + diopside, this is the temperature when the first melt appears.

b) For the composition considered above (70% anorthite), what is the liquid composition of the first melt?

The composition of the eutectic  is An42Di58. For any mixture of anorthite + diopside, this is the composition of the first melt.

c) For the composition considered above (70% anorthite), what is the temperature of the point of complete melting?

Complete melting for this composition occurs at 1450 °C. This is the point where the bulk composition crosses the phase boundary between the field of Liquid and the field of Liquid+Anorthite. The point of complete melting depends on the bulk composition we are considering.

d) For the composition considered above (70% anorthite), how does the composition of the liquid change as temperature increases, and why?

The first liquid composition (eutectic) is An42. At this point all of the diopside and some of the anorthite melts. As we increase the temperature, more of the anorthite melts and the liquid composition gradually approaches the bulk composition (An45 at 1300 °C, An62 at 1400 °C, An70 at 1450 °C).

e) For the composition considered above (70% anorthite), what is the total temperature range where the two-phase assemblage anorthite + melt is present?

The system consists of Anorthite + Liquid starting just above the eutectic (1275 °C) up to just below the temperature of total melting (1450 °C). In other words, the system consists of Anorthite + Liquid over a temperature range of 175 °C.

f) How does the temperature range of the two-phase assemblage vary if we change the proportions of anorthite and diopside in the rock we are considering (e.g. 90% anorthite, 50% anorthite, 10% anorthite)?

For 90% Anorthite, the temperature range is about 250 °C.

For 50% Anorthite, the temperature range is about 50 °C.

For 10% Anorthite, the temperature range is about 100 °C – for this composition, the two-phase assemblage is Diopside + Liquid.

Overall, there is a big difference in the range of temperatures where partial melting occurs, depending on the composition being considered.

a) Are the REE compatible or incompatible elements in olivine and pyroxene? – explain.

The REE are incompatible in olivine and pyroxene – all the partition coefficients are less than 1.

b) Compare olivine and orthopyroxene. Their mineral formulae are very similar. What could be the reason for their different partition coefficients?

Olivine and orthopyroxene have different arrangements of atoms. In olivine, silica tetrahedra are isolated. In pyroxene, silica tetrahedra are arranged in chains. The different silicate structures mean that there are different sized sites for cations, and the pyroxene structure is able to incorporate REE a little more than olivine.

c) Compare orthopyroxene and clinopyroxene.  What is the most significant chemical difference between the two types of pyroxene, and why might this have an effect on the partition coefficients?

The difference is that clinopyroxene contains significant amounts of Ca. Ca<sup>2+</sup> is a large ion. The presence of Ca opens up the structure and allows for more incorporation of large REE ions.

d) This graph shows the same data as above. Now add the partition coefficients for plagioclase and garnet to the graph.

e) Are the REE compatible or incompatible elements in garnet?

A mixture. The light REE (LREE) are incompatible (D < 1) and the heavy REE (HREE) are compatible (D > 1).

f) Are the REE compatible or incompatible elements in plagioclase?

Essentially all the REE are incompatible (D < 1), although Eu is slightly compatible (D just a little >1).

g) Why doesn’t Eu fit the main trend for plagioclase?

Eu prefers to occur as Eu<sup>2+</sup> whereas all the other REE occur as 3+ cations. This means it behaves differently. Eu<sup>2+</sup> can substitute easily into the Ca<sup>2+</sup> site in plagioclase because it has a similar size and charge, so it has a much higher partition coefficient than the other REEs.

h) The difference in behaviour of Eu is called a Eu anomaly. For plagioclase, this is described as a positive Eu anomaly. Which minerals show a negative Eu anomaly?

Orthopyroxene and clinopyroxene show negative Eu anomalies, although these are much less pronounced than the positive Eu anomaly in plagioclase.

i) In some minerals, La is more compatible than Lu. In others, the reverse is true. Which minerals show which patterns?

La is more compatible than Lu in plagioclase; Lu is more compatible than La in olivine, orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene, garnet.

## **C. Use the Rare Earth Elements (REE) to understand melting processes in the Earth’s mantle**

We are going to consider the composition of a liquid produced by melting of the mantle. As an approximation, we can calculate a “Bulk partition coefficient” that describes the composition of a liquid in equilibrium with a mixture of minerals.

Bulk partition coefficient      =       Bulk D     = x1D1 + x2D2 + x3D3 +…….

where x1 = fraction of mineral 1 in the rock.

For example, if we consider Ti in a rock that contains 50% olivine, 30% orthopyroxene and 20% clinopyroxene,

Bulk DTi  = (0.5 *<sup>olv</sup> DTi) + (0.3 *<sup>opx</sup> DTi) + (0.2 *<sup>cpx</sup> DTi)

If we produce a melt in the mantle, it will carry a signature of the minerals that were present in the source region. This can be an important indicator of the depth at which melting occurred. For example, mantle peridotite contains plagioclase at shallow depths (low pressures) but at greater depths (high pressures), plagioclase is not stable and garnet is preferred. If we measure the REE abundances in an erupted melt, we can learn about the depth of melt formation.

What characteristics of the REE pattern would you look for in a basalt, in order to argue that a melt was produced at high vs low pressure in the mantle?

A melt produced at high pressure would have garnet in its source. This means it would have a REE pattern with a steep slope and a small positive Eu anomaly. In contrast, a melt produced by melting at low pressure would have plagioclase in its source. This melt would have a REE pattern with a shallower slope and a significant negative Eu anomaly.

Have you noticed that the melt composition is the inverse (mirror image) of the partition coefficient? Also, notice that REE contents of the melts are high compared with the (unmelted) mantle.
