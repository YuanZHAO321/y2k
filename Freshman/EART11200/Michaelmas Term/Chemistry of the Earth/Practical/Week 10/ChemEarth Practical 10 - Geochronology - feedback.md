## **A. Radioactive decay and half-lives**

We are going to consider the decay of radioactive parent<sup>87</sup> Rb and the growth of daughter isotope 87Sr in a granite sample, over the course of six half-lives. We will assume that the granite initially contains 1.2 x 10<sup>20</sup> atoms of<sup>87</sup> Rb and 0.3 x 10<sup>20</sup> atoms of<sup>87</sup> Sr. In other words,

N0 = 1.2 x 10<sup>20</sup> and D0 = 0.3 x 10<sup>20</sup>

1. The half-life of<sup>87</sup> Rb is 48.8 x 10<sup>9</sup> years. Calculate the decay constant , using the equation:

2. Use the equation,   N/N0  =  e<sup>-t</sup>

3. To use the geochronology equation, we need the slope of the line. Choose two data points (minerals, whole-rock and/or the intercept) to determine the slope (y2-y1)/(x2-x1). Explain why you chose the two points you selected.

You might choose for example, ilmenite and plagioclase, since they are at the ends of the line. In this case, the slope m is equal to:

m =  (0.70490 – 0.69989)  /  (0.11270 – 0.00537)  =  0.04668

This is close to, but not exactly the same as, the slope determined in the linear regression shown above.

4. The slope of the line (m) is equal to (e<sup>t</sup> -1):

m = (e<sup>t</sup> -  1)

Rearrange the equation to determine t, the time since the rock formed.

m  =  (e<sup>t</sup> – 1) (m + 1) = e<sup>t</sup> ln (m + 1) = t

(ln (m + 1)) /  = t

Then calculate t, the age of the rock. Don’t forget to include units.

t   =  (ln (0.04668 + 1)) / (1.4201 x 10<sup>-11</sup> )

t   =  3.21 x 10<sup>9</sup> years   =  3.21 billion years

5. Compare the age you determined to ages that other students calculated using different pairs of minerals etc. to determine the slope. Comment on the differences between the calculated ages.

The age calculated using the slope calculated in the linear regression is 3.23 billion years. Calculating the slope of the isochron “by hand” the way we just did it is bound to be the least accurate method. However, it is not far from the mathematically best answer.

The uncertainty on the age of the rock determined by Nyquist et al. is ±0.11 billion years, based on fitting a linear regression to the data. Where do you think errors and uncertainties might arise?

Uncertainties can arise because of small deviations of one point from the isochron. The more points there are to define the isochron, the better the certainty of the fit.  Errors in the data can arise from analytical uncertainties. Think about how precisely we need to know the values of the isotope ratios – we are looking at the fourth decimal place for<sup>87</sup> Sr /<sup>86</sup> Sr (at least). Measuring these ratios requires a very good mass spectrometer. It is essential that the mineral grains analysed are not contaminated, for example by secondary minerals that have a younger age. It is also possible to contaminate the analysis if there is a tiny amount of Rb or Sr in the acids used to digest (dissolve) the mineral grains. Radiometric dating requires very precise analytical skill!
