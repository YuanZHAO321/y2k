4) Find an image of an **iron metorite,** either online or in the lecture slides. Also, make sure to look at the examples provided. Either insert the image here, or draw a sketch of it. Then describe in words what material the Core consists of:

Iron meteorites are pieces of iron-nickel metal. They are fragments of the cores of asteroids that underwent melting and core formation (this happened over 4.5 billion years ago). Iron meteorites have a criss-cross pattern of crystals that is only produced because they contain around 5% nickel. As the metal cools, it divides into two minerals, one Ni-rich and one Ni-poor, and the crystal structure is determined by this process.

Iron meteorites are our best analogue for the Earth’s core, but the Earth’s core is at much higher temperatures and pressures than are ever reached on a small asteroid. Also, the Earth’s core has a solid inner layer and a liquid outer layer. So iron meteorites are similar, but not an exact match, to the core of the Earth.

- 3) Speculate about the relationship shown on the graph. Try to form a hypothesis that would explain the relationship.

For the conditions where elements were condensing in the early Solar System, pressures were very low and condensation was mainly growth of solids directly from a gas, rather than liquids from gas.

7

The relationship is that with increasing condensation temperature, elements are more abundant. Stated another way, with decreasing condensation temperature, elements are less abundant. In other words, volatile elements are missing from the Earth, compared with the whole Solar System.

Describing the relationship is not a hypothesis. A hypothesis is a statement of a possible reason to explain the relationship. A hypothesis is also testable. In this case, a leading hypothesis is that when the Earth accreted (formed) it was quite close to the Sun. At this location, the disk of gas was warm enough that volatile elements had not condensed. In contrast, carbonaceous chondrites formed further from the Sun where it was cooler and more volatile elements had condensed. This hypothesis is testable – perhaps we could test it by investigating whether Mercury looks as if it formed at even higher temperatures than Earth. How else could we test it?

There are other reasonable hypotheses, for example perhaps the Earth had a composition similar to the Solar System when it formed, but it lost its volatile elements during the impact that formed the Moon.  In a 2017 paper in Nature, Norris and Wood argue that “the pattern of volatile element depletion in the silicate Earth is consistent with partial melting and vaporization rather than with simple accretion of a volatile-rich chondrite-like body”.
