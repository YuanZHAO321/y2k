## **<u>CHEMICAL WEATHERING</u>**

Chemical weathering is the process by which minerals at Earth’s surface interact with water (particularly acidic water) and the gases in the atmosphere (CO2 and O2) to form mineral assemblages that are thermodynamically more stable under the ambient pressure and temperature (P-T) conditions. Acids are produced from biogenic activity (plant activity, bacterial metabolism) as well as acidic rainwater.

Chemical weathering can involve:

- dissolution reactions e.g. dissolution of halite, carbonates

- hydration reactions e.g. formation of gypsum

Common _products_ of chemical weathering reactions are

- clay minerals e.g. kaolinite, smectite formed in hydrolysis reactions

- oxides, hydroxides e.g. hematite, goethite, gibbsite formed in oxidation reactions

## **B. Weathering Reactions**

**1. Dissolution Reactions** .  In a dissolution reaction, a mineral simply dissolves into water. Reactions are usually driven by acidic water. Carbonate rocks are very susceptible to chemical weathering of this type. Carbonic acid (see Part A) is an important acid that drives the reaction.

For calcite dissolution, the reaction is:

CaCO3 (s)  +  H2CO3 (aq)  =  Ca<sup>2+</sup> +   2HCO3<sup>-</sup>

This reaction is balanced. Notice that the CHARGES must balance, as well as the elements.

For dolomite dissolution, the reaction is:

CaMg(CO3)2 (s)      + **2** H2CO3 (aq)     =       Ca<sup>2+</sup> +     Mg<sup>2+</sup> + **4** HCO3<sup>-</sup>

This reaction is NOT balanced – your task is to balance it.

Why do rivers and groundwaters contain significant amounts of Ca<sup>2+</sup> and HCO3<sup>-</sup> ions? How would concentrations of these ions change if there was more CO2 in the atmosphere?

CO2 in the atmosphere dissolves in water, forming carbonic acid, H2CO3 (Reaction 1 above). When carbonic acid dissociates, it forms bicarbonate ions (HCO3<sup>-</sup> ) and H<sup>+</sup> ions (Reaction 2 above). H+ ions mean that the solution is acidic, and this enhances the dissolution of carbonate minerals.  When carbonate minerals dissolve in water, the water becomes rich in Ca<sup>2+</sup> and HCO3<sup>-</sup> ions.

If there was more CO2 in the atmosphere, water would be more acidic and this would increase the dissolution rate of carbonate minerals, thus increasing the amounts of these dissolved ions in water.

**2. Hydrolysis Reactions** .  Most silicate minerals weather in reactions that have one or more solid reaction products, in addition to dissolved species. A typical reaction is weathering of potassium feldspar into the clay mineral, kaolinite. This reaction occurs in an acidic solution. The reaction products include K<sup>+</sup> ions in solution, and silicic acid, H4SiO4. (Silicic acid is a weak acid and behaves in a similar way to carbonic acid – see below.)

The reaction is:

**2** KAlSi3O8 (s)  + **9** H2O (l)    + **2** H<sup>+</sup> =      Al2Si2O5(OH)4 (s)   + **2** K<sup>+</sup> + **4** H4SiO4 (aq) K-feldspar  +      water     +     acid   =        kaolinite    +    potassium ions     +     silicic acid

**3. Hydration Reactions and Solubility.** These reactions convert an anhydrous (water-free) mineral into a hydrated mineral. An example is seen in calcium sulphate minerals, in the reaction of anhydrite (CaSO4) to gypsum (CaSO4.2H2O). Anhydrite is formed in evaporite deposits, along with halite (NaCl).

a) Formation of an evaporite occurs when water evaporates from a lake. The water becomes saturated in salt ions, and salts precipitate.

For NaCl,              Na<sup>+</sup> (aq)  +  Cl<sup>-</sup> (aq)  =   NaCl (s)

Write the reaction for formation of anhydrite:

Ca<sup>2+</sup> +    SO4<sup>2-</sup> =    CaSO4

Now write the reaction that describes hydration of anhydrite to form gypsum:

CaSO4    +   2H2O   =   CaSO4.2H2O

(This transformation is reversible, with gypsum forming anhydrite by heating to around 200 °C under normal atmospheric conditions.)

b) Both halite and anhydrite are soluble in water.

For NaCl, the solubility product Ksp  is given by:   Ksp  =   [Na<sup>+</sup> ] * [Cl<sup>-</sup> ]

Write the equation for the solubility product of anhydrite:

Ksp  =   [Ca<sup>2+</sup> ] * [SO4<sup>2-</sup> ]

The equation for the solubility product of gypsum is the same as anhydrite, because the water in gypsum just becomes part of the solvent.

The solubility product for halite at 25 °C has a value of 36 (Ksp = 36).

This means that for solid halite in equilibrium with water, the concentration of Na (in mol L<sup>-1</sup> ) times the concentration of Cl (in mol L<sup>-1</sup> ) equals 36.

Ksp  =  [Na<sup>+</sup> ] [Cl<sup>-</sup> ]  = 36         so [Na<sup>+</sup> ]   =   [Cl<sup>-</sup> ]  =   6 mol L<sup>-1</sup>

In other words, a solution saturated in NaCl contains 6 mol L<sup>-1</sup> Na+ ions and 6 mol L<sup>-1</sup> Cl ions. This is equivalent to 6 moles of NaCl.
