### **B. Moles and Molecular Mass**

The Avogadro constant is the number of constituent particles, usually atoms or molecules, that are contained in one mole of the substance. (Number of molecules per mole.)

Avogadro constant: 6.022×10<sup>23</sup>

Example: 12 g of<sup>12</sup> C has 6.022×10<sup>23</sup> carbon atoms

1) Calculate the molecular mass of pyrite, FeS2, given that the atomic mass of Fe is 56, and the atomic mass of S is 32.

Molecular mass of FeS2 =  56 + (2*32) = 120

What is the volume of one mole of pyrite? (Density of pyrite = 5 g.cm<sup>-3</sup> )

### **C. Oxidation – Reduction Reactions**

In this section we will look at some important oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions in geochemical systems.

**a) Formation of uranium minerals** (see McSween, p. 132, Worked Problem 7.5)

Much of the world’s mineable uranium occurs as uraninite (UO2) in sandstone-hosted deposits. To explain this, we will look at some important redox reactions.

The primary source of uranium is granitic rocks, that can contain up to 15 ppm uranium. When a granite is weathered by reaction with water, uranium dissolves in the water as U<sup>6+</sup> ions, and then it is readily carried by the water in the form of complex ions. In acidic water, the uranyl ion is stable:

UO2<sup>2+</sup> 1*U<sup>6+</sup> and 2*O<sup>2-</sup> =      6+    +    4-   =    2+

In alkaline water, U<sup>6+</sup> may be transported as the complex ion:

UO2(CO3)3<sup>4-</sup> 1*U<sup>6+</sup> and 2*O<sup>2-</sup> and 3*(CO3)<sup>2-</sup> =   6+    +    4-    +   6-    =   4-

Show how the charge on the two ions above are a balance of U<sup>6+</sup> ions, O<sup>2-</sup> ions, and CO3<sup>2-</sup> ions.

If the water also begins to dissolve iron as Fe<sup>2+</sup> ions, the following reaction can occur:

UO2(CO3)3<sup>4-</sup> +    2Fe<sup>2+</sup> +   3H2O   =    UO2    +    2Fe(OH)3    +    3CO2

- What is the valence of U in UO2?     U<sup>4+</sup> .   One U<sup>4+</sup> and two O<sup>2-</sup> = overall charge of zero.

- Is U<sup>6+</sup> oxidized or reduced in this reaction? (explain)

   - U<sup>6+</sup> is reduced to U<sup>4+</sup> (gain of electrons)

- Is Fe<sup>2+</sup> oxidized or reduced in this reaction? (explain)

Fe<sup>2+</sup> is oxidized to Fe<sup>3+</sup> (loss of an electron)

The reaction takes place within a sandstone. Both UO2 (uraninite) and Fe(OH)3 (iron hydroxide or ferric hydroxide) are insoluble in water, so both of these minerals precipitate in the sandstone.

Another important U ore mineral is coffinite, USiO4. What is the valence of U in this mineral?

c) Banded Iron Formations (BIFs)  (see McSween p. 164)

In the early Earth, there was little free oxygen in the atmosphere. Photosynthetically generated O2, produced by <mark>cyanobacteria</mark> , first appeared around 2.7 billion years ago. For the next 400 million years, oxygen levels rose only slowly. Part of the evidence for this is the presence of <mark>uraninite (see (a) above) and pyrite (see (b) above) in sedimentary rocks</mark> from that _time_ . Both of these minerals indicate reducing conditions. Cyanobacteria were producing O2, but that O2 was consumed

efficiently in the production of banded iron formations.  Extensive BIFs were formed during this period, but they have not been formed since.

Oxygen released by cyanobacteria in surface ocean waters in the late Precambrian oxidized Fe<sup>2+</sup> that was dissolved in the ocean, producing Fe<sup>3+</sup> ions which form insoluble oxides (Fe2O3), carbonates and silicates. These minerals formed deposits on the sea floor. Fe<sup>2+</sup> in the oceans was most likely derived from <mark>seafloor volcanism</mark> . As the rate of volcanism slowed down, the Fe<sup>2+</sup> concentration in the oceans declined, and <mark>O</mark> 2 <mark>levels in the atmosphere</mark> began to rise. BIFs represent the transition to an oxygenrich atmosphere that ended around 2.3 billion years ago.

Banded iron formations, or BIFs, are a major resource as the main ore for mining iron – 90% of the world’s commercial iron supply.
