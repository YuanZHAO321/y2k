#### **B. Organic Chemistry and Oil Refineries.**

**1. Distillation.** The diagram on the front page shows the basic principles of an oil refinery. Crude oil, consisting of very long-chain hydrocarbons, is broken down through the process of distillation to produce a variety of different products consisting of shorter chain molecules.

a) What is the basis for the distillation process? How and why does the chemistry of the distillation product change as you go upwards through the refinery plant?

First, crude oil, which is a mixture of many different hydrocarbon molecules, is heated so that it all vaporizes. The gas passes into the distillation plant and condenses on plates that are held at different temperatures, as it rises. Longer chain molecules have high density and higher boiling points / higher condensation temperatures – these condense at the bottom of the column. Shorter chain molecules have lower density and lower boiling points / lower condensation temperatures – these rise to the top of the column where they are condensed and collected. Products go from solids (waxes, polishes) to liquids (fuels) to gases (image shows a canister of compressed gas) with height in the column.

In fact, refineries are more complex because chemical processes are carried out on the different distillates to make them more useful. <mark>Note that the name propene refers to the presence of a double bond in the molecule that is based on propane. Hept- is a prefix for 7, and dec- is a prefix for ten, as in heptathlon and decathlon.</mark>

<mark>The mixture of C5-C10 hydrocarbons obtained directly from the distillation of crude oil contains a high proportion of straight-chain alkanes. However, if this mixture is used as petrol, it does serious damage to a car's engine. Petrol containing a high proportion of straight chain alkanes tends to ignite in the cylinder of the car engine as the piston increases the pressure and before the cylinder reaches the optimum position. Ideally, the mixture of petrol vapour and air is ignited with a spark at a predetermined position of the piston in the cylinder. This problem of premature ignition is referred to as pre-ignition and also as engine knock. The term knock is used, as pre-ignition can be heard. Severe knock can cause serious engine damage. However, branched-chain alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons are much more resistant to knock. Straight-chain alkanes are converted into these compounds in a series of processes in the refinery.</mark>

<mark>The resistance of petrol to knock is measured in terms of an octane rating (octane number). The higher the number, the less likely is a fuel to pre-ignite. The octane rating is on a scale where heptane is given an arbitrary score of 0 and 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (iso-octane) one of 100. Thus, a petrol with the same knocking characteristics as a mixture of 95% 2,2,4-trimethylpentane and 5% heptane has an octane rating of 95. A rating of 95 does not mean that the petrol contains just isooctane and heptane in these proportions, but that it has the same tendency to knock as this mixture. The octane ratings of petrols usually available for cars range from 95 upwards and contain a mixture of straight-chain, branched, cyclic and aromatic hydrocarbons, produced by cracking and other processes described below.</mark>

#### **6. Polymerization**

When many molecules of a simple compound join together, the product is called a polymer and the process is termed polymerization. The simple compounds whose molecules join together to form the polymers are called monomers. Monomers are generally produced in the refinery and the polymers, such as poly(ethene) and poly(propene), are often manufactured nearby. The major differences between smaller molecules and polymers lie not with their chemical properties but with their physical ones.  Their larger sizes lead to much stronger intermolecular forces, leading in turn to much higher melting points, and the characteristic properties of hardness and flexibility found in plastics.
